Original Oxford Week 36 — complete instructional text

Main activity

Explain that this week, students will be honing their productive skills by exploring a specific speaking or writing genre and producing their own text and/or performance.

These sessions will enable students to practise their integrated language skills in enjoyable and creative ways.

They will produce a piece of work that focuses on an area of particular interest. Try to centre the work on a locally and/or personally relevant topic.

Start by familiarizing students with the chosen genre, and work together to identify and analyse its features. This might be:

video input (e.g. of an inspirational public speaker or a captivating storyteller);

a model text (e.g. a persuasive letter or a short story extract of your choice);

a visiting guest speaker (e.g. a local poet or playwright who performs their work to students in class, then answers students’ questions).

Provide opportunities for critical thinking, discussion, and interaction. For example:

Students could compare and contrast two texts and consider how they use different techniques to achieve their purposes, and why.

Students could talk about how a public speaker or performer delivers their speech successfully or unsuccessfully, and why.

In doing so, students will analyse the effectiveness of particular language choices and think about how to incorporate these into their own work. Where applicable, they will also examine the way in which a speaker delivers a text.

After the initial teacher-led input, tell students that they will be putting their newly acquired skills into practice by producing their own piece of written and/or spoken creative work. Allocate enough time (at least one or two lessons) for them to complete this.

Aim to include a peer feedback stage and, if possible, one-to-one student–teacher discussions to guide students’ thinking and to help them identify areas for improvement.

Remind students that the sessions are to experiment with ideas and techniques, and that there is no formal presentation or assessment.

You may choose one of the workshop ideas below.

Play scripts

If possible, arrange for students to watch a performance of a play in English by a local theatre group. Alternatively, watch a video of a short play or extract.

Explore with students the elements that go into a theatre production. Ensure that they understand that all the elements have a significant part to play in the overall effectiveness of a performance.

the script

How is a play structured?

What is included in a play script, apart from the dialogue?

How is a play script written on the page?

the actors

What is the actors’ role?

What makes a good performance by an actor?

the director

What is the director’s role?

the costumes

Why are costumes important?

How can they contribute to the performance?

the stage, props, and lighting

Why are the scenery, the props, and the lighting important?

How can they contribute to the performance?

music and sound effects

Why are the music and sound effects important?

How can they contribute to the performance?

While or after watching, encourage students to consider one or more of these elements, and how it contributes to an effective performance. For example:

How is the story ‘told’ through the dialogue?

How are other aspects of the story ‘told’?

How are the characters’ identities and stories told through the different elements?

How do the actors use their voices and their body language to express different emotions?

Set students the task of either writing and performing their own play script, or choosing a published play script to perform. This could be the play in ODF SB 3 pp.148–151.

If writing their own play script, students should base this on a familiar or traditional story, modernizing the setting, the characters, and use of language, but keeping the ideas and message of the original story. The play in ODF SB 3 pp.148–151 is an example of this.

Put students into groups to create a mind map or storyboard about:

the characters

What are their key characteristics?

What is their relationship with other characters?

What is their most significant moment in the story?

the key scenes or events

Make a timeline of the progression of the story

Which parts of the story need to be emphasized? How can you do this?

the atmosphere or mood

What is the mood of the story at different stages?

How does it change? How could you reflect these changes through the acting, music, or sound effects?

Give the class time to plan, write, and edit their play scripts. This could be achieved by groups taking on specific scenes, then reviewing each other’s scenes, with a full read-through to check for coherence.

For both students’ own play scripts and for published play scripts, help them to perform effectively:

Use students’ ideas about the key elements of a theatre production to create a set of success criteria for their performances. These should include all the elements of a performance.

Encourage students to organize their groups by allocating roles and responsibilities, and to plan their time effectively.

Allow enough time for rehearsal, encouraging students to support each other to meet the success criteria more effectively.

Monitor and give additional feedback, modelling alternative delivery and helping to identify and resolve any difficulties.

Invite students to perform or do a full read-through of their play for the class, or to a wider audience if appropriate.

Students should self- or peer-assess the process and the final piece of work, reflecting on what they have learned through this workshop.

Vlogging

With students’ help, identify suitable vloggers from a range of different topics, for example:

daily vlogs (routines, day in the life, food or exercise)

travel vlogs (local or international locations, facts and opinions)

informative vlogs (‘how to’ for crafts, cooking, tech skills)

educational vlogs (school subjects, languages, creative arts, sports skills)

music vlogs (your own music, explore popular artists or songs)

reviews (films, books, games, gadgets)

Watch two or three examples of vlogs, asking students to consider the following elements as or after they watch:

What is the message of this vlog? Is there a story, or is it teaching something? Does it focus on one story, or cover different ideas or themes?

How does the vlogger engage their audience?

How do they speak to the camera?

How do they use their voice?

Do they use questions and jokes?

What kind of language does the vlogger use? (formal, informal, chatty, colloquial)

What music is there? How is it used? Is it effective?

Does the vlog use an intro and an outro? How effective are they?

What special features does the vlog have? (e.g. transitions, special effects, or other editing features)

How long is the vlog? How many cuts can you see? (This indicates how many edits the vlogger did to achieve the final product, and so how much footage was originally filmed but not used.)

Use these elements to create a set of success criteria for students’ own vlogs.

Establish whether any students have specific experience or skills in vlogging, filming, or editing videos. These students might enjoy sharing their skills.

Put students into groups to choose a topic to cover in their vlog. They should think about their target audience and what would be of interest to them. For example:

local young people, including other year groups within the school

parents or teachers

students in another school (at home or abroad) with whom your students have a connection

Give the class time to plan their vlogs, focusing on the key features from the vlogs they watched together. Agree on a target length for the finished vlog.

Encourage them to film and edit their vlogs, including effective use of music and intros/outros. If possible, they can post these on the school website or on an alternative and appropriate online platform.

Students should self- or peer-assess the process and the final piece of work, reflecting on what they have learned through this workshop.

Travel writing

Identify some examples of travel blogs that will appeal to and be suitable for your students. Select three or four examples with different styles or themes.

Allocate one blog post to each group of students. Tell them to read it and to make notes about the following elements:

Who is the blog aimed at? How can you tell?

What is the message of the blog post? It could tell a story, be a descriptive or persuasive post, or have a chronological or narrative structure.

How does the writer create the atmosphere of the place they are visiting? Think about:

the content, and how the post focuses on different themes or topics;

their use of language, and in particular descriptive language;

their use of images and photos.

How long is the post? How is it organized?

Share students’ analyses to create a set of success criteria for a travel blog to encourage people to visit their town, region, or a local place that they love.

Ask groups to create a mind map or storyboard for their own travel blog.

Begin by deciding on the location:

What is the focus of your blog post – places of interest, activities, food experiences, something else?

What do you know about the location, and how could you find out more? Could you visit the place and talk to local people? Could you take some photos to include?

Write down all the points that you could include.

Are any of the points connected? Could any be subsidiary ideas to include under one of the main points?

Rank the ideas in order of importance, and discuss and decide on the key three or four main points to include in your post.

Think about language. Creating an atmosphere is a key part of travel writing.

Look at the travel posts online again, or at other examples. Identify useful language and how it is used.

How could you apply this language to your own writing?

Add any other vocabulary that would be useful for the place you are writing about.

Consider pictures.

Would photos, maps, or diagrams help make your post more interesting, or easier to understand?

How can you match the pictures to the text?

Give groups time to write and edit their travel blog.

They could post their travel blog on the school website, or on an alternative and appropriate online platform.

Students should self- or peer-assess the process and the final piece of work, reflecting on what they have learned through this workshop.
