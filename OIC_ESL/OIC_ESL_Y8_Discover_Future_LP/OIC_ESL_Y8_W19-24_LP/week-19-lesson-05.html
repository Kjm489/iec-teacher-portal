Original Oxford Week 19 — complete instructional text

Introductory activity

Tell students to close their eyes and take a few deep breaths. Today’s lesson will involve a lot of writing, so remind them that it’s OK to take some time out for a ‘mindful moment’ if they are feeling overwhelmed.

With their eyes still closed, ask them to think about a memorable movie that they have watched recently, or a favourite movie that they have watched multiple times. Encourage them to think about the plot, and what they liked and disliked.

Ask students to open their eyes. Explain that in today’s lesson they are going to write their own review for their movie of choice.

Main activity

Refer students back to the model reviews in ODF SB 3 p.66 and ODF WB 3 p.60. Recall how a review is structured and how the writer uses language effectively to engage the reader.

As a class, design some success criteria for a movie review in the form of Yes/No prompts. Elicit some example questions on content, language, and organization. For example:

Content: Have you included your opinion, saying what you liked and disliked?

Language: Have you included strong adjectives and adverbs to paint a vivid picture?

Organization: Have you organized your review into four paragraphs? (Introduction, Description of plot, Writer’s likes/dislikes, Conclusion)

Students spend most of the lesson writing their first draft, taking short ‘mindful moment’ breaks to focus their attention when necessary.

Instead of putting their name on their work, give students a fun code name (e.g. popular movie characters). Tell them not to share their code names with others!

Collect in all the first drafts. Put students into groups of three.

Allocate each group a random selection of three reviews. They work together to peer-edit the reviews, referring back to the success criteria agreed on as a class.

They should not make any corrections, but indicate errors accordingly:

Body of review – add a small number (1) next to the error;

Below the review or in the margin – explain the error, e.g. (1) = spelling error.

They can also add notes, such as suggestions for improvements. Remind students that we all make mistakes and that it is good to share and learn from them. Peer-editing helps to consolidate their understanding of language and different types of error.

Collect in the reviews and return them to the original writers under their code names. They review the feedback received and work through their corrections.

At the end of the lesson, discuss as a class what the common errors were.

Additional task

Students can write a final draft of their movie review based on the feedback received.

Learning review

Students have recalled the features of a movie review. They have written their own review for a movie of choice, using a variety of techniques to engage the reader. They have followed success criteria to peer-edit each other’s work.

Differentiation

Less confident students could write a review in pairs, or as a guided small-group activity.

Encourage more confident students to think about how they can use gradable and non-gradable adjectives (see ODF SB 3 p.67 ex.7), exclamations, and imperatives to capture the attention of the reader.
