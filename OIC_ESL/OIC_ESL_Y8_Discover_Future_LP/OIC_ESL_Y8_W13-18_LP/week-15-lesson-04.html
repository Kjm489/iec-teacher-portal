1. Warm-up & Activate Prior Knowledge · 8 min

Oxford introductory activity: Show students the pictures of the overflowing bus and the almost-full bus. Ask: Would you travel in a bus with this many passengers? Why / Why not?

Oxford introductory activity: Discuss what the safe number of passengers is for these buses. (In a lot of cases, buses are not allowed to take more than 50 passengers seated and more than 15 standing.)

Oxford introductory activity: Then ask: So you think buses, planes, trains, cars, and other means of transport have safety numbers? (yes) Elicit from students why safety numbers are important.

ICQ (IEC): What is the first task and what will you produce?

2. Teacher Modeling — I DO · 12 min

Teacher model (IEC adaptation): Demonstrate one representative example from the Oxford main activity.

3. Guided Practice — WE DO · 15 min

Oxford main activity — complete original sequence: Pair up students. Ask them to think about other professional fields where numbers are used to ensure that we stay safe. Bring the class back together and take answers. Accept all sensible, interesting, and correct ones and write them on the board.

Oxford main activity — complete original sequence: Then write the headings ‘Aviation’ and ‘Medicine’ on the board. See if students understand the meaning of the words. Give them a clue through mime.

Oxford main activity — complete original sequence: Ask: How are numbers used in aviation and medicine? To help them, say: In aviation, aeroplane tyres have a certain amount of air in them so they don’t burst. What’s more, if airport staff store too much heavy luggage, it can waste petrol when the plane is in the air. This would be dangerous if there is a problem and there isn’t enough petrol. On the other hand, if a plane has too much petrol, it could catch fire. In medicine, too much or too little medicine can make you very ill. In both fields, experts have to make very careful calculations to keep us safe!

Oxford main activity — complete original sequence: Put students into small groups of four. Allocate each group a professional field (e.g. aviation, medicine, rocket science, civil engineering, dentistry, farming, construction, sport, architecture, finance, meteorology), or ask them to choose one for themselves. Explain the task – they are going to draw upon their existing knowledge of the field and their research skills to compile information on how numbers are used in these professions (in all aspects, but focusing mainly on how they keep our physical health and our livelihoods safe).

Oxford main activity — complete original sequence: They must choose a format to best present their research in writing. It could be a poster, a leaflet, an infographic or diagram, or a slideshow.

Oxford main activity — complete original sequence: Tell students to allocate roles to different group members. The roles are: a project manager, who will support the team, ensure that the task is running to time, and check the work for accuracy before the presentation stage; a head researcher; and two head writers. They will lead in their allocated roles, but can also share ideas and offer help in other areas. When deciding on roles, encourage students to recognize each other’s strengths so they are allocated fairly and strategically.

Oxford main activity — complete original sequence: As students work, ask why safety is important to them and why safety measures should always be taken into account.

CCQ (IEC): What detail or example supports your answer?

4. Supported Pair/Group Practice · 10 min

Supported collaboration (IEC adaptation): Use the pair/group discussion or shared response within the Oxford task; provide a sentence frame and gradually remove it.

PERIOD 2 — 45 MINUTES

PERIOD 2 · 45 MINUTES — YOU DO, Differentiation, Review

5. Independent Practice — YOU DO · 25 min

Independent application (IEC adaptation): Learners independently produce an answer, explanation, or product demonstrating the original OIC outcome.

Formative check (IEC): Show your own response, not a copied group answer.

6. Extension & Differentiation · 10 min

Oxford differentiation: Create mixed-ability groups so peer-support can be provided in the research activity. Less confident students can choose aviation or medicine as their research topic, building on ideas already shared in class. Challenge more confident students to choose civil engineering (i.e. how numbers are important in building bridges) or meteorology.

Oxford additional tasks: Watch the Factflix video and complete exs.3–4 in ODF SB 3 p.49.

7. Review, Formative Assessment & Exit Ticket · 10 min

Oxford learning review: Students have considered how numbers are used to keep us safe in various professional fields. They have presented their ideas and research in a creative format.

Original OIC source sequence (reference only — expand to read)
YEAR 7 Identity
Week 1, Lesson 1: What is identity?
Learning outcomes: 7.1d, 7.2f

Context
This is the first lesson in the unit ‘Identity’.
Students will develop their listening and speaking skills by listening to how other people can be described and discussing the different ways we can identify ourselves. 
This lesson should take around 45 minutes to an hour, although the timing is flexible to suit different timetables.
