LESSON INFORMATION & OBJECTIVES

Topic: What's your name? (1)
Duration: 90 minutes (45 + 45)
OIC Outcomes: 1.1c, 1.1e

Learning Objectives & Success Criteria

By the end of the lesson, students will be able to:

  1. Participate in a simple English greeting and name exchange.
  2. Recognize and respond to six feelings expressions using gestures and spoken English.
  3. Listen to and participate in a greeting song and group movement routine.

Success: Each child responds to a name question and demonstrates understanding of at least four feelings expressions through speech, pointing or gestures.

Target Language, Materials & Preparation

Language: Hello. What's your name? My name is ___. How are you? I'm ___.

Feelings: good, great, hungry, not so good, tired, wonderful.

Materials: Class puppet, greeting song from the original lesson, feelings picture cards, name cards, board and space for movement.

Preparation: Rehearse the original greeting song and its actions. Prepare a clear gesture for each feeling. Confirm any source-specific song instructions before teaching.

PERIOD 1 — 45 MINUTES

1. Warm-up & Objective Discussion — 8 minutes

Teacher: Welcome children with the puppet. Say, "Hello! My name is [name]. What's your name?" Let the puppet ask several children. Use gestures and a friendly voice.

Students: Respond with their names, initially using a one-word answer if needed.

Scaffolding: Model "My name is..." while pointing to yourself; provide a name card and allow choral rehearsal.

ICQ: "When the puppet asks, do you say your name or your age?" Expected: "My name."

CCQ: "If I say 'My name is Anna,' am I telling you my name?" Expected: "Yes."

2. Teacher Modeling — I DO — 12 minutes

Teacher: Demonstrate a complete exchange with the puppet:

Teacher: "Hello! What's your name?"
Puppet: "My name is Sam."
Teacher: "Hello, Sam! How are you?"
Puppet: "I'm great!"

Introduce the six feelings cards one at a time. Say each word, model a facial expression or gesture, and repeat. Demonstrate the original greeting song with its actions while children watch first.

ICQ: "First, do you watch me or start singing?" Expected: "Watch."

CCQs: "If I'm hungry, do I want food?" Yes. "If I'm tired, do I need rest?" Yes. "Is 'great' a positive feeling?" Yes.

3. Guided Practice — WE DO — 15 minutes

Teacher: Play or sing the original greeting song. Lead children through the words and gestures. Pause to elicit key expressions. Display a feelings card, say the word and invite the class to copy the matching action.

Gradually remove prompts: first say and gesture together; then gesture without speaking; finally show only the picture.

Students: Sing, repeat, gesture and identify feelings as a class.

ICQ: "When I show a feelings card, do you say the feeling and show the action?" Yes.

CCQs: "If the puppet says 'I'm not so good,' is it feeling great?" No. "If the puppet is smiling and excited, could it feel wonderful?" Yes.

4. Supported Pair/Group Practice — 10 minutes

Teacher: Demonstrate a greeting exchange with one child. Place children in pairs. Partner A asks the name question; Partner B responds. They switch roles and add a feelings response using a card.

Students: Practise greetings and feelings with a partner, then join a short group movement routine.

Scaffolding: Keep the sentence frames and feelings cards visible; model again for learners who need support.

ICQs: "Do both partners speak?" Yes. "Do you change roles?" Yes.

CCQ: "When your partner asks 'What's your name?', do you answer with your name or a feeling?" Your name.

PERIOD 2 — 45 MINUTES

5. Independent Practice — YOU DO — 25 minutes

Activity A — Puppet introductions (8 min): Invite individual children to greet the puppet and answer "What's your name?" without a full teacher model. The puppet then asks "How are you?" Students choose an appropriate feeling and respond verbally or with a gesture.

Activity B — Feelings action challenge (8 min): Show the six feelings cards in a mixed order. Children identify the feeling and demonstrate its gesture. Gradually remove spoken prompts.

Activity C — Greeting song and movement (9 min): Children perform the original greeting song and movement routine with progressively less teacher support. Invite small groups to lead a familiar part.

ICQs: "Do you wait for the puppet to ask before answering?" Yes. "When you see a feeling, do you show its action?" Yes.

CCQs: "If someone asks 'How are you?', do you say 'I'm tired' or 'My name is tired'?" I'm tired. "Is 'hungry' the same as 'tired'?" No.

Assessment evidence: Record whether each child can respond to a name question, identify feelings and participate appropriately in the song.

6. Differentiation & Scaffolding — 10 minutes

Support:

  • Offer two feelings pictures instead of six.
  • Use gestures, repetition and puppet modeling.
  • Provide "My name is ___" and "I'm ___" sentence strips.
  • Allow pointing before requiring spoken responses.

Extension:

  • Ask and answer both name and feelings questions.
  • Perform a short exchange without visual prompts.
  • Lead a group in part of the greeting song.
  • Ask a follow-up question independently.

ICQ: "If you need help, can you use the picture or sentence strip?" Yes.

CCQ: Show a tired face. "Is this 'tired' or 'hungry'?" Tired.

7. Formative Assessment, Review & Exit Ticket — 10 minutes

Review: Display the feelings cards and ask children to identify them. Revisit the greeting exchange with the puppet.

Exit ticket: Each child demonstrates two responses:

  1. Answers "What's your name?" using their own name.
  2. Identifies or expresses one feeling using speech and/or a gesture.

Final CCQs: "Does 'What's your name?' ask about your name?" Yes. "Does 'How are you?' ask how you feel?" Yes.

Teacher records: Independent / With prompt / Not yet demonstrated.

TEACHER REFLECTION & NEXT STEPS

Post-Lesson Evaluation

Learning: Which students could respond independently to both greeting and feelings questions?

Misconceptions: Did students confuse name questions with feelings questions, or any of the six feelings?

Scaffolding: Which visual prompts, gestures or sentence frames were most effective?

Next lesson: Revisit any insecure language before progressing to the next original OIC lesson.
