Measurable Learning Objectives

  1. Identify or demonstrate key ideas about numbers and number relationships using the original OIC lesson tasks.
  2. Use appropriate lesson language during a guided or paired response.
  3. Complete one independent task and explain or show the answer.

Teacher QA check: Confirm the named target words, textbook task and OIC codes against the original source. Timings refer to the seven IEC stages (8+12+15+10 / 25+10+10 = 90 minutes); original source activity descriptions may need shortening to fit. Six games are optional alternatives, not six additional required tasks.

PERIOD 1 — 45 MINUTES

1. Warm-up & Activate Prior Knowledge · 8 min

Play a game with children. Say a number and ask them to get into groups of that number. Do this with numbers 1 to 20 in a random order. Ask children to count up to 20 with you. Then try counting backwards. Do this first in slow, clear speech so children can listen carefully and understand.

Diagnostic: Ask students to name, point to, or demonstrate one example before teaching.

Task ICQ: Do you listen and look first, then tell your partner one idea? Lesson CCQ: Using the lesson example for “What things do we count?”, which picture, action or response shows the target meaning? Explain or demonstrate.

2. Teacher Modeling — I DO · 12 min

Demonstrate the first task from the original OIC main activity using a visual or concrete example. Think aloud and model the target language: The number is ___. / I know because ___.

Check meaning: If we add one more object, does the number stay the same? Show how you know.

Task ICQ: Do you watch and listen to my example before trying it? Lesson CCQ: Using the lesson example for “What things do we count?”, which picture, action or response shows the target meaning? Explain or demonstrate.

3. Guided Practice — WE DO · 15 min

Explain to the class that they are going to practise using numbers in English over the next two weeks. Encourage children to think about: How do numbers help us in our lives? When do they help us at home or at school? Using the Think-Pair-Share technique, tell children to share their ideas with a partner first and then with the rest of the class. If they need further guidance, prompt their thinking by asking: Why do I count you every day? Turn…

Complete one example together. Elicit an answer, ask for the evidence, and correct a deliberate mistake.

CCQ: Are we counting the objects or guessing? How can we check the answer?

Task ICQ: Are we completing the first example together? Lesson CCQ: Using the lesson example for “What things do we count?”, which picture, action or response shows the target meaning? Explain or demonstrate.

4. Supported Pair/Group Practice · 10 min

In pairs, learners complete a fresh example using the pictures, book task or manipulatives. Partner A responds; Partner B checks and explains; then switch.

ICQs: Are you working alone or with a partner? Who checks the answer? Do you swap roles after one turn?

Support: The number is ___. / I know because ___.

Task ICQ: Does each partner have a turn to speak or show an answer? Lesson CCQ: Using the lesson example for “What things do we count?”, which picture, action or response shows the target meaning? Explain or demonstrate.

PERIOD 2 — 45 MINUTES

5. Independent Practice — YOU DO · 25 min

Learners complete the corresponding original OIC book/activity task individually. Select an unfamiliar example to check transfer, rather than repeating the model.

Success criteria: accurate response, appropriate numbers and number relationships vocabulary, and evidence of independent understanding.

Task ICQ: Will you complete your own answer before asking for help? Lesson CCQ: Using the lesson example for “What things do we count?”, which picture, action or response shows the target meaning? Explain or demonstrate.

6. Extension & Differentiation · 10 min

To support less able children, spend more time on the initial counting practice, focusing only on counting forwards and up to 10. Then put them in a group with more able children, who will continue to support them with the number names in English. Challenge more confident children to count on from 20. How far can they count in English?

Extension: Explain a number pattern or check a classmate’s calculation with objects.

Task ICQ: Can you use the picture or sentence starter if you need support? Lesson CCQ: Using the lesson example for “What things do we count?”, which picture, action or response shows the target meaning? Explain or demonstrate.

7. Review, Formative Assessment & Exit Ticket · 10 min

Children have thought about how they use numbers every day and have practised counting to 20.

Exit ticket: Solve or represent a new number example and explain the answer.

Task ICQ: Do you show or say one answer without copying a partner? Lesson CCQ: Using the lesson example for “What things do we count?”, which picture, action or response shows the target meaning? Explain or demonstrate.

YEAR 1 Working with numbers
Week 22, Lesson 1: What things do we count?
Learning outcomes: 1.1a, 1.2c
Context
This is the first lesson of the unit ‘Working with numbers’.
In this lesson, children will develop their listening and speaking skills by practising counting to 20 and by watching a video about things we count.
The core lesson should take around 45 minutes to an hour, with extension activities provided for extending to 90 minutes over two lessons. The timing is flexible to suit different timetables.
Introductory activity
Play a game with children. Say a number and ask them to get into groups of that number. Do this with numbers 1 to 20 in a random order.
Ask children to count up to 20 with you. Then try counting backwards. Do this first in slow, clear speech so children can listen carefully and understand.
Main activity
Explain to the class that they are going to practise using numbers in English over the next two weeks.
Encourage children to think about: How do numbers help us in our lives? When do they help us at home or at school? Using the Think-Pair-Share technique, tell children to share their ideas with a partner first and then with the rest of the class. If they need further guidance, prompt their thinking by asking: Why do I count you every day?
Turn to Oxford Discover SB 1 pp.86–87. Play the video about numbers in ex.A. As children watch, ask them how many things there are. At the end, play a game and see who can remember how many potatoes there were, etc.
Put children into teams of four or five. Instruct them to think of as many things as possible that we count every day, as well as considering when and why we count these things. Nominate a scribe in each team to note down their classmates’ ideas.
Set a time limit, depending on the needs of your class. At the end of the allocated time limit, ask children to count their answers out loud in English.
Hold a whole-class feedback. Find out which group has come up with the most answers. You could also ask children to think what is the biggest number of things we count (e.g. the number of children in class).
Additional tasks
Answer the introductory questions in Oxford Discover WB 1 p.74.
Ask children to make a list of numbers related to them (e.g. age, house number, number of siblings, etc.).
Learning review
Children have thought about how they use numbers every day and have practised counting to 20.
Differentiation
To support less able children, spend more time on the initial counting practice, focusing only on counting forwards and up to 10. Then put them in a group with more able children, who will continue to support them with the number names in English.
Challenge more confident children to count on from 20. How far can they count in English?
