PERIOD 2 — 45 MINUTES

5. Independent Practice — YOU DO · 25 min

Additional tasks Support children to write instructions for making butter. Draw a bar chart to illustrate children’s findings to the question ‘Do you like butter?’. Find out about other foods that come from animals (eggs, meat).

Gradually remove the picture/word prompt. Assess the learner’s independent response using: This is ___. / I eat ___. / It comes from ___.

Task ICQ: Will you complete your own answer before asking for help? Lesson CCQ: In the lesson example, what does “foods” mean? Point to or explain the matching picture/action.

6. Extension & Differentiation · 10 min

Differentiation Encourage less confident children by providing them with the food words in advance of the listening activity. Ask them to put them in order as they appear on the screen. As an extension task for more able children, ask them to find out about other foods we can make (e.g. cheese, yoghurt).

Fade support from teacher model → picture cue → sentence starter → independent explanation.

Task ICQ: Can you use the picture or sentence starter if you need support? Lesson CCQ: In the lesson example, what does “orange” mean? Point to or explain the matching picture/action.

7. Review, Formative Assessment & Exit Ticket · 10 min

Learning review Children have watched a video about food and where it comes from. They have explored the origin of foods further in a practical task by making some butter.

Exit ticket: Identify one food or drink from a new picture and explain one fact about it.

Teacher records who can respond independently, with prompts, or needs reteaching.

Task ICQ: Do you show or say one answer without copying a partner? Lesson CCQ: In the lesson example, what does “foods” mean? Point to or explain the matching picture/action.

Original OIC context Context
This is the first lesson of the unit ‘Food and drink’. 
In this lesson, children will develop their listening skills by watching a video about food and where it comes from. They will follow simple instructions for making butter.
The core lesson should take around 45 minutes to an hour, with extension activities provided for extending to 90 minutes over two lessons. The timing is flexible to suit different timetables.

Introductory activity Introduce the topic of food to children. You could tell them what you ate the night before and say: These are all … Elicit ‘foods’. Ask children: What do you eat? Where does it come from? Accept all answers.

Main activity Introduce the vocabulary items with flashcards or realia. Play some games. Divide the classroom into four colours – white, red/orange, green, and yellow. Say a food and children move to the appropriate colour corner (e.g. if you say ‘orange’, children go to the orange corner). Then ask children: Which foods come from animals? Children share their ideas and feedback. Elicit that milk comes from cows. We can make butter from milk. Play your chosen video, asking children to note down and remember the foods mentioned. You could also pause the video each time there is a food item and elicit the word. You could also look at p.108 of the Oxford Discover SB 1 and talk about where meat and milk come from. Review known fruit and vegetable vocabulary and ask if these come from animals. Ask children if they know how milk becomes butter. Elicit ideas. Show children one of the jars half-filled with cream. Elicit that by shaking the cream it becomes butter. Before you begin this activity, ensure that nobody in the class has any allergies or intolerances to any of the ingredients in the recipe. Put children into pairs and hand out the jars with cream. Children need to shake the jar vigorously for about 10 minutes. It’s quite hard, so when one child gets tired the other one can take over. Eventually, it will produce a little ball of butter and some liquid (whey). As children finish, invite them to bring their butter to you. If you feel it is safe and appropriate to do so, spread a little butter on some bread for children to taste. Hold a whole-class survey and find out whether children liked their butter.

Additional tasks Support children to write instructions for making butter. Draw a bar chart to illustrate children’s findings to the question ‘Do you like butter?’. Find out about other foods that come from animals (eggs, meat).

Learning review Children have watched a video about food and where it comes from. They have explored the origin of foods further in a practical task by making some butter.

Differentiation Encourage less confident children by providing them with the food words in advance of the listening activity. Ask them to put them in order as they appear on the screen. As an extension task for more able children, ask them to find out about other foods we can make (e.g. cheese, yoghurt).
