PERIOD 1 — 45 MINUTES

1. Warm-up & Activate Prior Knowledge · 8 min

Ask children to reflect on the English language skills they have used over the last couple of weeks, while working through the unit ‘Music’.

Encourage them to think about the core communicative skills: Listening, Speaking, Reading, and Writing. Lead a discussion with children about these skills. For example: Tell me an activity when we practised speaking in English. When we [played XX game], what skills did we use? Did we speak? Did we listen? What did you find easy/difficult? Why?

Help children to assess areas that need further work, what they are comfortable with, and where they feel they were stretched and challenged. Teachers should support lower-attaining children who may find this self-reflective process quite challenging.

ICQ: Do you listen first or play first? Show me when you will start.

Task ICQ: Do you listen and look first, then tell your partner one idea? Lesson CCQ: In the lesson example, what does “Music” mean? Point to or explain the matching picture/action.

2. Teacher Modeling — I DO · 12 min

Explain that this week, children will participate in one or more extended activities in small groups. They will practise their English language skills in a practical and relevant way.

Teachers may choose one of the following activity ideas:

Model the task step by step. Listen and point → teacher rhythm model → echo and clap → partner rehearsal → independent performance.

Task ICQ: Do you watch and listen to my example before trying it? Lesson CCQ: In the lesson example, what does “Michael Rosen performs We” mean? Point to or explain the matching picture/action.

3. Guided Practice — WE DO · 15 min

Children make a recycled musical instrument, such as a water bottle with the lid taped on, or a filled paper/plastic bag. Encourage them to experiment with different materials to see which kinds of sound they can produce. Elicit why it is good to reuse materials in this way.

Children draw a picture of a musical performance they would like to see.

Make a class book, with pictures and simple sentences, showing people doing different musical activities. Children can personalize this by drawing themselves.

CCQ: Does fast music have a quick beat or a slow beat? How can you tell?

Task ICQ: Are we completing the first example together? Lesson CCQ: In the lesson example, what does “Reflection on ‘Music” mean? Point to or explain the matching picture/action.

4. Supported Pair/Group Practice · 10 min

Learn a song together and encourage children to create a dance to accompany it. Children who prefer not to dance can accompany other dancers with their shakers.

Read or listen to the story We’re Going on a Bear Hunt (suggested online search: ‘Michael Rosen performs We’re Going on a Bear Hunt’). Play short extracts of the story and ask children to identify and copy the different sounds. Then help them to understand the main events. Afterwards, ask children to draw a picture of the items or landscape features whose sound they liked most. (If the story isn’t available in your context, source another story that makes use of music or onomatopoeia.)

Pairs practise a response to “Reflection on ‘Music’ — Review Session 3” with a visual or language frame. ICQ: Do you listen first or play first? Show me when you will start.

Task ICQ: Does each partner have a turn to speak or show an answer? Lesson CCQ: In the lesson example, what does “Music” mean? Point to or explain the matching picture/action.

PERIOD 2 — 45 MINUTES

5. Independent Practice — YOU DO · 25 min

Learn a song together and encourage children to create a dance to accompany it. Children who prefer not to dance can accompany other dancers with their shakers.

Read or listen to the story We’re Going on a Bear Hunt (suggested online search: ‘Michael Rosen performs We’re Going on a Bear Hunt’). Play short extracts of the story and ask children to identify and copy the different sounds. Then help them to understand the main events. Afterwards, ask children to draw a picture of the items or landscape features whose sound they liked most. (If the story isn’t available in your context, source another story that makes use of music or onomatopoeia.)

Collect one independent response or product as evidence of learning.

Task ICQ: Will you complete your own answer before asking for help? Lesson CCQ: In the lesson example, what does “Michael Rosen performs We” mean? Point to or explain the matching picture/action.

6. Extension & Differentiation · 10 min

Encourage children who need more support to answer simple questions with tick/cross cards. Provide language and gapped texts, and form mixed-ability groups.

Recognize individual children’s strengths – for instance, those who struggle linguistically might enjoy dancing or experimenting with music and sound. As they focus on these activities, drip-feed useful topic vocabulary, expressions, and questions.

For more able children, encourage them to do more. For example, they could write more sentences in the class book and help their less confident peers with their sentences.

Task ICQ: Can you use the picture or sentence starter if you need support? Lesson CCQ: In the lesson example, what does “Reflection on ‘Music” mean? Point to or explain the matching picture/action.

7. Review, Formative Assessment & Exit Ticket · 10 min

Children have identified the core English language skills that they would like to improve or consolidate, and have done so over the week’s activities. They have participated in an extended activity which has helped to develop and reinforce these skills, while firmly placing them in a real-world context.

Exit ticket: Demonstrate one correct example from “Reflection on ‘Music’ — Review Session 3” without teacher prompting.

Task ICQ: Do you show or say one answer without copying a partner? Lesson CCQ: In the lesson example, what does “Music” mean? Point to or explain the matching picture/action.
