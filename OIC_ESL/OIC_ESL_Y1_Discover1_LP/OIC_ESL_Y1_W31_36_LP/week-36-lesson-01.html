PERIOD 1 — 45 MINUTES

1. Warm-up & Activate Prior Knowledge · 8 min

Ask children to reflect on the English language skills they have used over the last couple of weeks, while working through the unit ‘Living and non-living things’.

Encourage them to think about the core communicative skills: Listening, Speaking, Reading, and Writing. Lead a discussion with children about these skills. For example: Tell me an activity when we practised speaking in English. When we [played XX game], what skills did we use? Did we speak? Did we listen? What did you find easy/difficult? Why?

Help children to assess areas that need further work, what they are comfortable with, and where they feel they were stretched and challenged. Teachers should support lower-attaining children who may find this self-reflective process quite challenging.

ICQ: Do you look at the object before you choose a category? Where will you put your answer?

Task ICQ: Do you listen and look first, then tell your partner one idea? Lesson CCQ: In the lesson example, what does “Living and non-living things” mean? Point to or explain the matching picture/action.

2. Teacher Modeling — I DO · 12 min

Explain that this week, children will participate in an extended activity in small groups. They will practise their English language skills in a practical and relevant way.

Teachers may choose one or more of the following activity ideas:

Model the task step by step. Real objects → two-category teacher model → guided sorting → partner explanations → individual classification.

Task ICQ: Do you watch and listen to my example before trying it? Lesson CCQ: In the lesson example, what does “chatterbox” mean? Point to or explain the matching picture/action.

3. Guided Practice — WE DO · 15 min

Make a ‘chatterbox’ game, for which you can find instructions online. Instruct children to include some living and non-living things which they must name and/or define their characteristics.

Design a sorting game. Children create cards by drawing pictures and/or writing words, and their opponents must sort them into two groups as quickly as possible based on the characteristics of living and non-living things.

Make a class book, with pictures and simple sentences, showing what children have learned about living and non-living things.

CCQ: Is a toy animal living? Why does looking like an animal not make it alive?

Task ICQ: Are we completing the first example together? Lesson CCQ: In the lesson example, what does “Living and non-living things” mean? Point to or explain the matching picture/action.

4. Supported Pair/Group Practice · 10 min

Work together to create a survey to ask children from another class about their favourite living and non-living things. Display the results in a pie chart or bar graph.

Children put on a performance of the story of the gingerbread man. They may follow the story they learned in class, or adapt it to suit their cultural context. Support them to make props and write a simple script. Those children who prefer more creative tasks instead of drama-based activities can write invitations and create a programme.

Pairs practise a response to “Reflection on ‘Living and non-living things’ — Review Session 1” with a visual or language frame. ICQ: Do you look at the object before you choose a category? Where will you put your answer?

Task ICQ: Does each partner have a turn to speak or show an answer? Lesson CCQ: In the lesson example, what does “chatterbox” mean? Point to or explain the matching picture/action.

PERIOD 2 — 45 MINUTES

5. Independent Practice — YOU DO · 25 min

If it is appropriate in your context, encourage children to find and read another fantasy story in which a non-living thing becomes living.

Collect one independent response or product as evidence of learning.

Task ICQ: Will you complete your own answer before asking for help? Lesson CCQ: In the lesson example, what does “Living and non-living things” mean? Point to or explain the matching picture/action.

6. Extension & Differentiation · 10 min

For less able children, provide language and gapped texts, and form mixed-ability groups.

For more able children, encourage them to do more. For example, they could write more sentences in the class book and help their less confident peers with their sentences.

Less confident but able children could stretch themselves by taking a leadership role during rehearsals for the class performance.

Task ICQ: Can you use the picture or sentence starter if you need support? Lesson CCQ: In the lesson example, what does “chatterbox” mean? Point to or explain the matching picture/action.

7. Review, Formative Assessment & Exit Ticket · 10 min

Children have identified the core English language skills that they would like to improve or consolidate, and have done so over the week’s activities. They have participated in an extended activity which has helped to develop and reinforce these skills, while firmly placing them in a real-world context.

Exit ticket: Demonstrate one correct example from “Reflection on ‘Living and non-living things’ — Review Session 1” without teacher prompting.

Task ICQ: Do you show or say one answer without copying a partner? Lesson CCQ: In the lesson example, what does “Living and non-living things” mean? Point to or explain the matching picture/action.

YEAR 1 Seasons
Week 36, Lesson 1: What do you know about seasons?
Learning outcomes: 1.1c, 1.1g
Context
This is the first lesson of the unit ‘Seasons’.
In this lesson, children will develop their listening skills by watching a video about seasons.
The core lesson should take around 45 minutes to an hour, with extension activities provided for extending to 90 minutes over two lessons. The timing is flexible to suit different timetables.
Introductory activity
Ask children to tell you the day and the month. Elicit or pre-teach the months of the year.
After eliciting the month, ask for the season. For example, say: December. Then ask: What season? Elicit: winter. If you are in a (sub)tropical area, adapt to your local context.
Play a game with children. Agree on some actions to represent each month (e.g. shivering for winter). Say a month and children mime the season.
Main activity
If you do not live in an area with four clear seasons, talk to children about different parts of the world with four seasons. They will need to know this for this unit.
Tell children they are going to watch a video about the four seasons.
Put children into groups. Ask them to think of four things they might see in each season. Children draw these items on their whiteboards.
Play the video. Turn to Oxford Discover SB 1 p.67.
Hold a whole-class feedback session. Write and draw vocabulary items on the board for children to copy down.
What can children see in the picture? Elicit ‘autumn leaves’ or ‘people playing outside’. Ask children what colour the leaves are and why. Talk to them about what happens to leaves in autumn. (The days are shorter and the leaves lose their green colour. The leaves turn red/orange/yellow and then they fall off the trees onto the ground.) If you live in a tropical region, see if there is a tree that is deciduous to show the class.
Children can complete the Big Question chart on p.67 of Oxford Discover SB 1.
Children complete the chart for the question: How are seasons different?
Encourage children to present their chart to a partner, then display them around the classroom.
Additional tasks
Complete the introductory questions in Oxford Discover WB 1 p.56.
They can draw a picture of themselves in their favourite season and label it.
Play the video again and stop at certain points, inviting children to comment on the frozen frame. What can you see? What season is it? What is the girl wearing?
Learning review
Children have learned about the four seasons in a short video.
Differentiation
To support less able children, ask them to list just one or two things for each season.
As an extension task for more able children, ask them to list more things for each season, including clothing and activities.
