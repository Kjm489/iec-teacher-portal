Measurable Learning Objectives

Demonstrate the original OIC learning outcomes (1.2b, 1.2e) in “When do we listen to music?” and independently explain or perform a relevant example.

Teacher QA check: Confirm the named target words, textbook task and OIC codes against the original source. Timings refer to the seven IEC stages (8+12+15+10 / 25+10+10 = 90 minutes); original source activity descriptions may need shortening to fit. Six games are optional alternatives, not six additional required tasks.

PERIOD 1 — 45 MINUTES

1. Warm-up & Activate Prior Knowledge · 8 min

Ask children when they normally listen to music. For example, they might listen to the radio in the car (recorded music), or to live music (a music lesson or a concert).

Then ask: Who likes live music? Elicit the features of live music – for instance, people sing or play their instruments at the time when you are watching, they might make a mistake, and it might be very loud.

Ask children how they feel when they hear very loud music. Elicit some feelings words. Reassure them that there is no wrong answer – we all like different things!

ICQ: Do you listen first or play first? Show me when you will start.

Task ICQ: Do you listen and look first, then tell your partner one idea? Lesson CCQ: In the lesson example, what does “–ing” mean? Point to or explain the matching picture/action.

2. Teacher Modeling — I DO · 12 min

Teach the lesson’s vocabulary using flashcards, pictures, or short video clips.

Encourage children to share how they felt if they have experienced any of these live performances.

Model the task step by step. Listen and point → teacher rhythm model → echo and clap → partner rehearsal → independent performance.

Task ICQ: Do you watch and listen to my example before trying it? Lesson CCQ: In the lesson example, what does “When do we listen to music?” mean? Point to or explain the matching picture/action.

3. Guided Practice — WE DO · 15 min

Turn to Oxford Discover SB 1 p.162 and do ex.A together.

Put children into groups of four or five. Give each group a different musical performance word, but ask them to keep it secret. Explain that they have three minutes to create a short role-play or tableau vivant to show the musical performance, but they can’t say the word. However, they can give clues: e.g. We are all playing the drums.

In their clues, encourage children to use the ‘–ing’ form (present continuous) which they practised in Week 31, Lesson 5.

CCQ: Does fast music have a quick beat or a slow beat? How can you tell?

Task ICQ: Are we completing the first example together? Lesson CCQ: In the lesson example, what does “nervous” mean? Point to or explain the matching picture/action.

4. Supported Pair/Group Practice · 10 min

Monitor actively, giving children enough time to come up with their ideas and to rehearse.

Invite each group to present to the class in turn. Can the other groups guess their word?

Pairs practise a response to “When do we listen to music?” with a visual or language frame. ICQ: Do you listen first or play first? Show me when you will start.

Task ICQ: Does each partner have a turn to speak or show an answer? Lesson CCQ: In the lesson example, what does “excited” mean? Point to or explain the matching picture/action.

PERIOD 2 — 45 MINUTES

5. Independent Practice — YOU DO · 25 min

In the same groups, tell children to imagine that they are going to perform live in the kind of performance they just recreated. How do they feel? Encourage children to share their feelings with each other. They might suggest ‘nervous’ or ‘excited’.

Now tell children that you want them to find out the most popular musical performance within their groups. Elicit different ways of getting the information (by asking each other or by doing a questionnaire) and the language they can use: e.g. Do you like …? What is your favourite …? Do you prefer … or …? Write these on the board. Give groups time to ask and answer using these question starters.

Bring the class back together and share results. Establish the class’s favourite musical performance.

Collect one independent response or product as evidence of learning.

Task ICQ: Will you complete your own answer before asking for help? Lesson CCQ: In the lesson example, what does “–ing” mean? Point to or explain the matching picture/action.

6. Extension & Differentiation · 10 min

Allow less able children to ask and answer Yes/No questions.

Challenge more able children to conduct further research into their classmates’ musical likes and dislikes. Support them to make their own questions.

Task ICQ: Can you use the picture or sentence starter if you need support? Lesson CCQ: In the lesson example, what does “When do we listen to music?” mean? Point to or explain the matching picture/action.

7. Review, Formative Assessment & Exit Ticket · 10 min

Children have thought about different types of musical performance and discussed each other’s preferences.

Exit ticket: Demonstrate one correct example from “When do we listen to music?” without teacher prompting.

Task ICQ: Do you show or say one answer without copying a partner? Lesson CCQ: In the lesson example, what does “nervous” mean? Point to or explain the matching picture/action.

YEAR 1 Seasons
Week 32, Lesson 2: What do you know about seasons?
Learning outcomes: 1.1c, 1.1g
Context
This is the first lesson of the unit ‘Seasons’.
In this lesson, children will develop their listening skills by watching a video about seasons.
The core lesson should take around 45 minutes to an hour, with extension activities provided for extending to 90 minutes over two lessons. The timing is flexible to suit different timetables.
Introductory activity
Ask children to tell you the day and the month. Elicit or pre-teach the months of the year.
After eliciting the month, ask for the season. For example, say: December. Then ask: What season? Elicit: winter. If you are in a (sub)tropical area, adapt to your local context.
Play a game with children. Agree on some actions to represent each month (e.g. shivering for winter). Say a month and children mime the season.
Main activity
If you do not live in an area with four clear seasons, talk to children about different parts of the world with four seasons. They will need to know this for this unit.
Tell children they are going to watch a video about the four seasons.
Put children into groups. Ask them to think of four things they might see in each season. Children draw these items on their whiteboards.
Play the video. Turn to Oxford Discover SB 1 p.67.
Hold a whole-class feedback session. Write and draw vocabulary items on the board for children to copy down.
What can children see in the picture? Elicit ‘autumn leaves’ or ‘people playing outside’. Ask children what colour the leaves are and why. Talk to them about what happens to leaves in autumn. (The days are shorter and the leaves lose their green colour. The leaves turn red/orange/yellow and then they fall off the trees onto the ground.) If you live in a tropical region, see if there is a tree that is deciduous to show the class.
Children can complete the Big Question chart on p.67 of Oxford Discover SB 1.
Children complete the chart for the question: How are seasons different?
Encourage children to present their chart to a partner, then display them around the classroom.
Additional tasks
Complete the introductory questions in Oxford Discover WB 1 p.56.
They can draw a picture of themselves in their favourite season and label it.
Play the video again and stop at certain points, inviting children to comment on the frozen frame. What can you see? What season is it? What is the girl wearing?
Learning review
Children have learned about the four seasons in a short video.
Differentiation
To support less able children, ask them to list just one or two things for each season.
As an extension task for more able children, ask them to list more things for each season, including clothing and activities.
