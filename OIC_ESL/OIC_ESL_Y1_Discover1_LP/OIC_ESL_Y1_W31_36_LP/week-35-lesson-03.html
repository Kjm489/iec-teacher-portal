Measurable Learning Objectives

Demonstrate the original OIC learning outcomes (1.1b, 1.2b, 1.3f) in “The gingerbread man (2)” and independently explain or perform a relevant example.

Teacher QA check: Confirm the named target words, textbook task and OIC codes against the original source. Timings refer to the seven IEC stages (8+12+15+10 / 25+10+10 = 90 minutes); original source activity descriptions may need shortening to fit. Six games are optional alternatives, not six additional required tasks.

PERIOD 1 — 45 MINUTES

1. Warm-up & Activate Prior Knowledge · 8 min

Recall the story of the gingerbread man so far.

Using the Think-Pair-Share technique, ask children to tell you what they think happens next.

ICQ: Do you predict before reading or after reading? Which picture gives you a clue?

Task ICQ: Do you listen and look first, then tell your partner one idea? Lesson CCQ: In the lesson example, what does “first” mean? Point to or explain the matching picture/action.

2. Teacher Modeling — I DO · 12 min

Turn to Oxford Discover SB 1 p.179. You can play the audio (track 3.26) or read the second part of the story aloud while children follow in their books.

Using the Think-Pair-Share technique, give children time to discuss how close their predictions were to what actually happened in the story.

Model the task step by step. Picture walk → model a prediction → sequence cards together → pair retelling → independent story response.

Task ICQ: Do you watch and listen to my example before trying it? Lesson CCQ: In the lesson example, what does “last” mean? Point to or explain the matching picture/action.

3. Guided Practice — WE DO · 15 min

As a class, talk about the ending of the story and if they liked it. Can they think of a better ending?

Turn to Oxford Discover SB 1 p.180 and complete the comprehension activities to check children’s understanding of the story. They can do this independently or in pairs.

Ask children: Why did everyone chase the gingerbread man? Did he do the right thing by running away? Did the old man and the old woman do the right thing by chasing him?

CCQ: Which event happened first in the story? What detail tells you?

Task ICQ: Are we completing the first example together? Lesson CCQ: In the lesson example, what does “family” mean? Point to or explain the matching picture/action.

4. Supported Pair/Group Practice · 10 min

Follow up by asking: Why does the gingerbread man listen to the fox (a stranger), but not to his ‘family’ (the old man and the old woman)? Help them to conclude that they should never trust strangers (people they don’t know). See ex.3 on the ‘Additional information’ page. There are many messages, so focus on what is relevant to your class. Explain that sometimes it isn’t easy to find the message straightaway, so we need to think deeply about what the story is trying to teach us (in order to stay safe and happy).

Put children into groups of four or five. Hand out the finger puppets. If they are making their own, give them time to cut out and colour a finger puppet character.

Pairs practise a response to “The gingerbread man (2)” with a visual or language frame. ICQ: Do you predict before reading or after reading? Which picture gives you a clue?

Task ICQ: Does each partner have a turn to speak or show an answer? Lesson CCQ: In the lesson example, what does “Additional information” mean? Point to or explain the matching picture/action.

PERIOD 2 — 45 MINUTES

5. Independent Practice — YOU DO · 25 min

Encourage groups to retell the story with their finger puppets. Each child should choose and play the role of one or two characters.

Collect one independent response or product as evidence of learning.

Task ICQ: Will you complete your own answer before asking for help? Lesson CCQ: In the lesson example, what does “The gingerbread man (2)” mean? Point to or explain the matching picture/action.

6. Extension & Differentiation · 10 min

Form mixed-ability groups during the finger puppet retelling so more confident children can support their less confident peers with vocabulary choices and simple sentences.

Allow more confident children to read the story independently, at their own pace.

More confident children can add more details (or animals) to their retelling of the story.

Task ICQ: Can you use the picture or sentence starter if you need support? Lesson CCQ: In the lesson example, what does “first” mean? Point to or explain the matching picture/action.

7. Review, Formative Assessment & Exit Ticket · 10 min

Children have read the second part of a traditional story about a gingerbread man. They have understood the main events and retold them in a finger puppet show.

Exit ticket: Demonstrate one correct example from “The gingerbread man (2)” without teacher prompting.

Task ICQ: Do you show or say one answer without copying a partner? Lesson CCQ: In the lesson example, what does “last” mean? Point to or explain the matching picture/action.

YEAR 1 Seasons
Week 35, Lesson 3: What do you know about seasons?
Learning outcomes: 1.1c, 1.1g
Context
This is the first lesson of the unit ‘Seasons’.
In this lesson, children will develop their listening skills by watching a video about seasons.
The core lesson should take around 45 minutes to an hour, with extension activities provided for extending to 90 minutes over two lessons. The timing is flexible to suit different timetables.
Introductory activity
Ask children to tell you the day and the month. Elicit or pre-teach the months of the year.
After eliciting the month, ask for the season. For example, say: December. Then ask: What season? Elicit: winter. If you are in a (sub)tropical area, adapt to your local context.
Play a game with children. Agree on some actions to represent each month (e.g. shivering for winter). Say a month and children mime the season.
Main activity
If you do not live in an area with four clear seasons, talk to children about different parts of the world with four seasons. They will need to know this for this unit.
Tell children they are going to watch a video about the four seasons.
Put children into groups. Ask them to think of four things they might see in each season. Children draw these items on their whiteboards.
Play the video. Turn to Oxford Discover SB 1 p.67.
Hold a whole-class feedback session. Write and draw vocabulary items on the board for children to copy down.
What can children see in the picture? Elicit ‘autumn leaves’ or ‘people playing outside’. Ask children what colour the leaves are and why. Talk to them about what happens to leaves in autumn. (The days are shorter and the leaves lose their green colour. The leaves turn red/orange/yellow and then they fall off the trees onto the ground.) If you live in a tropical region, see if there is a tree that is deciduous to show the class.
Children can complete the Big Question chart on p.67 of Oxford Discover SB 1.
Children complete the chart for the question: How are seasons different?
Encourage children to present their chart to a partner, then display them around the classroom.
Additional tasks
Complete the introductory questions in Oxford Discover WB 1 p.56.
They can draw a picture of themselves in their favourite season and label it.
Play the video again and stop at certain points, inviting children to comment on the frozen frame. What can you see? What season is it? What is the girl wearing?
Learning review
Children have learned about the four seasons in a short video.
Differentiation
To support less able children, ask them to list just one or two things for each season.
As an extension task for more able children, ask them to list more things for each season, including clothing and activities.
