Measurable Learning Objectives

Demonstrate the original OIC learning outcomes (1.2a, 1.2f, 1.4c) in “Let’s make a game!” and independently explain or perform a relevant example.

Teacher QA check: Confirm the named target words, textbook task and OIC codes against the original source. Timings refer to the seven IEC stages (8+12+15+10 / 25+10+10 = 90 minutes); original source activity descriptions may need shortening to fit. Six games are optional alternatives, not six additional required tasks.

PERIOD 1 — 45 MINUTES

1. Warm-up & Activate Prior Knowledge · 8 min

Put children into pairs. Give them one minute to think of as many living things as they can and write them on their whiteboard.

Repeat the activity with non-living things.

You could also do this as a whole-class activity in which more confident children are invited to write the words on the board.

ICQ: Do you look at the object before you choose a category? Where will you put your answer?

Task ICQ: Do you listen and look first, then tell your partner one idea? Lesson CCQ: In the lesson example, what does “Let” mean? Point to or explain the matching picture/action.

2. Teacher Modeling — I DO · 12 min

Tell children that they are going to make a board game to revise what they have learned this week about living and non-living things. Remind them that they must practise certain things over and over in order to remember them – and this can be fun, too!

Show children your racetrack template and some magazines. They are going to work in small groups of three or four. You want them to fill the spaces on the racetrack with pictures of living and non-living things that they cut out from the magazines.

Model the task step by step. Real objects → two-category teacher model → guided sorting → partner explanations → individual classification.

Task ICQ: Do you watch and listen to my example before trying it? Lesson CCQ: In the lesson example, what does “Let” mean? Point to or explain the matching picture/action.

3. Guided Practice — WE DO · 15 min

In their groups, encourage children to look through the magazines first and to plan together which items to cut out and use. Explain that this will avoid unnecessary waste.

Advise that they count the number of spaces on their racetrack first and decide how many pictures they need. Suggest that they find an equal number of living and non-living things, and make some easier than others.

Give children time to cut out and stick the pictures to their racetrack. Instruct them to number the pictures neatly.

CCQ: Is a toy animal living? Why does looking like an animal not make it alive?

Task ICQ: Are we completing the first example together? Lesson CCQ: In the lesson example, what does “Let” mean? Point to or explain the matching picture/action.

4. Supported Pair/Group Practice · 10 min

When all the games are ready, tell groups to give theirs to another group to play.

Encourage children to create their own rules. Alternatively, recommend that each child throws the dice or spins the spinner, lands on a picture, and says whether it is living or non-living. If they are correct, they stay in place; if they get the answer wrong, they must go back to the beginning or miss a turn. The first child to reach the end of the racetrack wins!

Pairs practise a response to “Let’s make a game!” with a visual or language frame. ICQ: Do you look at the object before you choose a category? Where will you put your answer?

Task ICQ: Does each partner have a turn to speak or show an answer? Lesson CCQ: In the lesson example, what does “Let” mean? Point to or explain the matching picture/action.

PERIOD 2 — 45 MINUTES

5. Independent Practice — YOU DO · 25 min

Play the games until the end of the lesson.

Collect one independent response or product as evidence of learning.

Task ICQ: Will you complete your own answer before asking for help? Lesson CCQ: In the lesson example, what does “Let” mean? Point to or explain the matching picture/action.

6. Extension & Differentiation · 10 min

To support less able children, allow them to play their own game instead of another group’s.

As an extension task for more able children, encourage them to say more about the picture they land on: e.g. It’s a penguin. It can move and it needs food. They can also increase the challenge level of their game by adding forfeits, bonuses, or a quiz question to a few of the spaces.

Task ICQ: Can you use the picture or sentence starter if you need support? Lesson CCQ: In the lesson example, what does “Let” mean? Point to or explain the matching picture/action.

7. Review, Formative Assessment & Exit Ticket · 10 min

Children have created a board game about living and non-living things to reinforce their learning this week.

Exit ticket: Demonstrate one correct example from “Let’s make a game!” without teacher prompting.

Task ICQ: Do you show or say one answer without copying a partner? Lesson CCQ: In the lesson example, what does “Let” mean? Point to or explain the matching picture/action.

YEAR 1 Seasons
Week 34, Lesson 5: What do you know about seasons?
Learning outcomes: 1.1c, 1.1g
Context
This is the first lesson of the unit ‘Seasons’.
In this lesson, children will develop their listening skills by watching a video about seasons.
The core lesson should take around 45 minutes to an hour, with extension activities provided for extending to 90 minutes over two lessons. The timing is flexible to suit different timetables.
Introductory activity
Ask children to tell you the day and the month. Elicit or pre-teach the months of the year.
After eliciting the month, ask for the season. For example, say: December. Then ask: What season? Elicit: winter. If you are in a (sub)tropical area, adapt to your local context.
Play a game with children. Agree on some actions to represent each month (e.g. shivering for winter). Say a month and children mime the season.
Main activity
If you do not live in an area with four clear seasons, talk to children about different parts of the world with four seasons. They will need to know this for this unit.
Tell children they are going to watch a video about the four seasons.
Put children into groups. Ask them to think of four things they might see in each season. Children draw these items on their whiteboards.
Play the video. Turn to Oxford Discover SB 1 p.67.
Hold a whole-class feedback session. Write and draw vocabulary items on the board for children to copy down.
What can children see in the picture? Elicit ‘autumn leaves’ or ‘people playing outside’. Ask children what colour the leaves are and why. Talk to them about what happens to leaves in autumn. (The days are shorter and the leaves lose their green colour. The leaves turn red/orange/yellow and then they fall off the trees onto the ground.) If you live in a tropical region, see if there is a tree that is deciduous to show the class.
Children can complete the Big Question chart on p.67 of Oxford Discover SB 1.
Children complete the chart for the question: How are seasons different?
Encourage children to present their chart to a partner, then display them around the classroom.
Additional tasks
Complete the introductory questions in Oxford Discover WB 1 p.56.
They can draw a picture of themselves in their favourite season and label it.
Play the video again and stop at certain points, inviting children to comment on the frozen frame. What can you see? What season is it? What is the girl wearing?
Learning review
Children have learned about the four seasons in a short video.
Differentiation
To support less able children, ask them to list just one or two things for each season.
As an extension task for more able children, ask them to list more things for each season, including clothing and activities.
