Measurable Learning Objectives

Demonstrate the original OIC learning outcomes (1.2a, 1.2b, 1.2f) in “Giving advice” and independently explain or perform a relevant example.

Teacher QA check: Confirm the named target words, textbook task and OIC codes against the original source. Timings refer to the seven IEC stages (8+12+15+10 / 25+10+10 = 90 minutes); original source activity descriptions may need shortening to fit. Six games are optional alternatives, not six additional required tasks.

PERIOD 1 — 45 MINUTES

1. Warm-up & Activate Prior Knowledge · 8 min

Tell children that throughout the class today, they will be asked to stop for a moment and to breathe deeply three times. You will raise your hand. When they see it, they should do the same. Then when everyone has their hand raised, gently tell them to place it on their stomach, close their eyes, and breathe deeply.

Do this once at the beginning of the lesson.

Then hand out a small piece of paper to each child. Ask them to secretly draw a living or a non-living thing on their piece of paper.

ICQ: Do you look at the object before you choose a category? Where will you put your answer?

Task ICQ: Do you listen and look first, then tell your partner one idea? Lesson CCQ: In the lesson example, what does “Giving advice” mean? Point to or explain the matching picture/action.

2. Teacher Modeling — I DO · 12 min

Show children a picture of a child riding a horse. Elicit the living and non-living things that they can see.

Focus their attention on the fact that the child is wearing a hard riding hat and a fluorescent riding jacket. Talk about what might happen if they weren’t wearing these things. Say: S/he should wear a hat.

Model the task step by step. Real objects → two-category teacher model → guided sorting → partner explanations → individual classification.

Task ICQ: Do you watch and listen to my example before trying it? Lesson CCQ: In the lesson example, what does “Giving advice” mean? Point to or explain the matching picture/action.

3. Guided Practice — WE DO · 15 min

Repeat with a picture of a person getting wet in the rain. Say: S/he should use an umbrella.

Talk about the use of should in your sentences. Elicit or explain that we use this word when we have an idea about what someone ought to or needs to do: e.g. You should work harder. You should smile for a photo. See also Oxford Discover SB 1 p.181 ex.E.

Then introduce the negative form shouldn’t.

CCQ: Is a toy animal living? Why does looking like an animal not make it alive?

Task ICQ: Are we completing the first example together? Lesson CCQ: In the lesson example, what does “Giving advice” mean? Point to or explain the matching picture/action.

4. Supported Pair/Group Practice · 10 min

On the board, draw a bottle of water, a sun hat, and some sunscreen. Invite children to make some should and shouldn’t sentences for a day at the beach. They can do this in pairs first and then share their ideas with the class. Write correct examples on the board.

Hand out or write on the board the list of situations provided. Put children into pairs to come up with some should and shouldn’t sentences to advise their friend in each situation.

Pairs practise a response to “Giving advice” with a visual or language frame. ICQ: Do you look at the object before you choose a category? Where will you put your answer?

Task ICQ: Does each partner have a turn to speak or show an answer? Lesson CCQ: In the lesson example, what does “Giving advice” mean? Point to or explain the matching picture/action.

PERIOD 2 — 45 MINUTES

5. Independent Practice — YOU DO · 25 min

Now put children into small groups to act out the scenes in which the should and shouldn’t sentences might occur. Allow them to experiment with their role-play freely, as long as they practise speaking in English and using should and shouldn’t.

Invite children who want to perform their role-play ideas to their peers to the front of the class, but let it be optional.

Collect one independent response or product as evidence of learning.

Task ICQ: Will you complete your own answer before asking for help? Lesson CCQ: In the lesson example, what does “Giving advice” mean? Point to or explain the matching picture/action.

6. Extension & Differentiation · 10 min

Spend more time teaching less able children the new grammar in an adult-led small group.

As an extension task for more able children, ask them to think of a should or shouldn’t sentence for their daily life: e.g. You should do your best.

Task ICQ: Can you use the picture or sentence starter if you need support? Lesson CCQ: In the lesson example, what does “Giving advice” mean? Point to or explain the matching picture/action.

7. Review, Formative Assessment & Exit Ticket · 10 min

Children have practised giving advice in different situations using should and shouldn’t.

Exit ticket: Demonstrate one correct example from “Giving advice” without teacher prompting.

Task ICQ: Do you show or say one answer without copying a partner? Lesson CCQ: In the lesson example, what does “Giving advice” mean? Point to or explain the matching picture/action.

Original OIC source activities — reference

Introductory activity / reflection

Tell children that throughout the class today, they will be asked to stop for a moment and to breathe deeply three times. You will raise your hand. When they see it, they should do the same. Then when everyone has their hand raised, gently tell them to place it on their stomach, close their eyes, and breathe deeply.

Do this once at the beginning of the lesson.

Then hand out a small piece of paper to each child. Ask them to secretly draw a living or a non-living thing on their piece of paper.

Now ask children who have drawn a living thing to stand up.

Say some sentences: e.g. You are green. Tell the green living things to go to one side of the classroom. Encourage the rest of the class to guess what some of the things are.

Repeat with non-living things and with a variety of sentences.

Main activity

Show children a picture of a child riding a horse. Elicit the living and non-living things that they can see.

Focus their attention on the fact that the child is wearing a hard riding hat and a fluorescent riding jacket. Talk about what might happen if they weren’t wearing these things. Say: S/he should wear a hat.

Repeat with a picture of a person getting wet in the rain. Say: S/he should use an umbrella.

Talk about the use of should in your sentences. Elicit or explain that we use this word when we have an idea about what someone ought to or needs to do: e.g. You should work harder. You should smile for a photo. See also Oxford Discover SB 1 p.181 ex.E.

Then introduce the negative form shouldn’t.

On the board, draw a bottle of water, a sun hat, and some sunscreen. Invite children to make some should and shouldn’t sentences for a day at the beach. They can do this in pairs first and then share their ideas with the class. Write correct examples on the board.

Hand out or write on the board the list of situations provided. Put children into pairs to come up with some should and shouldn’t sentences to advise their friend in each situation.

Now put children into small groups to act out the scenes in which the should and shouldn’t sentences might occur. Allow them to experiment with their role-play freely, as long as they practise speaking in English and using should and shouldn’t.

Invite children who want to perform their role-play ideas to their peers to the front of the class, but let it be optional.

Additional tasks

Complete Oxford Discover WB 1 pp.158–159.

Encourage children to think of and draw a situation for a partner: e.g. Maria is very hot. Their partner must give some advice: e.g. Maria should drink some water.

Write down some places and ask children to think of some should and shouldn’t sentences. For example, for the classroom, they could say: You should bring your pencil case to school.

Learning review

Children have practised giving advice in different situations using should and shouldn’t.

Differentiation

Spend more time teaching less able children the new grammar in an adult-led small group.

As an extension task for more able children, ask them to think of a should or shouldn’t sentence for their daily life: e.g. You should do your best.
