1. Warm-up & activate prior knowledge · 8 min

  • Ask students to imagine they are at a time capsule opening ceremony. The time capsule is from the year 1895.
  • Ask: How do you feel? What do you expect to see?
  • Then refer students to the text about time capsules in ODF SB 4 p.52. Ask them to read the third paragraph, beginning ‘However, …’ to its end ‘… very upset’.
  • Tell students to imagine that they were at the time capsule event in New Zealand. Encourage them to talk about how they felt after seeing the old button and muddy water. Ask: Were you disappointed? Why? Was the event a waste of time? Why? Was the smell from the capsule awful? Were the contents unimaginative?
  • As a class, speculate about what might have happened to the contents (e.g. the items might have degraded or broken down in the water; the button could have been made out of plastic, so it didn’t break down – this shows how bad plastic is for the environment!).

ICQ (IEC scaffold): What is the first step and how will you respond?

2. Teacher modeling — I DO · 12 min

  • Teacher demonstrates one example from the Oxford main activity using its original instructions.

Teacher move (IEC scaffold): Think aloud through the first relevant example before asking students to try.

3. Guided practice — WE DO · 15 min

  • If they haven’t already done so, allow students time to read the article on p.52 in full.
  • Form pairs of students. Explain that they are going to imagine being at one of the time capsule events mentioned and role-play a dialogue with their partner. One student will be a reporter, while the other will be a member of the public at the capsule opening.
  • Encourage students to describe what happened in their own words, explain how they feel following the reveal, speculate about what might have happened, and suggest what could have been done to make the time capsules more interesting.
  • They can also evaluate the contents by thinking carefully about what the time capsule items could have meant to their owners and how useful they might have been in the past. Remind them to use modals when making guesses.
  • Allow enough time for students to prepare and practise their role-plays.
  • Invite one or two pairs to perform their role-plays in front of the class.
  • Hold a whole-class discussion. Ask: How useful and important are time capsules? What role can they play in our lives? What item is important to you that you would like to add to a time capsule for future generations?

CCQ (IEC scaffold): Which detail in the Oxford text or task supports that answer?

4. Supported pair/group practice · 10 min

  • Rehearse a response to the Oxford main activity with a partner, taking turns and using a sentence frame where helpful.

ICQ (IEC scaffold): Will each partner explain their reasoning?

5. Independent practice — YOU DO · 25 min

  • Individually complete a response or product demonstrating the original learning outcomes: 9.2b, 9.2e, 9.2g.

Evidence (IEC scaffold): Collect a student-generated response rather than a copied group answer.

6. Extension & differentiation · 10 min

  • Pair up similar abilities in the role-play activity so students can work at a pace they are comfortable with and stretch and challenge each other.
  • Add a few role-play prompts to the board to guide less confident students through their dialogue. For example:
  • What do you think about …?
  • How do you feel about …?
  • This [item name] might/must/could have been used as/by a …
  • I think it’s important/interesting/surprising that …
  • I agree/disagree that …
  • Students can complete exs.6–7 in ODF WB 4 p.47.

7. Review, assessment & exit ticket · 10 min

  • Students have thought critically about the value of time capsules. They have practised using speculative language in a role-play activity.

Exit ticket (IEC scaffold): Provide one independent example of what you learned.
