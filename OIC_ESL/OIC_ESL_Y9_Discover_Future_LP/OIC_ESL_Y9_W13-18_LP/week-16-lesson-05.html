1. Warm-up & activate prior knowledge · 8 min

  • Ask: If you could travel back in time to the past, what time period would you choose? Why?
  • Students discuss their ideas in pairs. Encourage them to think about how living in their chosen time period would be different to living now.
  • Invite pairs to share their ideas with the class.
  • Model an example: Even in the last 40 years life has changed a lot. When my mother was young, there was no internet. She had to telephone her friends if she wanted to talk to them, although telephones were expensive so she couldn’t do it very often. She had to wait to see her friends the next day at school or at the weekend. Her television only had a few channels, which weren’t very interesting, so she must have spent more time outside in the fresh air than watching endless movies. What’s more, she had to wait for her favourite song to play on the radio, but she said this was exciting. I wouldn’t have been that patient!
  • Ask: Would you have liked living 40 years ago? What about 100 years ago? Discuss.

ICQ (IEC scaffold): What is the first step and how will you respond?

2. Teacher modeling — I DO · 12 min

  • Teacher demonstrates one example from the Oxford main activity using its original instructions.

Teacher move (IEC scaffold): Think aloud through the first relevant example before asking students to try.

3. Guided practice — WE DO · 15 min

  • Tell students they are going to imagine living 40 years ago, or during their favourite time period, and write a diary entry for one day in that imagined life.
  • As a class, make a mind map of ideas that students could write about, such as ‘School’, ‘Activities’, ‘Music’, ‘Sport’, ‘Family Life’, and ‘Getting Around’.
  • Ask students to choose one or two topics and make their own notes about what they expect a person from their chosen time period would have done, used, and felt. As they work independently, remind them to take mindful moments to reflect on their work so far.
  • Encourage them to use a range of interesting adjectives to describe how they feel, as well as a variety of modals in the present, past, and/or future tenses.
  • Pair up students. They read each other’s plans, highlight what they really enjoyed, and comment on possible factual inaccuracies or improvements to content or language. Remind the class of the importance of constructive feedback – the aim is to help their classmate identify mistakes and make their work even better, not to make them feel bad.
  • Using their revised plans, students write the first draft of their diary entry in the first person. They can be as creative as possible with their ideas.
  • Remind students to stop and take a few deep breaths if they are feeling overwhelmed about the writing task. This will help to develop their attention and executive function.
  • If there is enough time at the end of the lesson, invite a few volunteers to read aloud their diary entries in the role of their chosen character.

CCQ (IEC scaffold): Which detail in the Oxford text or task supports that answer?

4. Supported pair/group practice · 10 min

  • Rehearse a response to the Oxford main activity with a partner, taking turns and using a sentence frame where helpful.

ICQ (IEC scaffold): Will each partner explain their reasoning?

5. Independent practice — YOU DO · 25 min

  • Individually complete a response or product demonstrating the original learning outcomes: 9.4c, 9.4d, 9.4f.

Evidence (IEC scaffold): Collect a student-generated response rather than a copied group answer.

6. Extension & differentiation · 10 min

  • Support less able students by prompting them with questions or by leading them through the creative writing task in a small group.
  • Challenge more confident students to use modals in the future tense as their character speculates about life 100 years from now.
  • Students can write a final draft of their diary entry for display.
  • Encourage students to conduct further research into their chosen time period, focusing on how people lived, the equipment and tools they used, the activities they did, and if there are any artefacts (in museums or historical sites) that can teach us more about their culture.

7. Review, assessment & exit ticket · 10 min

  • Students have thought creatively to write a diary entry about life in the past.

Exit ticket (IEC scaffold): Provide one independent example of what you learned.

Introductory activity

Ask: If you could travel back in time to the past, what time period would you choose? Why?

Students discuss their ideas in pairs. Encourage them to think about how living in their chosen time period would be different to living now.

Invite pairs to share their ideas with the class.

Model an example: Even in the last 40 years life has changed a lot. When my mother was young, there was no internet. She had to telephone her friends if she wanted to talk to them, although telephones were expensive so she couldn’t do it very often. She had to wait to see her friends the next day at school or at the weekend. Her television only had a few channels, which weren’t very interesting, so she must have spent more time outside in the fresh air than watching endless movies. What’s more, she had to wait for her favourite song to play on the radio, but she said this was exciting. I wouldn’t have been that patient!

Ask: Would you have liked living 40 years ago? What about 100 years ago? Discuss.

Main activity

Tell students they are going to imagine living 40 years ago, or during their favourite time period, and write a diary entry for one day in that imagined life.

As a class, make a mind map of ideas that students could write about, such as ‘School’, ‘Activities’, ‘Music’, ‘Sport’, ‘Family Life’, and ‘Getting Around’.

Ask students to choose one or two topics and make their own notes about what they expect a person from their chosen time period would have done, used, and felt. As they work independently, remind them to take mindful moments to reflect on their work so far.

Encourage them to use a range of interesting adjectives to describe how they feel, as well as a variety of modals in the present, past, and/or future tenses.

Pair up students. They read each other’s plans, highlight what they really enjoyed, and comment on possible factual inaccuracies or improvements to content or language. Remind the class of the importance of constructive feedback – the aim is to help their classmate identify mistakes and make their work even better, not to make them feel bad.

Using their revised plans, students write the first draft of their diary entry in the first person. They can be as creative as possible with their ideas.

Remind students to stop and take a few deep breaths if they are feeling overwhelmed about the writing task. This will help to develop their attention and executive function.

If there is enough time at the end of the lesson, invite a few volunteers to read aloud their diary entries in the role of their chosen character.

Additional tasks

Students can write a final draft of their diary entry for display.

Encourage students to conduct further research into their chosen time period, focusing on how people lived, the equipment and tools they used, the activities they did, and if there are any artefacts (in museums or historical sites) that can teach us more about their culture.

Learning review

Differentiation

Challenge more confident students to use modals in the future tense as their character speculates about life 100 years from now.
