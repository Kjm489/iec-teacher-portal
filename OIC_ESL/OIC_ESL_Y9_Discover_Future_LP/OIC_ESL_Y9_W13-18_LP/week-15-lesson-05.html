1. Warm-up & activate prior knowledge · 8 min

  • Show students the picture of the caryatid in the British Museum. Ask: Do you know anything about this sculpture? (A ‘caryatid’ is a column in the shape of a female figure.)
  • Then show them the picture of the caryatids in Athens. Ask: Can you guess the connection between these figures? (The one in the British Museum is the missing one from Athens.)
  • Remind the class of the text ‘Stolen Treasures’ from Week 15, Lesson 2. What can they remember? In the past, people from more powerful countries sometimes stole or ‘looted’ ancient artefacts from weaker countries, especially during periods of war. They put them in museums and earned a lot of money. They often justified their actions by saying they were better able to preserve these precious objects and keep them safe. Some of the most famous examples are the artefacts taken from the Acropolis in Athens in the nineteenth century by Lord Elgin.
  • Ask: Do you think that the caryatid in the British Museum should be returned to Greece? Why / Why not?
  • Encourage students to reflect on how they would feel if something valuable of theirs was stolen and placed on display elsewhere. Ask: Is this fair?

ICQ (IEC scaffold): What is the first step and how will you respond?

2. Teacher modeling — I DO · 12 min

  • Teacher demonstrates one example from the Oxford main activity using its original instructions.

Teacher move (IEC scaffold): Think aloud through the first relevant example before asking students to try.

3. Guided practice — WE DO · 15 min

  • Form small groups of three to four students. Explain that they are going to research stolen artefacts exhibited in museums around the world.
  • They need to work together to find three different artefacts from three different museums, noting down their age, origin country/culture, host country and museum, what they were used for, and why they are culturally important.
  • Once they have compiled the information they need, ask them to create a timeline to illustrate their findings. They can print off photos or add their own drawings to the timeline to make it stand out.
  • Invite students to present their timelines to the class, or display them around the classroom for other students to read.
  • Review the ethical question about whether artefacts should be returned to their country or culture of origin. Today, encourage students to view the issue from the perspective of a ‘global citizen’. Ask: Are the arguments in favour of keeping artefacts in museums still valid today? Does it make a difference what kind of artefacts they are (e.g. human remains or religious artefacts)? Does it make a difference if the artefacts came from remote or unsafe places?

CCQ (IEC scaffold): Which detail in the Oxford text or task supports that answer?

4. Supported pair/group practice · 10 min

  • Rehearse a response to the Oxford main activity with a partner, taking turns and using a sentence frame where helpful.

ICQ (IEC scaffold): Will each partner explain their reasoning?

5. Independent practice — YOU DO · 25 min

  • Individually complete a response or product demonstrating the original learning outcomes: 9.3c, 9.3f, 9.4c.

Evidence (IEC scaffold): Collect a student-generated response rather than a copied group answer.

6. Extension & differentiation · 10 min

  • More able students/groups can research up to six artefacts to add to their timelines.
  • Support less confident students by giving them a pre-prepared list of artefacts for them to choose from and research. Encourage them to think about what search engine terms or websites they could use to find the information they need.
  • Students can complete exs.1–4 in ODF WB 4 p.46.

7. Review, assessment & exit ticket · 10 min

  • Students have researched the origin of artefacts in different museums and illustrated their findings in a timeline.

Exit ticket (IEC scaffold): Provide one independent example of what you learned.
