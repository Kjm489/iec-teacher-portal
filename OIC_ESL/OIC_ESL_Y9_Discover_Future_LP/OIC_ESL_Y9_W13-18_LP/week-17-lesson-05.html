1. Warm-up & activate prior knowledge · 8 min

  • Remind the class that in today’s lesson they are going to write their own article about a commemorative statue or monument that is meaningful to them.
  • To warm up students’ thinking, ask them to stand up and spread themselves out. You are going to say some features of a formal article and some features of a different written genre. If they think the feature should be included in their article, they do a star jump. If they think the feature shouldn’t be included in their article, they squat down.
  • Formal register (T)
  • Your personal opinion (F)
  • Chatty, with lots of exclamation marks (F)
  • A direct quotation (T)
  • Explains the Who/What/When/Where (T)
  • An attention-grabbing headline (T)
  • Emotive language (T)
  • For and against arguments (F)
  • Factual information, including dates (T)
  • A cartoon (F)

ICQ (IEC scaffold): What is the first step and how will you respond?

2. Teacher modeling — I DO · 12 min

  • Teacher demonstrates one example from the Oxford main activity using its original instructions.

Teacher move (IEC scaffold): Think aloud through the first relevant example before asking students to try.

3. Guided practice — WE DO · 15 min

  • On the board, outline the structure of an article in bullet points. Elicit that students must present their facts in a formal way, highlighting the positive impact of the person/event.
  • Dedicate most of the lesson to the written task. Allow students enough time to complete the first draft of their article, referring to their independent research, class-based research, and example article(s) from Week 17, Lesson 4 to help them.
  • Encourage them to use emotive language to capture the reader’s interest and incite an emotional reaction. They can use dictionaries and thesauruses to help them.
  • Trying to include a variety of different article features, a suitable tone of voice, and a range of interesting language all at once isn’t easy, but the task is helping to extend students’ understanding of a particular written genre.
  • Tell students to swap their first draft with a partner and complete a peer-correction exercise. They shouldn’t correct spelling and grammar mistakes, but underline them and indicate the error with a code.
  • Suggested code: Sp = spelling, T = tense, WO = word order is wrong, ᴧ = missing word, prep = preposition, WW = wrong word
  • They give each other constructive feedback, focusing primarily on how well their partner has organized their article and maintained the reader’s interest using key facts, quotations, and emotive language. They should say at least one thing their partner did successfully, and one thing that could be improved to make their article even better.

CCQ (IEC scaffold): Which detail in the Oxford text or task supports that answer?

4. Supported pair/group practice · 10 min

  • Rehearse a response to the Oxford main activity with a partner, taking turns and using a sentence frame where helpful.

ICQ (IEC scaffold): Will each partner explain their reasoning?

5. Independent practice — YOU DO · 25 min

  • Individually complete a response or product demonstrating the original learning outcomes: 9.4a, 9.4e, 9.4f.

Evidence (IEC scaffold): Collect a student-generated response rather than a copied group answer.

6. Extension & differentiation · 10 min

  • Less confident students can write their article in pairs or in a small group.
  • Pair up similar abilities in the feedback task so students can work at their own pace and help to stretch each other.
  • Students can write a final draft of their article based on their partner’s feedback.

7. Review, assessment & exit ticket · 10 min

  • Students have written an article about a commemorative statue or monument of their choice, using a range of linguistic devices to appeal to the reader.

Exit ticket (IEC scaffold): Provide one independent example of what you learned.
