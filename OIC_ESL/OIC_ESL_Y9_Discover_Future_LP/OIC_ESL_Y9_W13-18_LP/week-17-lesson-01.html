1. Warm-up & activate prior knowledge · 8 min

  • Ask students to discuss in pairs: What aspects of the past are you interested in?
  • To inspire ideas, you could model an answer, such as: I really enjoy learning about everyday life. For example, what women did in the 1840s. Did they work? What did they do at home? What did the children do? What did they learn at school?
  • Help students to understand that first-hand accounts of a moment in history are often more valuable and informative than what we read in history books.
  • Elicit from the class examples of first-hand accounts. They might suggest letters, postcards, diary entries, notes people make about their daily lives, eyewitness accounts, and oral history interviews.
  • Then ask: What other sources can we use to learn about the past? (e.g. artefacts, photographs, cartoons and illustrations, old advertisements and posters, archaeological remains, and official records [e.g. birth/marriage certificates or census records])

ICQ (IEC scaffold): What is the first step and how will you respond?

2. Teacher modeling — I DO · 12 min

  • Teacher demonstrates one example from the Oxford main activity using its original instructions.

Teacher move (IEC scaffold): Think aloud through the first relevant example before asking students to try.

3. Guided practice — WE DO · 15 min

  • Tell students that they are going to write a first-hand account of their experience of life in the present day and that it will be displayed in a museum of the future.
  • Allow students time to discuss ideas among themselves and decide on a topic they would like to write about. It should be something that they believe someone would enjoy reading in the future. For example, on the topic of transport, they could write about travelling on an aeroplane for the first time and how it made them feel, how fast commercial aeroplanes can travel today, where they go, and different types of aircraft.
  • The first-hand account will take the form of a letter, addressed to a future museum visitor. Quickly go through how letters are organized (date, an appropriate greeting [e.g. ‘Dear friend of the future’], an introduction, a main body in which the subject is developed, a conclusion, an appropriate sign-off [e.g. ‘Yours faithfully’ or ‘Your friend’]).
  • Encourage students to think about why digital formats might not work – they might be difficult to access in the future (e.g. we no longer have cassette players to play cassettes).
  • Students should spend most of the lesson writing the first draft of their letter. Encourage them to be as creative as possible, including strong adjectives and interesting facts and anecdotes that would appeal to the reader. The aim of the letter is to fascinate and inform a future friend in equal measure.
  • Remind them to regularly read through their writing to check for correct spelling, grammar, and punctuation, as well as appropriate language choices.
  • Creative writing tasks can be challenging as students try to show off their existing knowledge of grammar and vocabulary. They also need to use their imagination to come up with interesting and original ideas. Remind them that they are in the stretch zone.
  • Invite students to share their work with the class.

CCQ (IEC scaffold): Which detail in the Oxford text or task supports that answer?

4. Supported pair/group practice · 10 min

  • Rehearse a response to the Oxford main activity with a partner, taking turns and using a sentence frame where helpful.

ICQ (IEC scaffold): Will each partner explain their reasoning?

5. Independent practice — YOU DO · 25 min

  • Individually complete a response or product demonstrating the original learning outcomes: 9.4b, 9.4d, 9.4f.

Evidence (IEC scaffold): Collect a student-generated response rather than a copied group answer.

6. Extension & differentiation · 10 min

  • Less confident students can write their letter in pairs.
  • Challenge more confident students to include some future predictions in their letter.
  • Students can revise their work based on any feedback received and write up a final draft.
  • They can also record themselves presenting their letter as a kind of oral testimony.

7. Review, assessment & exit ticket · 10 min

  • Students have written a first-hand account of their life in present day for a future museum.

Exit ticket (IEC scaffold): Provide one independent example of what you learned.
