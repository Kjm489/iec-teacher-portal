Source integrity: Oxford activities are reproduced below in source order; IEC scaffolds are labeled separately.

1. Warm-up & activate prior knowledge · 8 min

  • Write on the board, in a random order, the following key words: ‘collection’, ‘country of origin’, ‘cultural importance’, ‘exhibit’, ‘ownership’, ‘preserve’, and ‘stolen treasure’. Ask students, in pairs, to try to define each one in their own words.
  • Then challenge them to predict the content of the reading text in today’s lesson using these words only.
  • Invite students to share their ideas. Check their understanding of the vocabulary before moving on.

ICQ (IEC scaffold): What is the first step and how will you respond?

2. Teacher modeling — I DO · 12 min

  • Teacher demonstrates one example from the Oxford main activity using its original instructions.

Teacher move (IEC scaffold): Think aloud through the first relevant example before asking students to try.

3. Guided practice — WE DO · 15 min

  • Divide students into groups of five. Give each group member one of the paragraphs from the text, removing the numbers 1–5 so they don’t know the order in which they appear.
  • Tell students to read their paragraph individually and to note down six key words to help them summarize their text to the rest of the group.
  • Students summarize their text orally, using the key words to help them. They mustn’t show their paragraphs to each other or read them aloud.
  • Then ask students to work together to arrange the paragraphs in the correct order.
  • While students are working, write the following questions on the board. Ask each group to answer them together to check how well they have understood each other’s summaries.
  • What does it mean to be ‘lost in admiration’?
  • Is Lord Elgin presented in a positive or a negative light? How do you know?
  • How did ‘powerful nations’ justify their possession of cultural objects?
  • Why do you think museums are described as ‘encyclopaedic’? (‘encyclopedic’ = US eng.)
  • Did the British Museum return Easter Island’s statue?
  • Invite students to check their answers by reading each other’s paragraphs.
  • Then write on the board the quotation: ‘We are just a body. You … have our soul.’ Hold a whole-class discussion about the meaning behind this statement. Ask: How does this make you feel? (Its aim is to provoke an emotional reaction, of sadness, solidarity, or guilt.)
  • In the same groups, challenge students to think of one or two solutions to this ethical issue, in consideration of both sides of the argument presented in the text. Hand out flipchart paper for them to note down their ideas.
  • Encourage students to walk around the classroom and check each other’s suggestions. If they agree with a suggestion, ask them to add a tally mark next to it. Review as a class.

CCQ (IEC scaffold): Which detail in the Oxford text or task supports that answer?

4. Supported pair/group practice · 10 min

  • Rehearse a response to the Oxford main activity with a partner, taking turns and using a sentence frame where helpful.

ICQ (IEC scaffold): Will each partner explain their reasoning?

5. Independent practice — YOU DO · 25 min

  • Individually complete a response or product demonstrating the original learning outcomes: 9.3a, 9.3b, 9.3e.

Evidence (IEC scaffold): Collect a student-generated response rather than a copied group answer.

6. Extension & differentiation · 10 min

  • For the summary activity, allow less confident students to work in pairs. Mix together abilities in pairwork.
  • Encourage fast finishers to research the ‘stolen treasures’ mentioned in the text online.
  • Students can complete exs.3–7 in ODF SB 4 p.50.

7. Review, assessment & exit ticket · 10 min

  • Students have orally summarized a paragraph using key words. They have arranged their paragraphs in the correct order and understood the main idea in each. They have proposed solutions to an ethical issue.

Exit ticket (IEC scaffold): Provide one independent example of what you learned.
