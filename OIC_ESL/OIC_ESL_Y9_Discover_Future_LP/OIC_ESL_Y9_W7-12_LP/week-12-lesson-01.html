Source integrity: Oxford activities are reproduced below in source order; IEC scaffolds are labeled separately.

1. Warm-up & activate prior knowledge · 8 min

  • Elicit from students what they understand by the word ‘predator’ and ask them to give some examples. Write their ideas on the board. Suggest ‘wolves’ if students don’t.
  • Ask: Why do you think some animals are predators? Introduce the concept of competition and ecological balance. You can use the input text in ODF WB 4 p.35 to aid your understanding.
  • Invite students to tell you what they know about wolves, in particular.

ICQ (IEC scaffold): What is the first step and how will you respond?

2. Teacher modeling — I DO · 12 min

  • Teacher demonstrates one example from the Oxford main activity using its original instructions.

Teacher move (IEC scaffold): Think aloud through the first relevant example before asking students to try.

3. Guided practice — WE DO · 15 min

  • Put students into groups of three. Write the words ‘ecologist’, ‘ecosystem’, ‘elk’, ‘endangered’, ‘export’, ‘plants’, ‘wolves’, and ‘Yellowstone Park’ on the board.
  • First, check that students understand all the words. Explain that they appear in the text they are going to read. In their groups, ask them to predict what the text is about based on these key words. Then challenge them to think of an appropriate title.
  • Invite volunteers to share their group’s conclusions and title. Discuss.
  • Refer students to the text ‘Why Competition Matters’ in ODF WB 4 p.35. Give groups time to compare and comment on how their titles reflect the actual title.
  • Now divide the class into five groups. Ask each group to choose one of the ‘topic slips’. Students work collaboratively to scan the text for information on their topic word, and note down what they learn.
  • Form new groups, consisting of one student from each of the previous groups. Each student will have notes on a different topic word. In turn, students tell each other about what they have learned about their topic word to get a full overview of the text.
  • Refer students to the text in ODF WB 4 p.35. Allow them time to read the full text and ask any questions.
  • Focus on the last paragraph, beginning with ‘What have we learned from the wolves of Yellowstone?’. Encourage further class discussion on why competition is essential in the animal world and how this benefits ecosystems.

CCQ (IEC scaffold): Which detail in the Oxford text or task supports that answer?

4. Supported pair/group practice · 10 min

  • Rehearse a response to the Oxford main activity with a partner, taking turns and using a sentence frame where helpful.

ICQ (IEC scaffold): Will each partner explain their reasoning?

5. Independent practice — YOU DO · 25 min

  • Individually complete a response or product demonstrating the original learning outcomes: 9.2e, 9.3a, 9.3b.

Evidence (IEC scaffold): Collect a student-generated response rather than a copied group answer.

6. Extension & differentiation · 10 min

  • Group together mixed abilities for the reading activities so more able students can guide less confident students through the text and help them to understand difficult words and concepts.
  • For the wider discussion, you may wish to talk about a common predator in your region to help students to better understand ecological balance.
  • Students choose a predator that they would like to learn more about. Ask them to research their predator and prepare a written or oral presentation on why its presence is essential in the animal world.

7. Review, assessment & exit ticket · 10 min

  • In group work, students have predicted the content of a reading text, learned new topic vocabulary, and shared their learning with their peers. They have thought critically about the predator–prey relationship in the animal world.

Exit ticket (IEC scaffold): Provide one independent example of what you learned.
