Year 9 · Week 11 Lesson 1 · Does competition make us better?

Original Oxford outcome(s): 9.1d, 9.1e, 9.2e

Week / lesson

11 / 1

Duration

90 minutes (2 × 45)

Oxford learning outcomes

9.1d, 9.1e, 9.2e

Source integrity: Oxford activities are reproduced below in source order; IEC scaffolds are labeled separately.

1. Warm-up & activate prior knowledge · 8 min

  • Ask students what they understand by the word ‘competition’. Then elicit examples of different types of competition (e.g. sports, video games, jobs, awards, between friends and family members, etc.). Add any missed examples, such as competing against yourself and your ‘personal best’.

ICQ (IEC scaffold): What is the first step and how will you respond?

2. Teacher modeling — I DO · 12 min

  • Teacher demonstrates one example from the Oxford main activity using its original instructions.

Teacher move (IEC scaffold): Think aloud through the first relevant example before asking students to try.

3. Guided practice — WE DO · 15 min

  • Write the following statement on the board: ‘Competition makes us better.’ Ask those who agree to go to one end of the classroom, and those who disagree to go to the other.
  • Divide both teams into smaller groups. Ask students to nominate a group member to take notes on why they agree/disagree with the statement. Invite each group to present their reasons in turn.
  • Now students are going to watch a Factflix video on the topic (see Unit 4, ODF SB 4 p.37). Before playing the video, choose four to five sentences from the transcript that you think hold key information (e.g. ‘Competition starts …’ and ‘Teenagers who play competitive sports get …’). On the board, write the openers to the sentences and challenge students to predict how the sentences end. They can do this in pairs or small groups.
  • Play the video twice. Tell students to listen out for the ends of the sentences. Pause the video after each play so students have the opportunity to share information.
  • Review answers as a whole class and elicit students’ reactions to the information learned. Recap some of the arguments for and against competition which were raised at the start of the main activity. Write them on the board.
  • Tell students to imagine they are going to interview a famous person about their views on competition. First, allow students to draft some questions for the interview. For example: Do you think you are a competitive person? Did you do well at school? Do you think there is a link between competition and academic performance? Do you think competition helps you to improve? Why / Why not? How do you feel when you win/lose? Is this a good thing?
  • In pairs, students practise their role-play, in which they think critically about the nature of competition.
  • Invite a few pairs to perform their role-plays to the class.

CCQ (IEC scaffold): Which detail in the Oxford text or task supports that answer?

4. Supported pair/group practice · 10 min

  • Rehearse a response to the Oxford main activity with a partner, taking turns and using a sentence frame where helpful.

ICQ (IEC scaffold): Will each partner explain their reasoning?

5. Independent practice — YOU DO · 25 min

  • Individually complete a response or product demonstrating the original learning outcomes: 9.1d, 9.1e, 9.2e.

Evidence (IEC scaffold): Collect a student-generated response rather than a copied group answer.

6. Extension & differentiation · 10 min

  • In the role-play, less able students can use the same arguments from the video, while more confident students can use their own arguments and more adventurous language.
  • Encourage students who don’t normally have the opportunity or confidence to take a leading role in group activities to be the group secretary (note-taker) or to present their arguments to the class.
  • Students can make their own short video, giving their personal view on whether competition makes us better. Encourage them to draw on their personal experiences.

7. Review, assessment & exit ticket · 10 min

  • Students have thought critically about competition and if it helps us to improve and perform better. They have listened to a video on the topic, then communicated what they have learned in a role-play activity.

Exit ticket (IEC scaffold): Provide one independent example of what you learned.
