1. Warm-up & activate prior knowledge · 8 min

  • Elicit from students what they know about Charles Darwin. If they are not forthcoming, provide a little information yourself. Explain that the phrase ‘the survival of the fittest’ is often attributed to him.
  • Ask them what they understand by this phrase and what it could mean in the animal world.

ICQ (IEC scaffold): What is the first step and how will you respond?

2. Teacher modeling — I DO · 12 min

  • Teacher demonstrates one example from the Oxford main activity using its original instructions.

Teacher move (IEC scaffold): Think aloud through the first relevant example before asking students to try.

3. Guided practice — WE DO · 15 min

  • Form groups of four students, consisting of Pair A and Pair B. Give each pair one of the titles of the texts: ‘Win or Die: Biological Competition’ and ‘Four Sneaky Tricks of the Natural World’ (see ODF SB 4 p.39). Ask them to predict what the text will be about based on these titles only. In their groups, students share their conclusions.
  • Distribute ten sentences (five from each text) to each group of four. Challenge students to work out which sentences go with which text, and why.
  • Review answers as a class.
  • Now give each group cut-up and jumbled-up copies of both texts (without the line and paragraph numbers). Challenge them to reconstruct both texts and check if their sentence predictions were correct.
  • Give Pair A the text ‘Win or Die’ and give Pair B the text ‘Four Sneaky Tricks’. Ask pairs to read their assigned text and prepare a minimum of five comprehension questions.
  • Tell pairs to swap texts. Give them a couple of minutes to skim-read.
  • Pair A and Pair B take turns asking and answering each other’s questions, based on their understanding of the text.
  • Bring the class back together. Have a quick discussion about what students have learned.
  • Check that students know what prefixes are. Ask pairs to look again at their text and find examples of prefixes (e.g. ‘intra–’, ‘inter–’, ‘dis–’, ‘co–’, ‘non–’, ‘un–’).
  • Praise students for their excellent teamwork in today’s lesson. Through good communication skills, they were able to complete a variety of reading tasks.

CCQ (IEC scaffold): Which detail in the Oxford text or task supports that answer?

4. Supported pair/group practice · 10 min

  • Rehearse a response to the Oxford main activity with a partner, taking turns and using a sentence frame where helpful.

ICQ (IEC scaffold): Will each partner explain their reasoning?

5. Independent practice — YOU DO · 25 min

  • Individually complete a response or product demonstrating the original learning outcomes: 9.2c, 9.3b, 9.3c.

Evidence (IEC scaffold): Collect a student-generated response rather than a copied group answer.

6. Extension & differentiation · 10 min

  • The reading texts include a lot of challenging vocabulary. Remind less confident students that they don’t need to understand every single word to understand the gist of the text, and they can use context and co-text to help them. Alternatively, pre-teach the vocabulary and establish the meaning of different prefixes before starting the reading activities.
  • Challenge more able students to close their books and discuss what they have learned about the natural world in their own words.
  • In their groups of four, ask students to reread both texts, identify the prefixes, and provide either a synonym or a definition for these words.

7. Review, assessment & exit ticket · 10 min

  • Students have practised their reading and speaking skills in group work, while considering the competitive and survival strategies of animals. They have completed a range of reading activities and identified prefixes in both texts.

Exit ticket (IEC scaffold): Provide one independent example of what you learned.
