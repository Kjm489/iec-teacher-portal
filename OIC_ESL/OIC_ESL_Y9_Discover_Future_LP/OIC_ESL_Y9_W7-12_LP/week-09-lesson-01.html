Source integrity: Oxford activities are reproduced below in source order; IEC scaffolds are labeled separately.

1. Warm-up & activate prior knowledge · 8 min

  • Introduce the topic of graphic novels. Find out what students understand by the term.
  • Ask if there are any graphic novel enthusiasts in the class who can offer further detail, explaining why they like them, what their favourite graphic novel is, and why.

ICQ (IEC scaffold): What is the first step and how will you respond?

2. Teacher modeling — I DO · 12 min

  • Teacher demonstrates one example from the Oxford main activity using its original instructions.

Teacher move (IEC scaffold): Think aloud through the first relevant example before asking students to try.

3. Guided practice — WE DO · 15 min

  • Show students the pictures from ODF SB 4 p.29 in blocks of three. After each block, encourage students to comment on what they think the pictures are about, first in pairs then as a class. Make clear that all ideas are welcome and that there are no ‘correct’ answers – the more imaginative they can be, the better!
  • Focus on the last picture and ask students what they think the shadow on the wall is. Does this help to explain what is happening in the previous pictures?
  • Invite title suggestions for the graphic novel based on the pictures. Then give the actual title: The Arrival. Ask: Do you think this is a good title? Why / Why not?
  • Form three groups of students (A, B, C).
  • Give Group A paragraph 1 of the text on p.29. They read the text and note down the features of a graphic novel.
  • Give Group B paragraph 2 of the text on p.29. They read the text and note down how mood is conveyed in The Arrival.
  • Give Group C paragraph 3 of the text on p.29. They read the text and make a timeline of the history of graphic storytelling.
  • First, students work individually on their assigned task. After, allow them time to share and discuss answers with the rest of their group.
  • Now form new groups with one student from each group (A, B, C). In turn, they communicate what they have learned to their group members so all students have information on all three paragraphs. Highlight the value of learning from each other to complete the reading task.
  • Lead an all-class round-up of the answers and invite comments from students.
  • Give out paragraph 4 of the text for students to read, then discuss as a class.
  • Ask students if they agree or disagree with the statement: ‘Graphic novels are not real literature.’ Lead a whole-class debate on this motion. It is likely that students will offer different perspectives on the motion, so remind them to be open to each other’s points of view even if they don’t agree. They might learn something new that they hadn’t considered previously.

CCQ (IEC scaffold): Which detail in the Oxford text or task supports that answer?

4. Supported pair/group practice · 10 min

  • Rehearse a response to the Oxford main activity with a partner, taking turns and using a sentence frame where helpful.

ICQ (IEC scaffold): Will each partner explain their reasoning?

5. Independent practice — YOU DO · 25 min

  • Individually complete a response or product demonstrating the original learning outcomes: 9.2e, 9.3c.

Evidence (IEC scaffold): Collect a student-generated response rather than a copied group answer.

6. Extension & differentiation · 10 min

  • Group work allows more able students to help less able students in the completion of the reading and information-sharing activities.
  • Pre-teach difficult vocabulary before the reading activity to prepare them for the task.
  • Allow less confident students to debate the motion in pairs or small groups, instead of in front of the class. This should help to build their confidence.
  • Students can write up their response to ‘Graphic novels are not real literature.’
  • Students can design their own graphic story with a sequence of three to five pictures.

7. Review, assessment & exit ticket · 10 min

  • Students have practised reading a text for main information and taking notes, before sharing their learning with other classmates. They have predicted the story behind a graphic novel and debated the relevance of graphic novels in popular culture.

Exit ticket (IEC scaffold): Provide one independent example of what you learned.
