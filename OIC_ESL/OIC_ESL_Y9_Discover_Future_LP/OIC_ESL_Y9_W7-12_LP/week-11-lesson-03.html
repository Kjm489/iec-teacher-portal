1. Warm-up & activate prior knowledge · 8 min

  • Share pictures showing the rivalry between Thomas Edison and Nikola Tesla (online search: ‘Edison vs Tesla’). Elicit from students what they know about these scientists and their ‘rivalry’ – a competitive relationship within the same area of interest.
  • It’s possible that students won’t be familiar with these figures, so give them a few more hints. (Edison invented direct current (DC) to power light bulbs; Tesla invented alternating current (AC) which allows electricity to be transmitted further and at a higher voltage.)
  • Encourage students to speculate on what their rivalry was about. Ask: Can we compare and evaluate the work of scientists?
  • Now show students a video clip created by the U.S. Department of Energy, titled ‘Edison vs Tesla’. Tell students to watch the two-minute clip and find out who is considered the better inventor and why. (Answer: Neither, they are both very different and were great inventors!)

ICQ (IEC scaffold): What is the first step and how will you respond?

2. Teacher modeling — I DO · 12 min

  • Teacher demonstrates one example from the Oxford main activity using its original instructions.

Teacher move (IEC scaffold): Think aloud through the first relevant example before asking students to try.

3. Guided practice — WE DO · 15 min

  • Ask: What is the difference between direct speech and reported speech? Elicit from students three examples of direct speech and write them on the board.
  • In pairs, students work together to report the sentences.
  • Review the tense changes that occur in reported speech:
  • simple present simple past; present continuous past continuous;
  • present perfect past perfect; will would; can could
  • Elicit different reporting verbs: e.g. ‘say’, ‘tell’, ‘ask’, ‘advise’, ‘persuade’, ‘argue’, ‘claim’. (See the purple box in ODF SB 4 p.119 to learn which patterns different verbs follow.)
  • Give each pair a set of ten cut-up sentences about Edison and Tesla. Instruct students to:
  • 1. put the dialogue into the correct order.
  • 2. report each sentence with the reporting verb provided.
  • Allow students time to compare their answers with another pair.
  • Refer students to the text ‘The War of the Currents’ in ODF SB 4 p.40. Ask them to:
  • 1. skim the text for gist.
  • 2. compare their reported sentences with the ones in the text. (The highlighted reporting verbs will help them to locate this information.)
  • If their reported sentences were incorrect or contained errors, encourage students to think about where they have gone wrong and why. Allow them to discuss their mistakes with a partner and talk through what they will do differently next time.
  • Ask students to complete ex.2 in ODF SB 4 p.40. Review as a class.
  • Ask the class: Who do you believe was the better inventor, Edison or Tesla? Why? Do you think the field of science has benefitted from their rivalry? Why?

CCQ (IEC scaffold): Which detail in the Oxford text or task supports that answer?

4. Supported pair/group practice · 10 min

  • Rehearse a response to the Oxford main activity with a partner, taking turns and using a sentence frame where helpful.

ICQ (IEC scaffold): Will each partner explain their reasoning?

5. Independent practice — YOU DO · 25 min

  • Individually complete a response or product demonstrating the original learning outcomes: 9.2d, 9.2e.

Evidence (IEC scaffold): Collect a student-generated response rather than a copied group answer.

6. Extension & differentiation · 10 min

  • See ODF SB 4 p.119 to provide additional guidance on reported speech.
  • As an extension task, more able students can research the Edison–Tesla rivalry in greater depth and report new information back to the class.
  • Additional information: Week 11, Lesson 3
  • Model sentences: Edison and Tesla
  • (For teachers, the order below is the correct order. Cut up and rearrange these sentences before handing out to students.)
  • Put the dialogue into the correct order. Report the sentence(s) with the reporting verb provided.
  • (1882) “I have great pleasure in switching on the world’s first commercial power station using direct current (DC).” ANNOUNCE – Edison
  • “Mr. Tesla, please come and work for our company. Your skills are exactly what we are looking for.” PERSUADE – Edison’s company
  • “We would like you to design some much-needed system improvements. Of course, we will pay you $50,000.” ASK/MENTION – Tesla’s manager
  • “Now the task has been completed, can you pay me the money owed?” ASK – Tesla
  • “Are you joking? The offer was not serious.” CLAIM – Tesla’s manager
  • “Well, if that is how you are going to treat me, I’m leaving. I won’t work another hour in your company.” TELL – Tesla
  • (1886) “I’m planning to use a groundbreaking new technology to supply homes with alternating current (AC).” ANNOUNCE – Tesla
  • “If I were you, I wouldn’t touch alternating current. It can be very dangerous in your homes.” ADVISE – Edison
  • “There is no way we’ll accept the Nobel Prize for Physics together.” REFUSE – Edison and Tesla
  • “Edison was a great scientist who used thorough experimentation methods.” PRAISE – other scientists
  • Students can complete ex.4 in ODF SB 4 p.41, and exs.5–6 in ODF WB 4 p.37.

7. Review, assessment & exit ticket · 10 min

  • Students have discussed the effect of competition on science, looking at the Edison–Tesla rivalry as a case study. They have reviewed how to report speech using reporting verbs, and identified grammatical errors in their work.

Exit ticket (IEC scaffold): Provide one independent example of what you learned.
