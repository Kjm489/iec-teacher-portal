1. Warm-up & activate prior knowledge · 8 min

  • Divide the class into small groups. Give each group a quotation to discuss, and decide whether they agree with it. Suggested quotations:
  • ‘When in doubt, go to the library.’ –J.K. Rowling, Harry Potter and the Chamber of Secrets
  • ‘The only thing that you absolutely have to know, is the location of the library.’ –Albert Einstein
  • ‘Cutting libraries during a recession is like cutting hospitals during a plague.’ –Eleanor Crumblehulme
  • ‘I have found the most valuable thing in my wallet is my library card.’ –Laura Bush
  • ‘Great libraries build communities.’ –David Lankes
  • Invite groups to share their views on the quotations and libraries in general. Highlight that the function of public libraries is a current debate.
  • Remind students that their classmates might have different opinions, and it is an important life skill to try and understand their perspective, even if they don’t agree completely.

ICQ (IEC scaffold): What is the first step and how will you respond?

2. Teacher modeling — I DO · 12 min

  • Teacher demonstrates one example from the Oxford main activity using its original instructions.

Teacher move (IEC scaffold): Think aloud through the first relevant example before asking students to try.

3. Guided practice — WE DO · 15 min

  • Give each group flipchart paper and markers. Instruct them to create a mind map showing all the services that libraries offer.
  • Invite groups to share their ideas with the class (e.g. borrowing books and other digital resources; free internet access; access to all, regardless of income, ethnicity, physical ability; expert staff with specialist knowledge to find information; learning opportunities – computer and literacy skills; a meeting place for reading groups and people with similar interests; a ‘safe place’ to meet – protection of privacy and confidentiality).
  • Establish that libraries have a lot of positive things to offer.
  • Set students the task of planning a ‘for and against’ essay in support of libraries. They need to think of two to three arguments in favour.
  • Give each student a graphic organizer, which they will complete with notes for the introduction, body, and conclusion.
  • In pairs, students present their graphic organizers to each other, showing their partner how they plan to support their arguments. Encourage them to help each other to improve their work, by asking questions and offering suggestions related to the organization of their essay, the strength of their arguments, and the language used.
  • Return to a whole-class discussion. Have students changed their opinions about libraries since the introductory activity? How would they feel if their local public library closed permanently?

CCQ (IEC scaffold): Which detail in the Oxford text or task supports that answer?

4. Supported pair/group practice · 10 min

  • Rehearse a response to the Oxford main activity with a partner, taking turns and using a sentence frame where helpful.

ICQ (IEC scaffold): Will each partner explain their reasoning?

5. Independent practice — YOU DO · 25 min

  • Individually complete a response or product demonstrating the original learning outcomes: 9.2e, 9.4a, 9.4d.

Evidence (IEC scaffold): Collect a student-generated response rather than a copied group answer.

6. Extension & differentiation · 10 min

  • As an extension task, more able students can begin writing the first draft of their essay, using their graphic organizer.
  • Support less confident students in a small group, guided by an adult. Allow them to plan their essay together.
  • Refer students to ODF SB 4 pp.34–35. Ask them to read the text and complete exs.3–4.

7. Review, assessment & exit ticket · 10 min

  • Students have planned a ‘for and against essay’ in support of public libraries. They have considered what public libraries can still offer us in an increasingly digital world.

Exit ticket (IEC scaffold): Provide one independent example of what you learned.
