Year 9 · Week 4 Lesson 3 · Money shapes our lives

Source integrity: Oxford activities are reproduced below in source order; IEC scaffolds are labeled separately.

1. Warm-up & activate prior knowledge · 8 min

  • Write the title ‘Lack of money closes doors, but it opens others’ on the board. Ask the class: What do you think this means? Invite students to discuss their ideas.
  • Write the following words on the board and tell students to copy them: ‘supermarket’ (6), ‘accident/injury’ (3), ‘nursing degree’ (8), ‘tough’ (5), ‘final school exams’ (1), ‘part-time study’ (7), ‘stay at home’ (4), ‘economics degree’ (2).
  • You are going to read aloud an article about a young man called Jacob (see ODF SB 4 p.18). Instruct students to put the words in the order they hear them by adding numbers 1–8. Read the text slowly and clearly.
  • Ask students to compare their answers with their neighbour. Then ask them to use the words to summarize Jacob’s story.

ICQ (IEC scaffold): What is the first step and how will you respond?

2. Teacher modeling — I DO · 12 min

  • Teacher demonstrates one example from the Oxford main activity using its original instructions.

Teacher move (IEC scaffold): Think aloud through the first relevant example before asking students to try.

3. Guided practice — WE DO · 15 min

  • In pairs, students discuss: 1. How does the title sum up Jacob’s story? 2. How does Jacob’s story illustrate the lesson title – ‘Money shapes our lives’?
  • Invite students to share their thoughts with the class (e.g. 1. Jacob’s mother’s accident meant that his plans to go to university changed and he stayed at home to work and look after the family. Although this was hard, he became closer to them, he got more life experience, and it helped him to choose a career that he was passionate about. 2. His lack of money meant Jacob changed his plans from working in business to being a nurse.).
  • Highlight that the article is about Jacob’s past, but it includes future tenses to describe past predictions, plans, and arrangements. Ask students to do ex.3 in ODF SB 4 p.18, then go over the answers.
  • Then ask students to read the paragraph in ex.4 p.19 and complete exs.4–5.
  • Ask pairs of students to write a paragraph using the model on p.19, using a real-life example if possible. To help them, write the following questions on the board:
  • When did this happen?
  • What were you planning to do and when? (use was going to)
  • What happened to prevent this?
  • What alternative arrangements were made? (use past continuous)
  • How did you feel about this?
  • What prediction did you make about the new arrangement? (use would)
  • Before students begin, provide your own real-life paragraph. For example:
  • I was 12 and I was going to move to secondary school after the summer. I was really worried about this but then, unexpectedly, my Dad got a job in South Korea. So instead I was moving to live abroad. I was very nervous, but it would be an amazing chance to experience another culture.
  • Invite students to read out their paragraphs. Encourage the rest of the class to ask questions to elicit further information.

CCQ (IEC scaffold): Which detail in the Oxford text or task supports that answer?

4. Supported pair/group practice · 10 min

  • Rehearse a response to the Oxford main activity with a partner, taking turns and using a sentence frame where helpful.

ICQ (IEC scaffold): Will each partner explain their reasoning?

5. Independent practice — YOU DO · 25 min

  • Individually complete a response or product demonstrating the original learning outcomes: 9.2d, 9.2e.

Evidence (IEC scaffold): Collect a student-generated response rather than a copied group answer.

6. Extension & differentiation · 10 min

  • More able students can write their own stories individually. Less able students may need additional grammar reinforcement (see ODF SB 4 p.115).
  • Complete ex.6 in ODF WB 4 p.17.

7. Review, assessment & exit ticket · 10 min

  • Students have discussed how money can shape people’s lives. They have learned to use the past continuous, was(n’t) going to, and would to talk about the future in the past.

Exit ticket (IEC scaffold): Provide one independent example of what you learned.
