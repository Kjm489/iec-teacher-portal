1. Warm-up & activate prior knowledge · 8 min

  • Ask students to close their eyes for two minutes and focus on all the sounds they can hear.
  • Invite students to share the sounds they heard and note these on the board. Highlight that many sounds go unnoticed until we focus on them.
  • Invite students to call out as many words as they can which imitate sound (e.g. hiss, rustle, growl chirp). Explain that these words are called ‘onomatopoeia’.
  • Note the words on the board (e.g. bark, cackle, flutter, honk, pitter patter, rattle, purr, snarl, squelch, creak, fizz, jangle, screech, slither, slap, whack).
  • In pairs, ask students to organize these words into ‘pleasant’ and ‘unpleasant’ sounds, and discuss how they make them feel. Are their views the same or different?
  • Go over students’ results as a class. Establish what the sound words refer to (e.g. purr – the sound a cat makes; bark – the sound a dog makes; pitter patter – the sound of rain on the window; slither – the sound we associate with something sliding, like a snake).

ICQ (IEC scaffold): What is the first step and how will you respond?

  • Teacher demonstrates one example from the Oxford main activity using its original instructions.

Teacher move (IEC scaffold): Think aloud through the first relevant example before asking students to try.

  • Elicit from students the effect that sound has on us:

  • an emotional effect – psychological
  • an effect on the way we act – behaviour
  • an effect on how we learn – cognitive

  • In groups of four, ask students to discuss types of sound (e.g. meditation music, birdsong, a song on the radio, traffic noise, a loud classroom, rain, a school bell, waves on the beach) and their effects in these four categories. Tell them to make notes in the first row only.
  • Invite students to share their ideas with the class and discuss the results.

Tell them to make notes in the second row as they listen. Play the audio twice for students to check their answers.
  • Make A–B pairs. Ask students to exchange information and complete their tables.
  • Review the listening as a class and engage in a short discussion, comparing students’ initial ideas to the listening text. Ask: How different were they?
