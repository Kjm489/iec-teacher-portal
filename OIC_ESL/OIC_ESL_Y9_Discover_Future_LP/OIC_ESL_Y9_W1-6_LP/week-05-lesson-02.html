Source integrity: Oxford activities are reproduced below in source order; IEC scaffolds are labeled separately.

1. Warm-up & activate prior knowledge · 8 min

  • Ask: Do you know who Andy Warhol is and why he is famous? If not, give a little background information on the late artist.
  • Ask: Do you know any other famous artist, either traditional or contemporary? Can you name any famous works of art?

ICQ (IEC scaffold): What is the first step and how will you respond?

2. Teacher modeling — I DO · 12 min

  • Teacher demonstrates one example from the Oxford main activity using its original instructions.

Teacher move (IEC scaffold): Think aloud through the first relevant example before asking students to try.

3. Guided practice — WE DO · 15 min

  • Tell students that they are going to read a text entitled ‘Trash or Treasure?’. Encourage them to predict what the text might be about. Write their ideas on the board.
  • Direct students to the text in ODF WB 4 p.15. Allow a few minutes for students to read the text for gist and see if any of their predictions are correct.
  • As a whole-class discussion, elicit all the information that students have gathered from their ‘flash’ reading.
  • Hand out a set of pre-prepared ‘scan’ questions which focus on the numbers in the text. Set a short time limit for the activity. Students compare their answers in pairs.
  • Hand out a set of pre-prepared comprehension questions which focus on the red heads and the cookie jars. Set a short time limit for the activity. Students compare their answers in pairs. Then go through the answers as a whole class.
  • While students are working through the questions, display the pictures around the classroom. Tell students to get out of their seats, look carefully at the pictures, and individually rate them as ‘Trash’ or ‘Treasure’. Allow them to compare each other’s answers, highlighting that art is ‘subjective’ and there is no right answer!
  • On the board, write the sale price of the unusual works of art (Warhol’s soup can: $117m, Emin’s ‘My Bed’: $3.77m, Hirst’s shark: $12m, Cattelan’s banana: $120k). See if students can match the prices to the art.
  • Students compare their answers in pairs. Reveal the answers and gauge students’ reactions. Ask: Are you surprised? Why? Do you agree/disagree? Would you classify these examples as ‘art’?
  • Discuss as a class: How do you value art?

CCQ (IEC scaffold): Which detail in the Oxford text or task supports that answer?

4. Supported pair/group practice · 10 min

  • Rehearse a response to the Oxford main activity with a partner, taking turns and using a sentence frame where helpful.

ICQ (IEC scaffold): Will each partner explain their reasoning?

5. Independent practice — YOU DO · 25 min

  • Individually complete a response or product demonstrating the original learning outcomes: 9.2d, 9.2e, 9.3b.

Evidence (IEC scaffold): Collect a student-generated response rather than a copied group answer.

6. Extension & differentiation · 10 min

  • Encourage less able or less confident students to participate actively in the initial prediction activity and the open discussion. They are likely to have an opinion on whether a work of art is worthy of its price tag.
  • More able students can create their own reading comprehension questions to test each other after completing the initial activities.
  • Students can produce a short written summary of ‘Trash or Treasure?’.
  • Students can research a contemporary artist of their choice online and create a profile.

7. Review, assessment & exit ticket · 10 min

  • Students have read a text for gist and specific information. They have thought critically about the nature and value of art, and understood that art is subjective.

Exit ticket (IEC scaffold): Provide one independent example of what you learned.
