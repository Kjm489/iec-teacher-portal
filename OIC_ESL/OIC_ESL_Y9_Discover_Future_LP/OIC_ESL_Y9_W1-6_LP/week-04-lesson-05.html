1. Warm-up & activate prior knowledge · 8 min

  • Ask students if they know what a ‘decision map’ is. If somebody knows, invite them to explain its purpose to the other students.
  • If nobody knows, show the class an example decision map (see ODF SB 4 p.20) and elicit how it works. Establish that it is a diagram (or flowchart) that offers a variety of statements for you to ‘agree’ or ‘disagree’ with. Your responses will lead you down a particular path to a final decision or outcome, based on your values and the kind of person you are.
  • Ask: When would a decision map be useful?

ICQ (IEC scaffold): What is the first step and how will you respond?

2. Teacher modeling — I DO · 12 min

  • Teacher demonstrates one example from the Oxford main activity using its original instructions.

Teacher move (IEC scaffold): Think aloud through the first relevant example before asking students to try.

3. Guided practice — WE DO · 15 min

  • Before giving students the decision map handouts with the green box text removed, list the missing outcomes (e.g. ‘Social Enterprise’) on the board.
  • In pairs, students match the outcomes to the correct boxes, using the statements as a guide. Allow time for the pairs to read the decision map carefully and decide on their final answers. Review as a class, asking students to explain why they made certain decisions. Then compare with the complete map on p.20.
  • Prompt students to use the decision map individually and discover their result. In small groups, ask students to compare and contrast their results, which might lead to an ethical debate. Remind students to listen carefully and respect each other’s points of view – there is no wrong answer. Ask: How did different people arrive at different outcomes? What do you agree and disagree on? Do you think your final result accurately reflects who you are as a person? What are the ethics behind your choices? Reinforce that this exercise sheds light on our ethics and what we consider important.
  • Give students the steps involved in making a decision map, but in the wrong order. The correct order is as follows:
  • define the problem
  • outline the solutions (outcomes)
  • outline the reasons for and against the solutions (outcomes)
  • outline the evidence to support this
  • In their groups, students try to arrange the steps in the correct order. Go through the correct answers as a class.

CCQ (IEC scaffold): Which detail in the Oxford text or task supports that answer?

4. Supported pair/group practice · 10 min

  • Rehearse a response to the Oxford main activity with a partner, taking turns and using a sentence frame where helpful.

ICQ (IEC scaffold): Will each partner explain their reasoning?

5. Independent practice — YOU DO · 25 min

  • Individually complete a response or product demonstrating the original learning outcomes: 9.2a, 9.2d, 9.2e.

Evidence (IEC scaffold): Collect a student-generated response rather than a copied group answer.

6. Extension & differentiation · 10 min

  • Peer-support within groups allows more able students to help less able students.
  • Complete exs.5–6 in ODF SB 4 p.114.
  • Challenge students to use the decision map framework to create their own decision map on a problem of their choice. They can test each other and assess the similarities and differences within the class.

7. Review, assessment & exit ticket · 10 min

  • Students have worked together to solve a problem related to a reading text – a decision map. They have completed, followed, and discussed the results of a decision map on charitable causes, thinking critically about ethics.

Exit ticket (IEC scaffold): Provide one independent example of what you learned.
