Source integrity: Oxford activities are reproduced below in source order; IEC scaffolds are labeled separately.

1. Warm-up & activate prior knowledge · 8 min

  • Write on the board or dictate the following questions (add more if you wish):
  • When should you plan your essay?
  • A: after your first draft
  • B: before you start writing ✓
  • What’s important when selecting evidence for your essay?
  • A: it is relevant ✓
  • B: it is complex
  • What introduces the main point at the start of a paragraph?
  • A: a topic sentence ✓
  • B: an introduction
  • Your conclusion should:
  • A: introduce additional points.
  • B: answer the essay question. ✓
  • Allow students time to discuss the answers in pairs, then review as a class.

ICQ (IEC scaffold): What is the first step and how will you respond?

2. Teacher modeling — I DO · 12 min

  • Teacher demonstrates one example from the Oxford main activity using its original instructions.

Teacher move (IEC scaffold): Think aloud through the first relevant example before asking students to try.

3. Guided practice — WE DO · 15 min

  • Ask the class: How can planning what you want to say help with your essay writing? What information should you include / not include in a plan? Elicit ideas.
  • In groups of three, ask students to think of different ways they can make a plan (e.g. a mind map or graphic organizer). Invite them to write their ideas on the board.
  • In new groups of three (ABC), hand out some essay plan examples. Ask students to discuss the advantages of each method. For instance, an advantage of sticky notes is that you can write one fact on each sticky note and then arrange them in the order you want.
  • Tell students they are going to practise using different planning methods. Give each group member a different method (of your choice) to plan their essay: e.g. Student A – sticky notes; Student B – a mind map; Student C – a bulleted list.
  • Give students the essay title from the last lesson: ‘How do animals use colour?’. Encourage them to note down facts from their own knowledge before allowing them to use the internet to find the extra information they need. Remind students that they need one example for each fact. Allow enough time for students to write their plans.
  • Collect the plans and display them around the classroom. Invite students to walk around and read each other’s ideas.
  • Ask: Which essay planning method do you prefer? Highlight that while it is important to make a plan before writing a factual essay, the planning method they use is an individual choice. They can alter their plans as their ideas evolve and improve.

CCQ (IEC scaffold): Which detail in the Oxford text or task supports that answer?

4. Supported pair/group practice · 10 min

  • Rehearse a response to the Oxford main activity with a partner, taking turns and using a sentence frame where helpful.

ICQ (IEC scaffold): Will each partner explain their reasoning?

5. Independent practice — YOU DO · 25 min

  • Individually complete a response or product demonstrating the original learning outcomes: 9.4a, 9.4c.

Evidence (IEC scaffold): Collect a student-generated response rather than a copied group answer.

6. Extension & differentiation · 10 min

  • Less confident students could do the planning activity in pairs rather than individually.
  • Encourage more able students to write tips about how to write an effective essay plan to share with less confident students.
  • Write an essay checklist which can be saved and used in the next lesson.
  • In pairs, students can produce an essay plan for another factual essay: ‘How do plants use colour?’ (ODF WB 4 p.70 ex.1).

7. Review, assessment & exit ticket · 10 min

  • Students have practised using different planning methods and chosen their favourite one.

Exit ticket (IEC scaffold): Provide one independent example of what you learned.
