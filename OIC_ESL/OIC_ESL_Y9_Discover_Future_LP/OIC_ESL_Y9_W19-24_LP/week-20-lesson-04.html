Main activity

Explain that this week, students will work in small groups on a project of their choice. They can extend their learning into an area of particular interest to them, and practise their English language skills in a practical and relevant way that they could apply to a real-world setting.

Students will focus on a project related to the topics ‘How do we remember the past?’ or ‘What is special about home?’. They should work in small groups with shared areas of interest.

Students can choose their own project, linked to either topic, or they can develop a project from one of the options below.

Encourage students to use the IDEAS to Action framework, which they will be familiar with from their Global Skills Projects lessons. Remind them that this is a smaller-scale project, so they may need to adapt and modify it to suit their needs.

Students will be responsible for the planning and management of their project. This should involve:

suggesting and allocating specific tasks

researching and drafting creative work

reviewing drafts and contributing constructive ideas and suggestions

joint presentation (the following week)

formative self-assessment and peer-assessment (the following week).

Students can present their work in a way that best suits their project, depending on resources and time available. See how creative they can be with their presentations. Here is a non-exhaustive list of different kinds of presentation:

A speech or oral retelling (perhaps supported by visual resources, such as a PowerPoint presentation, photographs, a campaign poster, a model, artefacts, graphs or charts, etc.)

A pitch (to sell a product or service)

A skills demonstration

A debate

An art/photography exhibition or display

An interview with an expert in the field of enquiry

A short film or documentary

A creative performance

Remind students that they have five days to complete the planning, research, and execution of their project. At the end of Day 1, teachers could look over each group’s plan to ensure that they have allocated tasks appropriately and know what to accomplish each day.

Topic 1 (How do we remember the past?)

A documentary about the past: Choose an artefact in a museum or a historical event that your group is interested in exploring further. Then complete the following tasks:

Using a variety of research tools and resources, find out more about:

the origins of the artefact or event;

what happened (to it), including any events leading up to what happened/the discovery of the artefact;

its importance today and what it can teach us;

how people lived during the time period it relates to;

any other pertinent information (e.g. where the artefact is exhibited now and whether it is ethical).

Present your findings in a short film, allocating different roles to different group members (e.g. presenter(s), videographer, director, script writer, etc.). Consider what you can do to make your film visually interesting and inspiring, so the audience is motivated to learn more about your area of research.

Can you guess the decade?: Choose a decade (during any century) that your group is interested in exploring further. Then complete the following tasks:

Using a variety of research tools and resources, find out more about:

how people lived in your chosen decade, considering what kinds of job they did, what school was like, and what household chores were like;

popular culture in your chosen decade – this might include clothes and fashion, sport, entertainment and leisure, literature, and music;

any important events (at a local or global level) that took place in this decade.

Don’t tell other groups what decade you have chosen. Once the research stage is complete, design a time capsule to represent this decade. It can be a physical time capsule (e.g. a shoe box with realia items and photographs [printed from the internet, cut out of magazines/newspapers, or borrowed from friends and family members]) or a large, annotated drawing.

Present the objects in your time capsule to the class, without explaining their significance. Invite members of the audience to guess which decade your capsule represents. You can offer clues to prompt their thinking. Once the correct decade is guessed or revealed, explain your thinking behind each object and why they embody the decade your group has chosen. Every group member should have a speaking role in the presentation.

Topic 2 (What is special about home?)

A poem about home: In continuation of your learning in Week 18, Lesson 3, write another poem about home (or a place that makes you feel safe/at home) independently or in pairs.

You can choose the format of your poem, in line with your own creative interests, but try to use sensory language to capture the feeling of the place.

It can be a rhyming or non-rhyming poem.

Share your poem with a small ‘focus group’ of classmates, who will provide constructive criticism on your ideas and language choices. You can use this feedback to revise and produce a final, illustrated draft of your work.

Present your poem to the class in a creative way.

A nomad’s life: In small groups, create a role-play set in a television talk show/panel show studio. One student will play the role of the host/anchor and the other students will play the roles of interviewees who are either in favour or against nomadic lifestyles. One student can pretend to be somebody who is living a nomadic lifestyle in a campervan. The aim of the task is to engage in a debate about different lifestyles and to reflect on which one offers the best way of living (in terms of comfort, happiness levels, safety, and sustainability). Spend the week planning, scripting, and rehearsing the role-play. You might also want to make or provide props to get into character. When you perform your role-play, invite members of the audience to ask different characters questions.
