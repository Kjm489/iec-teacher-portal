1. Warm-up & activate prior knowledge · 8 min

  • Form small groups of students. Hand out a set of two pictures to each group – one of a messy bedroom, and one of a neat and tidy bedroom.
  • Encourage students to discuss what their initial reactions to these spaces are. How does the messy bedroom make them feel? How does the organized bedroom make them feel? It is likely that different students will have different opinions, as some people find comfort in messy spaces, whereas others find comfort in calm and tidy surroundings. Highlight that this is normal.
  • Bring the class back together and hold a discussion about each other’s thoughts. Make a tally of students who prefer the messy bedroom and those who prefer the tidy one.
  • Elicit that trying to sleep, relax, or do homework in a chaotic or messy space can be distracting. Do students agree/disagree? Why / Why not?

ICQ (IEC scaffold): What is the first step and how will you respond?

2. Teacher modeling — I DO · 12 min

  • Teacher demonstrates one example from the Oxford main activity using its original instructions.

Teacher move (IEC scaffold): Think aloud through the first relevant example before asking students to try.

3. Guided practice — WE DO · 15 min

  • To remind students of their previous learning, play the audio from Week 19, Lesson 1 (track 1.17). Allow them to follow on their transcript handout.
  • Form pairs of students. Explain that they are going to research the benefits of being messy and the benefits of being neat and tidy, on top of what they have already learned from the listening activity. They will then weigh up which of the two is better, in their joint opinion, and share their ideas in a leaflet or slideshow.
  • As students work together to gather information and present their ideas in their preferred format, remind them to check that their main points and supporting reasons are logical. They shouldn’t be based purely on personal preference without evidence to back it up. Elicit that they should be as objective as possible when reading, gathering, processing, and presenting information.
  • Allow students enough time to complete the activity.
  • Invite a few volunteer pairs to present their work in front of the class.
  • At the end of the lesson, ask: Should we always do or try to follow what research advises even if we don’t agree with it? Do you think it matters if some of us have messy bedrooms and some of us have organized bedrooms? How would you feel if everybody was the same?

CCQ (IEC scaffold): Which detail in the Oxford text or task supports that answer?

4. Supported pair/group practice · 10 min

  • Rehearse a response to the Oxford main activity with a partner, taking turns and using a sentence frame where helpful.

ICQ (IEC scaffold): Will each partner explain their reasoning?

5. Independent practice — YOU DO · 25 min

  • Individually complete a response or product demonstrating the original learning outcomes: 9.4a, 9.4c, 9.4f.

Evidence (IEC scaffold): Collect a student-generated response rather than a copied group answer.

6. Extension & differentiation · 10 min

  • Encourage less confident students to extract main points from one of the monologues in the listening activity to inspire their thinking. You could also write them on the board.
  • Form mixed-ability pairs for the main activity so peer-support can be provided.
  • Complete exs.8–10 in ODF WB 4 p.58.
  • Students can present the results from the whole-class tally in a graph (a bar chart or pie chart) then write a short report on their findings. Ask: Are you surprised by the results? What have they taught you?

7. Review, assessment & exit ticket · 10 min

  • In pairs, students have researched the benefits of being messy or organized and presented their opinion in a creative way.

Exit ticket (IEC scaffold): Provide one independent example of what you learned.
