Year 9 · Week 31 Lesson 1 · Why is speed dangerous?

Original Oxford outcome(s): 9.2d, 9.2e, 9.3c

Source integrity: Oxford activities are reproduced below in source order; IEC scaffolds are labeled separately.

1. Warm-up & activate prior knowledge · 8 min

  • Put students into pairs to mind map both the health benefits and the hazards (dangers) of walking and cycling. Encourage pairs to share their ideas with another pair before discussing as a whole class. Pre-teach the words ‘hazard’ (n.) and ‘hazardous’ (adj.).
  • Ask: Which is more hazardous, walking or cycling? Tell students to move to one side of the classroom if they think walking is more hazardous, and to the other if they think cycling is. In these groups, encourage them to share their reasons why.
  • Follow up by asking: Why are pedestrians or cyclists more at risk than people in vehicles?

ICQ (IEC scaffold): What is the first step and how will you respond?

2. Teacher modeling — I DO · 12 min

  • Teacher demonstrates one example from the Oxford main activity using its original instructions.

Teacher move (IEC scaffold): Think aloud through the first relevant example before asking students to try.

3. Guided practice — WE DO · 15 min

  • Ask: Why does it take a vehicle longer to brake when it is travelling faster? Take and discuss responses.
  • Check students know what an ‘emergency stop’ is. Elicit examples of when an emergency stop might be needed (e.g. if a pedestrian walks into the road without looking).
  • Put students into small groups to think of other reasons, apart from speed, that could make stopping distances longer. Invite volunteers to the board to write their ideas.
  • Turn to the ‘Speed Kills’ leaflet in ODF SB 4 p.98. Encourage students to check their ideas on the board against those listed in the leaflet – tiredness and distractions, wet or icy road conditions, downhill driving, tyres in a bad condition, poor visibility. Talk about the meaning of unfamiliar words, and see if students can make logical guesses based on the information they know (context, co-text [i.e. surrounding words], word form).
  • Hold a class discussion about all of these aspects, then focus students’ attention on the picture. Elicit any hazards that they can see (the girl is using her phone while crossing the road, so not paying attention on the traffic). Reinforce that this is dangerous behaviour.
  • Explain to the class that, in their small groups, you want them to use the information from the leaflet to make a five-question survey about the risks that people take on roads.
  • Recommend that they focus on a particular group of people (e.g. cyclists, pedestrians, vehicle passengers). To give an example, they could ask a vehicle passenger: Have you ever distracted a driver by asking them to look at something on your phone?
  • Give groups time to agree on their five best questions and to write them out neatly. They can then collect answers from their classmates in a mingle activity. Group members should also answer their own survey questions to collate results from the whole class.
  • Allow groups time to discuss and analyse their results. Then invite a spokesperson from each group to share them with the class. Discuss if any results were surprising. Students might want to consider changing their habits to be safer on roads.

CCQ (IEC scaffold): Which detail in the Oxford text or task supports that answer?

4. Supported pair/group practice · 10 min

  • Rehearse a response to the Oxford main activity with a partner, taking turns and using a sentence frame where helpful.

ICQ (IEC scaffold): Will each partner explain their reasoning?

5. Independent practice — YOU DO · 25 min

  • Individually complete a response or product demonstrating the original learning outcomes: 9.2d, 9.2e, 9.3c.

Evidence (IEC scaffold): Collect a student-generated response rather than a copied group answer.

6. Extension & differentiation · 10 min

  • Support less able students by pre-teaching new vocabulary and/or eliciting question forms.
  • Challenge more confident students to use a variety of question forms and different types of question (e.g. multiple choice, Yes/No, rating, ranking, and open-ended) in their survey.
  • Ask students to write up the results of their survey. They can also display them graphically (e.g. in pie charts or bar graphs).
  • Discuss the questions in exs.2–3 in ODF SB 4 p.99.

7. Review, assessment & exit ticket · 10 min

  • Students have read a leaflet about vehicle stopping distances and the factors that affect them. They have produced a short survey on the risks people take on roads, and collected answers in a mingle activity.

Exit ticket (IEC scaffold): Provide one independent example of what you learned.
