Main activity

Explain that this week, students will work in small groups on a project of their choice. They can extend their learning into an area of particular interest to them, and practise their English language skills in a practical and relevant way that they could apply to a real-world setting.

Students will focus on a project related to the topics ‘Is speed important?’ or ‘Why do we need art?’. They should work in small groups with shared areas of interest.

Students can choose their own project, linked to either topic, or they can develop a project from one of the options below.

Encourage students to use the IDEAS to Action framework, which they will be familiar with from their Global Skills Projects lessons. Remind them that this is a smaller-scale project, so they may need to adapt and modify it to suit their needs.

Students will be responsible for the planning and management of their project. This should involve:

suggesting and allocating specific tasks

researching and drafting creative work

reviewing drafts and contributing constructive ideas and suggestions

joint presentation (the following week)

formative self-assessment and peer-assessment (the following week).

Students can present their work in a way that best suits their project, depending on resources and time available. See how creative they can be with their presentations. Here is a non-exhaustive list of different kinds of presentation:

A speech or oral retelling (perhaps supported by visual resources, such as a PowerPoint presentation, photographs, a campaign poster, a model, artefacts, graphs or charts, etc.)

A pitch (to sell a product or service)

A skills demonstration

A debate

An art/photography exhibition or display

An interview with an expert in the field of enquiry

A short film or documentary

A creative performance

Remind students that they have five days to complete the planning, research, and execution of their project. At the end of Day 1, teachers could look over each group’s plan to ensure that they have allocated tasks appropriately and know what to accomplish each day.

Topic 1 (Is speed important?)

Speed Awareness Week: Plan a Speed Awareness Week to promote speed awareness in your school. Consider:

A theme: This will help to connect the week’s activities and give the event a catchy title, such as ‘The need for speed!’ or ‘Speed thrills … and kills!’.

Activities to promote speed awareness: This might involve challenges, games, and competitions, such as the best road safety slogan, the creation of a school speed awareness video, or a presention or talk with an invited guest (e.g. a traffic police officer) at assembly.

Event promotion: Think about how the event will be promoted. You could design flyers and/or posters, or draft a letter or email to parents/carers or other students.

Organizers/Helpers: Think about who can help with the project both internally (at school) and outside of school (e.g. a local newspaper or radio station).

The need for speed: Deliver a presention on ‘The need for speed’. Follow these steps:

Think about your audience: Who will watch your presentation, and how will you adapt your language choices and register accordingly?

Select a topic: Agree on a speed-related topic that will be interesting and relevant to your chosen audience. Suggestions: speed world records, speed in space, the impact of speed on the environment (e.g. Can motorsports, like Formula 1, be green sports?), or recent innovations that have been transformed by speed.

Research the topic: Work together to find fascinating facts and supporting evidence, ensuring that the information sources you choose are reliable and can be backed up by other information sources. Also consider incorporating visuals and sound.

Identify the key message: What is the aim of your presentation? To inform, entertain, or persuade? Think about the key points you wish to raise.

Preparation: Consider how your presentation will be structured, and how you will engage your audience with effective language choices. You may wish to create a slideshow – in which case, decide what information you want to include on each slide and which details or useful expressions you want to include on your note cards. Alternatively, you may wish to incorporate drama into your presentation, in the form of a short play or role-play, to grab the attention of your audience and to send a clear message.

Topic 2 (Why do we need art?)

Design an art competition: In small groups, design a competition that will exhibit the artistic talents of students within your school. First, agree on a competition format by working through the following:

Choose an age group: Who will your competition be aimed at? Will you set age restrictions, or will the competition be open to all?

Decide on the purpose of the competition: What will you ask participants to do? How can you make the rules simple and problem-free?

Agree on an appealing theme: You might want to choose an artist or particular art style as an inspiration for students’ entries. Alternatively, you could ask students to submit an artwork with a clear, educational message (e.g. to care for the environment or to be kind to others).

Think about how participants’ entries will be displayed: Will you create a physical display or exhibition? Will they be uploaded to an online platform or using social media? If the latter, what can you do to prevent users from leaving negative feedback?

Agree on a prize and a judging process. Remember to choose a prize suitable for the age group.

Once your group has a final competition design, present it to the class as a slideshow or poster.

Debate: Plan, prepare for, and hold a debate on the importance of art within the community.

Identify an issue which is important for your country or your local community. This could be a widespread issue, such as the presence of graffiti in public spaces or how art can be used to prevent crime; or a specific issue, like the allocation of funds to build a new art gallery or sculpture at the centre of town. Decide on a question or statement (the ‘motion’) that you are going to debate.

Research how debates are usually organized, plan how you will structure yours, then decide who will take on the different roles.

Form two teams to research the arguments for each side of the debate. Remember to research your opponent’s arguments as well, and to prepare an argument against them.

Plan how you are going to hold your audience’s attention and give effective speeches.

Your presentation will be your debate.
