Main activity

Explain that this week, students will be honing their productive skills by exploring a specific speaking or writing genre and producing their own text and/or performance.

These sessions will enable students to practise their integrated language skills in enjoyable and creative ways.

They will produce a piece of work that focuses on an area of particular interest. Try to centre the work on a locally and/or personally relevant topic.

Start by familiarizing students with the chosen genre, and work together to identify and analyse its features. This might be:

video input (e.g. of an inspirational public speaker or a captivating storyteller);

a model text (e.g. a persuasive letter or a short story extract of your choice);

a visiting guest speaker (e.g. a local poet or playwright who performs their work to students in class, then answers students’ questions).

Provide opportunities for critical thinking, discussion, and interaction. For example:

Students could compare and contrast two texts and consider how they use different techniques to achieve their purposes, and why.

Students could talk about how a public speaker or performer delivers their speech successfully or unsuccessfully, and why.

In doing so, students will analyse the effectiveness of particular language choices and think about how to incorporate these into their own work. Where applicable, they will also examine the way in which a speaker delivers a text.

After the initial teacher-led input, tell students that they will be putting their newly acquired skills into practice by producing their own piece of written and/or spoken creative work. Allocate enough time (at least one or two lessons) for them to complete this.

Aim to include a peer feedback stage and, if possible, one-to-one student–teacher discussions to guide students’ thinking and to help them identify areas for improvement.

Remind students that the sessions are to experiment with ideas and techniques, and that there is no formal presentation or assessment.

You may choose one of the workshop ideas below.

Public speaking

Before students give their own speech, build up their confidence in public speaking by practising with storytelling cards.

Pre-prepare sets of cards by writing the name of a person, a place, and an object on three cards in each set. Invite a confident volunteer to select a set of cards, then challenge them to tell a story to the class in one to two minutes, connecting the words on the three cards. Repeat with another volunteer. Students can then continue to play the game in pairs or small groups. If they feel confident, increase the number of cards they select.

If possible, invite a guest speaker into the classroom and record their talk. If this is not possible, encourage students to search for a talk that interests them online. Suggest appropriate websites for your class – for instance, they could choose a TED talk by a young person (online search: ‘TED talks by brilliant kids and teens’).

Encourage students to watch the talk they chose several times so they can study the speaker’s techniques. Ask them to take notes on the following points:

Study their body language.

What do the speakers do with their hands?

How do they stand? Do they pace or stand still?

Do they look at their audience?

Study their voice.

Do they speak quickly or slowly, loudly or softly?

Does their intonation go up or down?

Do they pause? When and why?

Study their vocabulary.

What type of words do they use?

Do they repeat their main message?

How do they introduce and end their talk?

Do they use ‘presentation language’? If so, what phrases do they use?

For their own talk, encourage students to choose a topic that interests them – one which they are passionate or knowledgeable about (e.g. the benefits of a specific sport or activity on mental health, the value of computer games to learning, why young women should be encouraged to study STEM subjects, etc.). Alternatively, more general topics could be:

a favourite character from a book, TV series, or film;

a topical news story of interest.

Set a time limit for each talk, depending on students’ ability and confidence levels (between 5 and 15 minutes). They may do this individually or in pairs. Make sure that they have plenty of practice time.

Encourage students to write their key points on note cards to refer to during their talk. They shouldn’t write their entire talk on these cards if they have the confidence, and sufficient fluency in English, to improvise.

Invite students to perform their talks in small groups or to the whole class.

Students should self- or peer-assess the process and their final talk, reflecting on what they have learned through this workshop.

Persuasive writing

Discuss the term ‘persuade’ and what it means – to convince somebody of your point of view. Recall students’ learning from Week 10, Lessons 4 and 5, when they wrote ‘for and against’ essays in support of public libraries.

Show the class an example of a persuasive essay, letter, poster, or speech. Analyse its structure together. In the case of an essay, it would have a similar structure to the following:

Introduction – a clear opening paragraph that outlines the issue and where you stand on the matter.

Body paragraphs – an argument and a counter-argument, so readers are aware of and can assess all viewpoints. For each key point, reinforce how your viewpoint is the most logical and the best one to adopt, undermining the counter-argument.

Use linking words to connect ideas so your argument flows smoothly: e.g. ‘but’, ‘so’, ‘however’, ‘although’, ‘on the one hand’, ‘on the other hand’, ‘likewise’, ‘similarly’, ‘whereas’, ‘in contrast’, ‘alternatively’.

Support your key points with reasons, facts, and real-life examples.

Strong conclusion – a summary of the arguments outlined in the body paragraphs, before reinforcing your original viewpoint. Leave readers thinking and/or encouraged to get involved.

Elicit other features of persuasive writing:

Personal tone of voice

Use of personal pronouns like ‘you’ and ‘your’

Emotive language and ‘shock tactics’ (i.e. unknown details or surprising facts that will make the reader pay attention and/or have an emotional impact on them)

Repetition of key points for emphasis

Rhetorical questions

Be assertive (e.g. The use of the word ‘is’ instead of ‘some/many people believe that’ will make your point stronger by presenting it as a fact instead of an opinion.)

Put students into pairs to find an example of each feature in the persuasive writing example(s) you have provided. Review as a class.

Work together to plan a piece of persuasive writing on the topic of shortening or lengthening the school day.

Divide the class into two teams (or smaller groups, if preferable) to represent both sides of the argument.

Give teams time to think of up to three key points with supporting evidence.

Bring the class back together and invite volunteers from each team to write their key points on the board. Discuss them as a class, and elicit from each team how one of their key points can undermine a key point from the opposition.

Invite students to suggest examples of emotive language or facts, rhetorical questions, or powerful adjectives that they could use to make their argument even more persuasive.

Now ask students to choose their own topic for a piece of persuasive writing. Give them the freedom to work independently or in pairs, and to choose a format (a persuasive essay, letter, poster, or speech). They should have at least one lesson to plan their work, and one to two lessons to write their first and final drafts.

Students should self- or peer-assess the process and their final piece of work, reflecting on what they have learned through this workshop.

Job interviews

If your Year 9 students studied the world of work in Year 8, elicit what they can remember about applying and interviewing for a job (see Year 8, Week 24).

To get a job interview, a successful CV has to be written and submitted first. It is important that students understand what a CV is and what it should include. Explore the importance of CVs. Some points to consider:

The meaning of CV and what it stands for

Why and when a CV is used

What should be included in a school CV

Qualification expectations or grades

Relevant experience (e.g. trips, project work, volunteering)

Responsibilities (e.g. school monitor or council roles, being a buddy or class mentor, or even looking after a younger brother or sister)

Skills and achievements (e.g. certificates and awards, or the positive outcome of teambuilding activities or projects)

Extra-curricular interests (e.g. sports, crafts, clubs and activities, volunteering)

Search online for simple CV templates. Include both ‘good’ and ‘not so good’ examples. Discuss with students the strengths and weaknesses of the different template types.

Give students a template and an example of a completed CV. Give them time to complete their own CV and to compare it with a partner’s.

Tell students that each time they submit a CV, they must ‘tailor it’ (make it specific) to that role. Create a job analysis checklist as a class. For example:

What is the broader context of the job?

What (job-specific and transferable) skills are they looking for?

What do I need to highlight in my CV?

What do I not need to include in my CV?

Have I included a career objective statement?

Career objective statements should be:

clear, specific, and positive;

tailored to each role applied for;

be no more than four lines long;

show a clear understanding of the experience and skills needed for the role;

refer to relevant skills and experiences;

reflect longer-term career goals.

Put students into small groups to make a tips checklist for a job interview. Suggestions:

Research the company.

Think about your skills in relation to the job.

Consider what the employer is looking for.

Consider what questions the employer will ask.

Dress appropriately.

Switch off your mobile phone or put it on silent.

Ask at least one question.

Write a thank you or follow-up note/email.

Review checklists as a class and bring together ideas to write a final one on the board.

Next, ask groups to produce a list of challenging interview questions (relevant to teenagers), and then to collaborate on answering them. For example:

What are you learning at school that will help you in this role?

How has school prepared you for working/volunteering with us?

Can you think of a time when you overcame a challenging situation?

Watch videos of good/poor interview techniques. Discuss these with students. Can they add any more tips to the whole-class checklist?

Create two or three simple job advertisements for students to choose from. Set them the task of tailoring their CV to this job and preparing for the job interview.

In pairs or groups of three, set up a role-play with one applicant and one or two interviewers. Give the interviewer(s) a few minutes to decide what questions they will ask the interviewee. Remind interviewees to use the checklist on the board. They can then swap roles so everyone has a chance to be interviewed.

Interviews could be recorded so that students are able to reflect on their performance and think about how they could improve. Encourage sensitive peer feedback that focuses on what went well, as well as areas for improvement.
