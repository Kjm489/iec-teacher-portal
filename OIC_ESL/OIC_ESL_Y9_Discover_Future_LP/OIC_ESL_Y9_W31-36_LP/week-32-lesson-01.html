1. Warm-up & activate prior knowledge · 8 min

  • Display the picture of a cave painting. Put students into small groups to discuss: What can you see? Where is it? When do you think it was painted? Why do you think it was painted?
  • Introduce ‘cave painting’, if not mentioned already by students. Invite groups to share any knowledge they have of this kind of art.

ICQ (IEC scaffold): What is the first step and how will you respond?

2. Teacher modeling — I DO · 12 min

  • Teacher demonstrates one example from the Oxford main activity using its original instructions.

Teacher move (IEC scaffold): Think aloud through the first relevant example before asking students to try.

3. Guided practice — WE DO · 15 min

  • Give each pair of students a set of gapped statements, or write them on the board. Tell them to read the sentences and guess which words go in the gaps.
  • Write the list of missing words on the board in a random order. Ask pairs to check their guesses and to complete the statements with these words, reminding them to use context to help them. Were their guesses correct?
  • Put students into small groups to discuss which statements they agree or disagree with and why. Invite a few volunteers to share their ideas.
  • Tell the class that they are going to watch a video that refers to these statements. Play the video (see ex.3 in ODF SB 4 p.103) and ask students to note down what Ryan and Ava say.
  • Give students time to compare their answers in pairs before reviewing as a class.
  • Divide the class in half (As and Bs) and play the video a second time. Ask A students to listen for information about Ryan’s purpose, Ancient Greek pottery, and contemporary artists; while B students should listen for information about cave paintings, ordinary people, and fish sculptures.
  • Put students into AB pairs to share their notes before reviewing as a class.
  • Then write the following questions on the board for small groups to discuss:
  • Should art be beautiful, or do you agree with Ava that it’s an old-fashioned idea?
  • What is your favourite piece of art? Why?
  • How are famous people commemorated in your country? Give examples.
  • Bring the class back together. Have a discussion about the question: How effective is art at highlighting important issues, such as global warming or homelessness?

CCQ (IEC scaffold): Which detail in the Oxford text or task supports that answer?

4. Supported pair/group practice · 10 min

  • Rehearse a response to the Oxford main activity with a partner, taking turns and using a sentence frame where helpful.

ICQ (IEC scaffold): Will each partner explain their reasoning?

5. Independent practice — YOU DO · 25 min

  • Individually complete a response or product demonstrating the original learning outcomes: 9.1a, 9.1b, 9.1f, 9.2e.

Evidence (IEC scaffold): Collect a student-generated response rather than a copied group answer.

6. Extension & differentiation · 10 min

  • To support less able students, pre-teach vocabulary introduced in the video, or encourage more able students to explain new vocabulary in their own words.
  • Allow less able students to follow the transcript as they watch the video.
  • Challenge less confident students to give at least one opinion during group discussion.
  • Challenge more confident students to listen for all the main points in the video.
  • Complete exs.1–2 in ODF WB 4 p.96.
  • Create a whole-class list of art-related vocabulary.
  • Encourage students to research a famous artist from their own country, including photographs of their work, and to write down five facts about them. Invite them to present their findings to the rest of the class.

7. Review, assessment & exit ticket · 10 min

  • Students have thought critically about the purpose of art. They have watched a video on the topic, identifying main information and sharing their learning with others. They have considered how effective art is at highlighting important issues.

Exit ticket (IEC scaffold): Provide one independent example of what you learned.
