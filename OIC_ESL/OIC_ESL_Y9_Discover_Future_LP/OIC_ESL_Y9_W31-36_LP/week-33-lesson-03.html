Source integrity: Oxford activities are reproduced below in source order; IEC scaffolds are labeled separately.

1. Warm-up & activate prior knowledge · 8 min

  • Display the pictures of your chosen artworks. Give students time to observe them, and then to describe what they can see in pairs.
  • As students are working, write the following questions on the board. Hold a class discussion in response to the questions, before sharing information about each artist.
  • What do you think influenced these artists?
  • Do you think artists influence other artists? How?

ICQ (IEC scaffold): What is the first step and how will you respond?

2. Teacher modeling — I DO · 12 min

  • Teacher demonstrates one example from the Oxford main activity using its original instructions.

Teacher move (IEC scaffold): Think aloud through the first relevant example before asking students to try.

3. Guided practice — WE DO · 15 min

  • In small groups, ask students to think of topics which might influence artists. Elicit ideas.
  • If not mentioned in feedback, ask: What is ecological art? (artwork that raises awareness about environmental issues or ‘rehabilitates’ [brings back to health] polluted spaces) How do you think ecological art can influence us?
  • Display some examples of ecological artworks. Suggestions to research:
  • Patricia Johanson, Leonhardt Lagoon, 1985 – a sculpture
  • Agnès Dénes, Tree Mountain, A Living Time Capsule, 1992 – a manmade mountain
  • Guerra De La Paz, Spring Sprang Sprung, 2009 – an installation of recycled clothing
  • Angeles Peña, Deshielo glaciar, 2015 – photography
  • Lorenzo Quinn, Support, 2017 – a sculpture
  • Then introduce some key phrases of observation and inference. Refer students to the examples in ex.5 in ODF SB 4 p.109. Write them on the board if required.
  • Divide the class in half (AB) and then into pairs. Tell students they are going to practise these key phrases while discussing the ecological artworks you have displayed.
  • Ask Group A pairs to discuss:
  • What is happening in the artwork?
  • What do you think it is about?
  • What shapes and colours has the artist used? Why?
  • Ask Group B pairs to discuss:
  • Can you describe its texture? If you could touch it, what would it feel like?
  • Where was it created? Why?
  • How does the artwork make you feel? Why?
  • Form groups of four (one A pair and one B pair). Ask students to exchange ideas, reminding them to use the key phrases of observation and inference when doing so.
  • Bring the class back together for feedback.
  • Write on the board: ‘Art is nothing if you don’t reach every segment of the people.’ – Keith Haring (subway pop graffiti artist). Allow students time to consider the quotation. Discuss what they think the speaker meant and whether or not they agree.

CCQ (IEC scaffold): Which detail in the Oxford text or task supports that answer?

4. Supported pair/group practice · 10 min

  • Rehearse a response to the Oxford main activity with a partner, taking turns and using a sentence frame where helpful.

ICQ (IEC scaffold): Will each partner explain their reasoning?

5. Independent practice — YOU DO · 25 min

  • Individually complete a response or product demonstrating the original learning outcomes: 9.2a, 9.2b, 9.2f.

Evidence (IEC scaffold): Collect a student-generated response rather than a copied group answer.

6. Extension & differentiation · 10 min

  • Pair up mixed abilities so more confident students can provide linguistic support and encouragement to their less confident peers.
  • Challenge more confident students to summarize the artist’s message in their own words.
  • Complete exs.6–7 in ODF SB 4 p.109.
  • Students can design their own ecological artwork on an environmental issue.

7. Review, assessment & exit ticket · 10 min

  • Students have learned about and discussed ecological art using key phrases of observation and inference.

Exit ticket (IEC scaffold): Provide one independent example of what you learned.
