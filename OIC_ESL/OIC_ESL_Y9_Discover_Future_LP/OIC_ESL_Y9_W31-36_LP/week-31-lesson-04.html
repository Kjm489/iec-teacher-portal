1. Warm-up & activate prior knowledge · 8 min

  • Remind students that in yesterday’s lesson (Week 31, Lesson 3) they read a description of an exciting experience. Ask: What kind of experience was it? (See ODF SB 4 p.100.)
  • Put students into small groups to list everything they learned about what makes descriptions interesting. Go through the answers together (see ‘Writing strategy’ in ODF SB 4 p.100). By revising these strategies, students are helping to build their long-term memory of this learning.

ICQ (IEC scaffold): What is the first step and how will you respond?

2. Teacher modeling — I DO · 12 min

  • Teacher demonstrates one example from the Oxford main activity using its original instructions.

Teacher move (IEC scaffold): Think aloud through the first relevant example before asking students to try.

3. Guided practice — WE DO · 15 min

  • In today’s lesson, students are going to make a plan for a description of an experience that was either fast (like the description of the skier) or slow.
  • Give students a minute to quietly think about (a) suitable experience(s) to write about. Then hand out the flipchart or A3 paper to small groups of students. Set a time limit for groups to note down and discuss all their fast and slow experience ideas.
  • Instruct groups to swap their mind map with another group and to add their ideas.
  • Repeat until each mind map has returned to its original group.
  • Now ask groups to choose one of the ideas, and to write down five or more exciting words or phrases they could use underneath. They may refer back to their notes from previous lessons to remind them of their learning.
  • Using their mind map notes, set groups the task of writing an opening sentence to their description that will capture the reader’s attention and set the scene.
  • Encourage groups to share their opening sentence with another group. Give them time to offer constructive feedback and to help with error correction. Remind them that making mistakes and receiving feedback from our peers helps us to learn and improve.
  • Invite a volunteer from each group to stand up and read their opening sentence to the class. Encourage them to speak clearly and with feeling. The class should freely draw what they think or feel as they listen. Reassure students that their drawings do not need to be great – it is a strategy to help them evaluate how well the opening sentences grab the reader’s attention and set the scene.
  • If they feel comfortable, invite students to show their drawings and explain their thoughts as they were listening. Which opening sentences were the most inspiring or easy to draw?
  • Next lesson, students will write their description of a fast or slow experience, so encourage them to reflect on their ideas after class. Keep their plans safe for the next lesson.

CCQ (IEC scaffold): Which detail in the Oxford text or task supports that answer?

4. Supported pair/group practice · 10 min

  • Rehearse a response to the Oxford main activity with a partner, taking turns and using a sentence frame where helpful.

ICQ (IEC scaffold): Will each partner explain their reasoning?

5. Independent practice — YOU DO · 25 min

  • Individually complete a response or product demonstrating the original learning outcomes: 9.4a, 9.4b.

Evidence (IEC scaffold): Collect a student-generated response rather than a copied group answer.

6. Extension & differentiation · 10 min

  • Students who are less able linguistically may excel at drawing. Acknowledge their strengths.
  • Challenge less confident students to read aloud their group’s opening sentence, then praise them for speaking in front of the class.
  • Fast finishers can produce a more detailed plan, outlining their ideas paragraph by paragraph.
  • Complete exs.1–4 in ODF WB 4 p.90 (if not completed previously).
  • Make a whole-class list of interesting language in preparation for next lesson.

7. Review, assessment & exit ticket · 10 min

  • Students have worked together to create a plan for a descriptive piece of writing. They have written an opening sentence and considered the effectiveness of others’ opening sentences in a drawing activity.

Exit ticket (IEC scaffold): Provide one independent example of what you learned.
