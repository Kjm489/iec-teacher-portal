Source integrity: Oxford activities are reproduced below in source order; IEC scaffolds are labeled separately.

1. Warm-up & activate prior knowledge · 8 min

  • Invite students to stand up and find a partner. They should stand facing each other and study what their partner is wearing for 30 seconds.
  • Ask them to stand back to back and take turns describing what their partner is wearing.
  • Bring the class back together for feedback. Check that students know, or can work out, what the word ‘observant’ means before asking: How observant are you?

ICQ (IEC scaffold): What is the first step and how will you respond?

2. Teacher modeling — I DO · 12 min

  • Teacher demonstrates one example from the Oxford main activity using its original instructions.

Teacher move (IEC scaffold): Think aloud through the first relevant example before asking students to try.

3. Guided practice — WE DO · 15 min

  • Put students into groups of five. Ask them to make a mind map of the benefits of being observant. For instance, we can tell when a friend is upset, or it helps us to recognize danger. Invite students to write their ideas on the board for discussion. Elicit that being observant helps with things like assessing someone’s mood and noticing what they need.
  • Ask: Can we learn to be more observant? If so, how? Discuss as a class.
  • Tell students that they are going to read a blog post with the title ‘5 Easy Steps to Being More Observant’. Dictate the following words from the blog: ‘location’, ‘senses’, ‘headphones’, ‘first impression’, and ‘notebook’. Put students into pairs to predict the five steps using both the title and the key words to help them. Elicit ideas.
  • Returning to the same groups of five from earlier, give each student a different step to read. Encourage them to make notes. Remind them that they do not need to know every word to understand the gist of the paragraph. Then give students time to share what they have read, using their notes, with the rest of their group.
  • Check students’ understanding of the text as a whole class, then challenge them to use the reading strategies they know to work out the meaning of unknown words.
  • Refer students to the complete blog post (see ODF SB 4 p.108). Ask: Which tips do you think are the most and least realistic? Why?
  • Remind students that when we read any text, we should think critically about who wrote it and why it was written. It is also important to identify facts and opinions. Ask:
  • Who do you think wrote the blog post?
  • What motivated them to write it?
  • How knowledgeable is the writer? How do you know?
  • Is the blog based on fact or opinion? How do you know?
  • Remind students that in the introductory activity they discovered how observant they were. Encourage them to reflect individually on their own observation skills, and to consider in which situations improving their observations skills would be helpful.

CCQ (IEC scaffold): Which detail in the Oxford text or task supports that answer?

4. Supported pair/group practice · 10 min

  • Rehearse a response to the Oxford main activity with a partner, taking turns and using a sentence frame where helpful.

ICQ (IEC scaffold): Will each partner explain their reasoning?

5. Independent practice — YOU DO · 25 min

  • Individually complete a response or product demonstrating the original learning outcomes: 9.3a, 9.3b, 9.3e.

Evidence (IEC scaffold): Collect a student-generated response rather than a copied group answer.

6. Extension & differentiation · 10 min

  • For additional support, pre-teach observation verbs using ODF SB 4 p.130 exs.5–6.
  • Give less able students either paragraphs 1 or 2 to read and summarize.
  • Fast finishers can research ‘observation skills development’, making notes of key points to present to the class.
  • Encourage students to write down three personal goals to be more observant.
  • Set students grammar practice exercises on observation verbs in ODF SB 4 p.130 exs.5–6 and in ODF WB 4 p.98 exs.9–10.

7. Review, assessment & exit ticket · 10 min

  • Students have predicted the content of a blog post using the title and key words. In groups, they have read and summarized five pieces of advice on being more observant, before reflecting on their own observation skills.

Exit ticket (IEC scaffold): Provide one independent example of what you learned.
