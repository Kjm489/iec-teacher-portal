1. Warm-up & activate prior knowledge · 8 min

  • Display the pictures of fast activities. Put students into pairs to describe what the people in each sport or activity are doing.
  • Encourage students to say whether they would do any of these activities, giving reasons and examples to support their answers.
  • Introduce the term ‘thrill seeker’. Ask: Why do you think some people get a thrill from going fast? What is their motivation?

ICQ (IEC scaffold): What is the first step and how will you respond?

2. Teacher modeling — I DO · 12 min

  • Teacher demonstrates one example from the Oxford main activity using its original instructions.

Teacher move (IEC scaffold): Think aloud through the first relevant example before asking students to try.

3. Guided practice — WE DO · 15 min

  • Write the following words on the board: ‘maths’, ‘typing’, and ‘eating’. Tell students they are going to read three paragraphs that include these words in the headings.
  • In their pairs, ask students to predict what the paragraphs have in common.
  • Put students into groups of three (ABC). Give paragraph 1 to Student A, 2 to B, and 3 to C. Instruct them to read their paragraph quickly and make notes of the key points.
  • Now ask students to turn their paragraph over so it is face down on the table. Using their notes, they summarize what they read to their group.
  • Ask groups to decide between themselves what the three paragraphs have in common (they are competitions based on speed). Ask: Were your predictions correct?
  • Discuss further: Would you like to take part in these competitions? Why / Why not?
  • Explain to the class that the school wants to organize a speed-related competition to compete against other schools in the area. Encourage groups to come up with an exciting idea to ‘pitch’ (present) to the school’s trustees. They should consider:
  • What the activity is (ensuring that it is safe);
  • How the speed of the activity will be measured;
  • Who can take part;
  • Who will judge it;
  • What prizes, if any, will be awarded;
  • Why the school trustees should choose their idea.
  • Give groups enough time to come up with a good idea. Remind them how using persuasive language (see Week 29, Lesson 4) will help to convince their audience of their idea’s worth. Encourage them to refer back to their notes on persuasive phrases.
  • In turn, invite groups to pitch their ideas to the class. Encourage listening students to ask questions at the end. Hold a whole-class vote on which idea(s) should be taken forward.

CCQ (IEC scaffold): Which detail in the Oxford text or task supports that answer?

4. Supported pair/group practice · 10 min

  • Rehearse a response to the Oxford main activity with a partner, taking turns and using a sentence frame where helpful.

ICQ (IEC scaffold): Will each partner explain their reasoning?

5. Independent practice — YOU DO · 25 min

  • Individually complete a response or product demonstrating the original learning outcomes: 9.2a, 9.2f, 9.2g.

Evidence (IEC scaffold): Collect a student-generated response rather than a copied group answer.

6. Extension & differentiation · 10 min

  • The group speaking activity should allow all students to play to their strengths, as linguistically less able students will be able to contribute creative ideas. Encourage more able students to take the lead in the pitch and to support their peers with topic-related and persuasive language.
  • Students can write a short blog post about their imagined competition.
  • Complete exs.6–7 in ODF WB 4 p.87.

7. Review, assessment & exit ticket · 10 min

  • Students have used headings to predict what parts of a text have in common. They have come up with a speed competition idea for the school to pitch in a persuasive way.

Exit ticket (IEC scaffold): Provide one independent example of what you learned.
