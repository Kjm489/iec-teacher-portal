Source integrity: Oxford activities are reproduced below in source order; IEC scaffolds are labeled separately.

1. Warm-up & activate prior knowledge · 8 min

  • Remind students that last lesson they produced a plan for a cause and effect essay: ‘How can our actions affect water?’.
  • Ask students to return to their groups. Hand out their plans. Give them time to remind themselves of their main points, as well as the features of a good cause and effect essay. They may refer back to ODF SB 4 p.88 and ODF WB 4 p.80 to guide them.
  • Ask: Can you remind me how to structure a good cause and effect essay? Note their responses on the board. Explain that they have to practise certain things over and over in order to master them.

ICQ (IEC scaffold): What is the first step and how will you respond?

2. Teacher modeling — I DO · 12 min

  • Teacher demonstrates one example from the Oxford main activity using its original instructions.

Teacher move (IEC scaffold): Think aloud through the first relevant example before asking students to try.

3. Guided practice — WE DO · 15 min

  • In today’s lesson, students are going to work together in their groups to write their cause and effect essay, applying all of their learning from the unit. They should spend most of the lesson working on this.
  • Work together as a class to create a suitable checklist for a cause and effect essay. Groups will refer to this checklist after writing their first draft. Try to categorize checklist items (e.g. language [including cause and effect verbs and phrases], structure, register and tone).
  • Tell groups to write an introduction to their essay. Remind them that they need one sentence to introduce the topic, and one to outline its content.
  • Next, give groups enough time to write their paragraph explaining the causes (i.e. the things that we do that use too much water).
  • Repeat for the paragraph explaining the effects (i.e. the results of our water consumption) and the conclusion.
  • Encourage groups to read through their work, checking for accuracy, for activity requirements, for meaning, and for effect on the reader.
  • Distribute the essays between groups. Say: Imagine you are the teacher. What three things did the group that wrote this essay do well, and what two things could they improve? Note down the feedback you want to give. Use the checklist to help you.
  • Allow groups time to give and receive feedback sensitively and to make helpful suggestions for improvement.
  • Invite volunteers to share what their group did well and what they could do to improve their work. Reinforce that constructive criticism is a useful tool that helps us to improve – if we never made mistakes, we would never learn and grow!

CCQ (IEC scaffold): Which detail in the Oxford text or task supports that answer?

4. Supported pair/group practice · 10 min

  • Rehearse a response to the Oxford main activity with a partner, taking turns and using a sentence frame where helpful.

ICQ (IEC scaffold): Will each partner explain their reasoning?

5. Independent practice — YOU DO · 25 min

  • Individually complete a response or product demonstrating the original learning outcomes: 9.4d, 9.4e, 9.4f.

Evidence (IEC scaffold): Collect a student-generated response rather than a copied group answer.

6. Extension & differentiation · 10 min

  • Less able groups/students should receive extra guidance during the writing task. Monitor them carefully and elicit what they need to do at each stage. Provide a list of useful sentence starters to help them.
  • Challenge more confident students to include complex topic vocabulary learned in the unit in their essays.
  • Groups can rework their essay based on the feedback received and produce a final draft.

7. Review, assessment & exit ticket · 10 min

  • Review the original Oxford learning outcomes.

Exit ticket (IEC scaffold): Provide one independent example of what you learned.
