Introductory activity

Write the following quotation on the board: ‘There is more to life than simply increasing its speed’ – Mahatma Gandhi.

Put students into small groups to discuss: What do you think Gandhi meant when he said this? How far do you agree with this statement?

Encourage students to give reasons to support their ideas in a whole-class discussion.

Ask the class: What can you remember about the advantages of speed from last lesson?

Put students into groups of four (AABB). Tell pair AA to mind map the advantages of completing homework quickly, and pair BB to mind map the disadvantages. (If students don’t have homework at your school, adapt the theme accordingly.)

Bring the groups back together. Encourage them to have a short debate for and against completing homework quickly. Remind them to acknowledge what their classmate said before introducing a counter-argument: e.g. A: I believe completing homework quickly is advantageous because …; B: I see what you mean. However, …

Ask the class: If you could stop time, what would you do and why? Invite volunteers to share their responses. They can be as a creative and silly as possible!

Then write the following list on the board: ‘watching TV’, ‘doing homework’, ‘playing sports’, ‘having new experiences’, ‘sleeping’.

Encourage students to discuss in pairs: Does time seem to speed up or slow down during these activities?

Share ideas as a class. Introduce the well-known expression: ‘Time flies when you’re having fun!’ Ask students to consider why people say this and whether they agree.

Put the three slips of paper on three different tables. Divide the class into three groups.

Say: You are going to time travel together! You will travel to the past, the present, and the future (indicate to each table as you say each time period).

When groups arrive at each table, they should discuss things they have in common with each other about that time period. For instance, they might share memories of old technology or childhood toys (the past), talk about how they travel to and from school (the present), or discuss their interest in buying the latest robot (the future).

Give each group three to five minutes at a table then move them on to the next table.

Bring the class back together. Ask: If you could travel to any time, in the past or in the future, to which time would you travel and why?

Ask students to stand up and form a circle. Tell them they are going into space and can take one possession with them. Say: I’m going into space and I’m taking my cat (or similar). Indicate to a student and ask them to repeat what you said and add their own item: e.g. I’m going into space and I’m taking my cat and my slippers. Continue around the circle until everyone has added an item to the list. Can they remember all the items?

Students return to their seats. Ask: If you had the opportunity to travel into space and stay there for two years, would you go? Why / Why not?

Main activity

Tell students they are going to watch a video that answers the question: What is the need for speed? Put students into pairs to predict the content of the video.

Play the video once (ODF SB 4 p.93 ex.3) and encourage students to listen for gist. After the first listen, ask: Were your predictions correct? Discuss as a class.

Play the video a second time. Now ask students to note down the advantages of speed that Ryan and Ava mention.

Give students time to compare their answers in pairs, before sharing with the class.

Hand out the gapped transcripts. Ask students to skim the text so they know where the gaps are. Then play the video a third time. Challenge students to complete the gaps with the missing words or phrases.

Give students time to compare their answers in pairs, before sharing with the class.

Check students understand the meaning of the key vocabulary and write it on the board. Ask, for example: Can you work out the meaning of ‘at a snail’s pace’? How quickly does a snail move? Can you work out the meaning of the idiom ‘get a move on’ by breaking the phrase down into parts? What is the register of this phrase? What is implied by ‘breakneck speed’? What two words is ‘breakneck’ made up of?

Ask students to try to include this vocabulary in five questions for their classmates to answer. Give students an example: e.g. Do you enjoy travelling at breakneck speed on a rollercoaster? Questions should be personal, and ones that their classmates can answer.

Once students have produced their five questions, invite them to stand up, mingle, and take turns asking and answering their questions. Tell them to ask five different classmates a question to increase the number of people they talk to.

Bring the class back together. Hold a class vote to establish who loves the adrenaline rush of speed (on a rollercoaster, skydiving, etc.) and who hates it. Encourage students to consider the differences and similarities in the class, and to appreciate that we are all different with varying interests.

In the same pairs, ask students to think of as many phrases as they can for explaining advantages and disadvantages.

Elicit suggestions and write them on the board. See the examples in ODF SB 4 p.93 ex.5.

In follow-up to the short introductory debate, dictate the following school-related topics: Getting to School, Lunchtime, Playtime, Sports, Exams, Maths lessons, School Holidays.

Form new groups of four students. Tell half the groups to think of the advantages of speed in relation to these topics, and the other half to consider the disadvantages. Encourage them to be creative. For instance, one group might suggest that a shorter lunch break will mean that students can spend more time studying, while the other group might suggest that an hour-long lunch break is important for students’ mental wellbeing at school.

Remind students to think of examples to support their ideas.

When groups have some ideas, challenge them to write down some questions someone with an opposing view might ask them. Explain that this will help them to prepare for the debate. This is a ‘stretch zone’ activity to practise their speaking skills and to develop their ability to formulate and respond to an effective argument.

Form larger groups with an equal number of students on both sides of the argument. Tell groups to choose a topic and then to hold a short debate related to the advantages and disadvantages of speed within the context of school. Each group member should have a speaking role. Remind them to acknowledge what their classmate said before introducing a counter-argument, and to alternate between sides.

Encourage students to pause and take a few breaths before they answer a question.

Bring the class back together and talk about how students are feeling. Did they feel stretched? Did they enjoy listening carefully and thinking of a counter-argument ‘on the spot’? Did they feel supported by their group members? What could they improve?

Display the heading and subheading of the reading text in ODF SB 4 p.95. Tell students to read it to themselves before putting them into groups of three. Ask:

What do you think ‘time is relative’ means?

What questions do you think the article will answer?

Share ideas as a class. Hand out a set of headings to each group. Tell them to read them through and to see if their questions were similar.

Then hand out copies of the text with the headings removed. Tell groups to work together to match the headings to the paragraphs. They can do this using their preferred strategy – by reading each paragraph together, or by allocating a paragraph to each group member (fast finishers can work on the remaining paragraphs). Remind students to support each other to understand difficult vocabulary and concepts.

Give students enough time to complete the task, then check answers as a whole class. Encourage students to explain why they made particular decisions.

Ask students to read the text again independently. Discuss as a class what most and least surprised them.

Ask: Can you find me an example of a fact? Can you find me an example of speculation?

Explain that Einstein published his theory in 1905. Ask: What do you think people thought about Einstein’s theory when he first published it? Would people have believed him? How do you think Einstein felt about this?

Write on the board: ‘Time Travel Agent’. Put students into pairs and ask them to consider what a time travel agent might do (encourage people to visit a particular time period). Elicit suggestions.

Tell students that they are going to pretend to be time travel agents and write a persuasive speech to encourage their peers to visit a particular destination. Write the prepared list of destinations on the board. Invite pairs to select a destination they are interested in, ensuring an equal coverage, or give them the opportunity to think of their own.

Before beginning the speaking task, hand out the worksheets to familiarize the class with persuasive language. Give students enough time to make a good attempt at completing the gapped phrases, then go through the answers as a class. Encourage students to use persuasive phrases in their speech.

Give pairs enough time to write and rehearse a short speech. They may use the internet to find key facts about their time period.

Invite pairs to deliver their speeches in small groups to help build their confidence. Remind them to use word stress for emphasis.

After each speech, encourage the listening students to summarize what they heard, and to highlight what aspects were delivered in a persuasive way.

Praise students for their effort in using persuasive language in their speeches.

Put students into pairs. Hand out, or display, the pre-prepared questions about the International Space Station. Encourage them to try to work out the answers. Then share ideas as a whole class.

Direct students to the website where the answers can be located and challenge them to find the information. Tell them that there may be words they don’t understand, so they will need to decide which are relevant to helping them find the answers. They can work in pairs or individually on this task.

Elicit the answers and discuss which facts most and least surprised them.

Write the job titles on the board. In small groups, ask students to discuss and note down the character traits needed for each job. To support, prompt them to think about what the job will involve. Allow them to research the jobs online, if necessary.

Bring the class back together. Invite students to write their chosen personality traits on the board and discuss/debate as a class. Ask: Are there some unusual personality traits needed for jobs in space?

Inform groups that they are going to work in the International Space Station for 12 months. Before leaving, they will need to decide who within their group will do each job, based on each person’s strengths and character traits. Give groups enough time to discuss and make their final decision.

Bring the class back together. Encourage students to present and explain their final decision, highlighting their classmates’ and their own strengths as they do so.

Discuss as a class: Some people believe space exploration is a waste of money and resources. Do you agree or disagree?

Additional tasks

Students can use the internet to find other speed-related quotations to discuss in groups.

Encourage them to make a broader list of the advantages of speed. (Note: The disadvantages will be covered in Week 29, Lesson 2.)

Put students into pairs to role-play ‘An interview with Einstein’. One will play the role of Albert Einstein, the other will play the role of a local reporter. Encourage students to ‘get into character’ and to consider their motivations. Give them time to prepare questions and answers before performing their role-play.

Complete exs.3–5 in ODF SB 4 p.94.

Learning review

Students have predicted the content of a video about speed, then listened for gist and detail. They have incorporated new vocabulary in a question-and-answer mingle activity.

Students have worked in groups to think of the advantages and disadvantages of speed within the context of school. They have considered questions those with an opposing view may ask in preparation for a short debate.

Students have predicted the content of a text using its heading and subheading. They have worked together to match the headings to the paragraphs.

Students have practised using persuasive language. In pairs, they have worked together to write and deliver a persuasive speech about a chosen time period.

Students have used the internet to find specific information about the International Space Station. They have discussed the character traits needed for jobs in space, then allocated jobs to group members based on their strengths. They have discussed if space exploration is worthwhile.

Ask students to write an opinion piece about the advantages and disadvantages of the school week being shortened from five days to four. Remind them that there should be equal weight on both sides of the argument.

Ask pairs to work together to find more persuasive phrase to create a whole-class list.
