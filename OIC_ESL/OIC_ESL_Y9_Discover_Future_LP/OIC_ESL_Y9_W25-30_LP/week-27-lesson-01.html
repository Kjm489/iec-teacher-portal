Main activity

Explain that this week, students will work in small groups on a project of their choice. They can extend their learning into an area of particular interest to them, and practise their English language skills in a practical and relevant way that they could apply to a real-world setting.

Students will focus on a project related to the topics ‘Why do colours matter?’ or ‘How does water affect our lives?’. They should work in small groups with shared areas of interest.

Students can choose their own project, linked to either topic, or they can develop a project from one of the options below.

Encourage students to use the IDEAS to Action framework, which they will be familiar with from their Global Skills Projects lessons. Remind them that this is a smaller-scale project, so they may need to adapt and modify it to suit their needs.

Students will be responsible for the planning and management of their project. This should involve:

suggesting and allocating specific tasks

researching and drafting creative work

reviewing drafts and contributing constructive ideas and suggestions

joint presentation (the following week)

formative self-assessment and peer-assessment (the following week).

Students can present their work in a way that best suits their project, depending on resources and time available. See how creative they can be with their presentations. Here is a non-exhaustive list of different kinds of presentation:

A speech or oral retelling (perhaps supported by visual resources, such as a PowerPoint presentation, photographs, a campaign poster, a model, artefacts, graphs or charts, etc.)

A pitch (to sell a product or service)

A skills demonstration

A debate

An art/photography exhibition or display

An interview with an expert in the field of enquiry

A short film or documentary

A creative performance

Remind students that they have five days to complete the planning, research, and execution of their project. At the end of Day 1, teachers could look over each group’s plan to ensure that they have allocated tasks appropriately and know what to accomplish each day.

Topic 1 (Why do colours matter?)

Seeing red: In pairs or small groups, research ways in which the colour red can affect us mentally and physically. Complete the following tasks:

Use the internet to carry out research. You can focus on one key area or theme, or choose to include contrasting ideas from different cultures. Include background information, examples, and reasons.

Take photographs of examples of the colour red at school, at home, or outdoors, and consider its function or impact. Use these photographs to create and carry out a survey which tests how people feel about the colour red. Decide on a survey group (e.g. students at school, teachers, parents/carers, etc.).

Compare the survey results with your initial research findings. Are they as expected? If not, can you suggest reasons why?

Create a collage, or another kind of artistic display, together with charts and graphs to highlight the findings of the survey and your own research.

Present your findings in a presentation. Consider the role of each team member and think of ways to make the presentation engaging.

Design a colour competition: In small groups, design a competition in celebration of colour. First, agree on a competition format by working through the following:

Choose an age group. Who will your competition be aimed at? Will you set age restrictions, or will the competition be open to all?

Decide on the purpose of the competition. What will you ask participants to do? How can you make the rules simple and problem-free?

Think about how participants’ entries will be displayed. Will you create a physical display or exhibition? Will they be uploaded to an online platform or using social media? If the latter, what can you do to prevent users from leaving negative feedback?

Agree on a prize and a judging process. Remember to choose a prize suitable for the age group.

Once your group has a final competition design, present it to the class as a slideshow or poster.

Topic 2 (How does water affect our lives?)

Design a board game: In small groups, design a board game to raise awareness about the value (and dangers) of water. Complete the following tasks:

Think about the question: What makes a good board game? Share ideas and note down the most important or exciting features of familiar board games.

With these chosen features in mind, work together to select a theme and sketch a game idea for at least four players.

Create a playable prototype.

Invite volunteer groups to play the game and give constructive feedback. Find out what features they enjoyed the most, and what could be improved or didn’t quite work.

Use this feedback to adapt the prototype and produce a final design.

Consider the retail price of the game, and whether a digital or larger version could be created.

Produce an advertising campaign in promotion of the game. This might include producing a poster, writing a blog post, or making a short video.

Pitch the game to a potential manufacturer (your teacher and the rest of the class).

Smart Water: In small groups, produce a short film with the title ‘Smart Water’. Complete the following tasks:

Think about the audience of your short film (e.g. students at your school, parents/carers, the general public, or local government), and how you will adapt the content and language to suit this audience.

Agree on the short film’s purpose (e.g. educational/factual, persuasive, entertaining, or more than one of these examples).

Decide on the content of the short film:

What will be its key message(s) or theme?

What do you want to teach your audience about being ‘water smart’?

What do you want people to do differently after watching the video? (Consider what you can do to make your film inspiring so that the audience are motivated to make small changes.)

Work together co-operatively to produce the short film. Allocate different roles to different group members, based on their strengths and interests (e.g. director, presenter, actor(s), lead script writer, videographer).

Consider how the short film will be viewed (e.g. uploaded to a video sharing platform, or to the school’s website) and plan how the audience’s reaction will be measured. How can viewers give feedback?

Present the video to the class, naming your intended audience.
