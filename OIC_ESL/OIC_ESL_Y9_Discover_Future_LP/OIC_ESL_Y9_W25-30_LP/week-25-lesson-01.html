1. Warm-up & activate prior knowledge · 8 min

  • Introduce the topic by asking: How much water do you drink every day? How much water do you think people should drink every day?
  • Encourage students to think about where drinking water comes from. Ask: Do you know where your drinking water comes from? What is it like for people who are unable to get clean drinking water?

ICQ (IEC scaffold): What is the first step and how will you respond?

2. Teacher modeling — I DO · 12 min

  • Teacher demonstrates one example from the Oxford main activity using its original instructions.

Teacher move (IEC scaffold): Think aloud through the first relevant example before asking students to try.

3. Guided practice — WE DO · 15 min

  • To establish the value of water, put students into pairs and ask them to make a list of activities that use water. Encourage them to consider domestic, agricultural, and industrial uses. Invite students to share their lists and discuss briefly.
  • Turn to ODF SB 4 p.81 ex.3. Play the Factflix video and ask students to note down any numerical data and time references they hear.
  • Allow students time to share their answers in pairs. Then elicit and write answers on the board. (Answers: 70% x 2; a month; a week; 2.5%; 17%)
  • Play the video a second time. Now ask students to add factual information about the numerical data and time references to their notes. Encourage them to share their answers, first in pairs and then in small groups.
  • Take answers in a whole-class feedback session. (Answers: 70% of the Earth’s surface is covered with water; 2.5% of the Earth’s water is fresh water; 70% of fresh water is frozen in glaciers; 17% of global electricity is hydroelectricity; a month is how long we can survive without food; a week is how long we can survive without water.)
  • Briefly discuss: What information is the most and least surprising?
  • Play the video a third time. Challenge students to listen specifically for solutions to the problem of global water shortages.
  • Use the answers to draw students’ attention to the last line of the video: ‘So we’re in a difficult situation, but at least there are some positive new developments with water!’. Discuss as a class: What is the future of the world’s water supply?

CCQ (IEC scaffold): Which detail in the Oxford text or task supports that answer?

4. Supported pair/group practice · 10 min

  • Rehearse a response to the Oxford main activity with a partner, taking turns and using a sentence frame where helpful.

ICQ (IEC scaffold): Will each partner explain their reasoning?

5. Independent practice — YOU DO · 25 min

  • Individually complete a response or product demonstrating the original learning outcomes: 9.1d, 9.1e, 9.2e.

Evidence (IEC scaffold): Collect a student-generated response rather than a copied group answer.

6. Extension & differentiation · 10 min

  • Pair and group work allows more able students to help less able students by sharing information after each listening phase.
  • For less able students, provide a simple gap-fill exercise to help them listen for key words.
  • The open discussion allows all students to participate.
  • Complete ex.1 in ODF WB 4 p.76.
  • Students can use the internet to find out facts about different dams around the world. Encourage them to discuss the pros and cons of building dams in small groups.

7. Review, assessment & exit ticket · 10 min

  • Students have watched a video about the value of water. They have listened for specific information and compiled their answers in pairs and small groups. They have discussed the future of the world’s water supply.

Exit ticket (IEC scaffold): Provide one independent example of what you learned.
