1. Warm-up & activate prior knowledge · 8 min

  • Put students into pairs. Say: Describe your ideal home.
  • Invite students to share their descriptions before displaying photos of six to eight ‘unusual’ homes. Discuss as a class: Which would be your most/least favourite place to live? Why?

ICQ (IEC scaffold): What is the first step and how will you respond?

2. Teacher modeling — I DO · 12 min

  • Teacher demonstrates one example from the Oxford main activity using its original instructions.

Teacher move (IEC scaffold): Think aloud through the first relevant example before asking students to try.

3. Guided practice — WE DO · 15 min

  • Tell students to choose two homes each from the introductory activity.
  • In pairs, encourage them to make comparisons between their two chosen homes without telling their partner which homes they are comparing. Their partner has to guess before taking their turn.
  • Tell the class they are going to read a blog post with the title ‘Staying afloat’. Invite volunteers to predict what it will be about.
  • Hand out copies of the text. Ask students to skim-read and check their predictions.
  • Now ask students to read the blog post again. This time, challenge them to think of a suitable word or phrase for each gap. Tell them that they are in the stretch zone – this isn’t an easy task, but it will help to stretch their thinking and extend their understanding of language. Give them time to work alone before sharing their ideas with a partner. Encourage them to explain their thinking behind their choices.
  • As students compare their words or phrases, write the missing comparisons using modifiers from the text on the board (not in the order of the text). Ask students to work together in their pairs to match them to the gaps. Remind them to use the context to help them, and to agree, disagree, and justify their decisions.
  • Elicit the correct answers in a whole-class feedback session. Ensure that students understand the function of each modifier. Instruct them to write the modifiers down for their future reference.
  • Refer students back to the unusual homes from the introductory activity. Put them in small groups to compare the homes again. However, this time they should use the modifiers from the reading activity.
  • Invite a volunteer from each group to share one of their comparison sentences. Discuss as a class.
  • Focus students’ attention on the final question in the blog post. Discuss as a class: Can floating homes help us face the challenges of global warming around the world?

CCQ (IEC scaffold): Which detail in the Oxford text or task supports that answer?

4. Supported pair/group practice · 10 min

  • Rehearse a response to the Oxford main activity with a partner, taking turns and using a sentence frame where helpful.

ICQ (IEC scaffold): Will each partner explain their reasoning?

5. Independent practice — YOU DO · 25 min

  • Individually complete a response or product demonstrating the original learning outcomes: 9.2b, 9.2c, 9.3a, 9.3e.

Evidence (IEC scaffold): Collect a student-generated response rather than a copied group answer.

6. Extension & differentiation · 10 min

  • Support less confident students with additional grammar practice in a small group – see the grammar explanation and exs.1–2 in ODF SB 4 p.127.
  • Challenge more able students to make comparison sentences using modifiers without referring back to the text or their notes.
  • Ask students to write an opinion piece on the class discussion question: Can floating homes help us face the challenges of global warming around the world?
  • Complete ex.6 in ODF WB 4 p.77.

7. Review, assessment & exit ticket · 10 min

  • Students have compared different homes. They have identified how comparisons are made using modifiers in a blog post, then practised using this grammar in a speaking task. They have debated whether floating homes can help fight global warming.

Exit ticket (IEC scaffold): Provide one independent example of what you learned.
