1. Warm-up & activate prior knowledge · 8 min

  • Ask the class: Which animal is the fastest on Earth? (cheetah)
  • Write on the board or dictate the following questions (without revealing the answers):
  • What speed can cheetahs reach? (112 km/h)
  • How long does it take them to reach this speed? (three seconds)
  • How long are their bodies? (1.1 m–1.4 m)
  • How long is their tail? (65 cm–80 cm)
  • Put students into pairs to guess the answers before revealing them.

ICQ (IEC scaffold): What is the first step and how will you respond?

2. Teacher modeling — I DO · 12 min

  • Teacher demonstrates one example from the Oxford main activity using its original instructions.

Teacher move (IEC scaffold): Think aloud through the first relevant example before asking students to try.

3. Guided practice — WE DO · 15 min

  • Display the picture of the Saharan silver ant. Ask: What record do you think this ant holds? (It is the fastest ant on Earth.)
  • Tell students you are going to read out a short text about the Saharan silver ant. Before you do, write on the board the numerical data from the text. In their pairs, ask students to predict what the data refers to.
  • Read aloud the text in clear speech. Instruct students to listen carefully and note down what the data refers to. Allow them to compare their answers in pairs before checking answers as a class. If necessary, read aloud the text again. Were their predictions correct?
  • Hand out copies of the text. Allow students time to read it and make notes of other key points. They share their findings in pairs.
  • Form small groups of students. Tell them to use the internet and/or reference books to research facts about other animals which are the world’s fastest in some way (e.g. the trap-jaw ant has the world’s fastest bite).
  • Remind them to think critically about the sources of information they find – are they trustworthy? How do they know? If possible, encourage them to find video and photographic evidence.
  • Invite groups to present their research orally, sharing video clips and photographs where applicable. Before beginning their presentation, tell them to take a few deep breaths to help them focus and calm their nerves.
  • If there is time at the end of the lesson, create a whole-class list of the world’s fastest animals, agreeing on the best way to categorize them.

CCQ (IEC scaffold): Which detail in the Oxford text or task supports that answer?

4. Supported pair/group practice · 10 min

  • Rehearse a response to the Oxford main activity with a partner, taking turns and using a sentence frame where helpful.

ICQ (IEC scaffold): Will each partner explain their reasoning?

5. Independent practice — YOU DO · 25 min

  • Individually complete a response or product demonstrating the original learning outcomes: 9.1b, 9.2a, 9.3c.

Evidence (IEC scaffold): Collect a student-generated response rather than a copied group answer.

6. Extension & differentiation · 10 min

  • Allocate animals and suitable websites to aid less able groups in their research.
  • Encourage more able students to explain difficult vocabulary to their peers.
  • Fast finishers can research two animals and compare them in their presentations.
  • Ask groups to write five comprehension questions about their researched animal for another group to answer.

7. Review, assessment & exit ticket · 10 min

  • Students have predicted what numerical data refers to in a text before listening to check their answers. They have read the input text to confirm their understanding of the main points. They have researched and presented facts about the world’s fastest animals.

Exit ticket (IEC scaffold): Provide one independent example of what you learned.
