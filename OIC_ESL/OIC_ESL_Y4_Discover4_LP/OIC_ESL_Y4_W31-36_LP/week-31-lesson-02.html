PERIOD 1 — 45 MINUTES

Oxford introductory activity (original order)

  1. Teach the present perfect tense (see Oxford Discover SB 4 p.141). Write one positive and one negative sentence on the board related to the unit: e.g. Many buildings in San Francisco have collapsed during earthquakes. / I haven’t seen a tsunami.
  2. Elicit the form and underline it in the examples. Circle any contractions. Help children to understand that we use it to say what happened at unspecified times in the past.
  3. Check answers as a class, ensuring that children have a good understanding of the tense.
  4. Play a mingle game to practise the present perfect. Distribute the handouts. Explain that children must try to find someone who has (or hasn’t) done the listed activities, using the present perfect to ask and answer questions. Discuss observations as a class.

Teacher ICQ: Are you discussing the opening question with a partner/group before reporting back?

Scaffolding: Elicit prior knowledge, provide relevant visuals or word cues, and model one short response.

Oxford main activity — teacher demonstration and input

  1. Put the class into small groups. Give each group some modelling clay, drinking straws, and pieces of card. No other materials can be used. Challenge them to make a model school that can withstand nature’s power within a time limit you set. The model must stand on one piece of card. Children will need to practise decision-making and clearly communicating their design ideas to their teammates.

TESOL teacher modeling: Demonstrate the language or skill in the first Oxford step with a think-aloud, accurate pronunciation, and one worked response.

Oxford main activity — guided practice

  1. Warn children that their models will be exposed to forms of extreme weather and natural disasters when the time is up. Monitor actively as they build their structures.
  2. Invite groups to show you their model in turn. First, ask them to predict if it will survive an earthquake. Then shake the model’s foundations. If the model remains standing, ask children to predict how it will cope in a hurricane or a tornado. Use a hairdryer to try to blow it down. The model that withstands nature’s power the best is the winner!

TESOL ICQ: Will we complete this part together before trying it ourselves?

TESOL CCQ: Which word, picture, example, or detail tells us that our answer fits?

Oxford main activity — collaborative practice

  1. Discuss as a class what happened to the models when confronted with nature’s power. Encourage children to describe the series of events, using this as an opportunity to remind them of cause and effect – a cause is the reason why an event happens; the effect is the result of the cause. Help them to understand that a cause must happen before any of its effects are felt (see Oxford Discover SB 4 p.149 ex.D).

TESOL scaffold: Provide a vocabulary bank or sentence starter; check both partners take turns, then gradually remove prompts.

PERIOD 2 — 45 MINUTES

Oxford main activity — increasing independence

  1. Demonstrate cause and effect by verbally outlining the series of events in the model-testing process. Ask children to listen carefully. For example:
  2. Cause: There were strong winds (hairdryer). Effects: 1. The school (model) began to shake. 2. It stayed standing for ten seconds. 3. The walls of the school collapsed. 4. The builders (children) thought about how to build a stronger structure.

Independent evidence: Each student completes an individual oral, written, reading, or practical response linked to the original outcome; record whether the response was independent or prompted.

Oxford additional tasks

  1. Complete exs.F–G in Oxford Discover SB 4 p.141 and ex.E on p.143; and complete exs.A–F in Oxford Discover WB 4 pp.128–129 and exs.C–D on p.136.
  2. Write some jumbled-up events on the board. Challenge pairs of children to put them into the correct order using the cause-and-effect strategy learned.

Oxford differentiation

  1. To support less able children to learn the present perfect, revise conjugations and past participles of common and relevant verbs, both regular and irregular.
  2. Put more sentences on the board for more confident children, including some sentences where the past tense ought to be used. Leave some gaps, but give an infinitive prompt: e.g. I [ate] (eat +) pizza for lunch today. / I [haven’t eaten] (eat –) meat for a year.
  3. Additional information: Week 31, Lesson 2
  4. Find someone who …
  5. Ask questions with the present perfect and complete the table.
  6. e.g. Have you visited another continent?

IEC TESOL adaptation: Provide targeted support to learners who need it and an optional deeper challenge for learners who are ready. Keep the original Oxford task and outcomes central.

Oxford learning review

  1. Children have learned the present perfect tense. They have explored cause and effect in a model-making activity.

Exit check: Ask each learner to demonstrate one lesson-specific target independently; note secure / developing / needs reteaching.

YEAR 4 Extreme weather
Week 31, Lesson 2: Cause and effect
Learning outcomes: 4.1k, 4.2b, 4.2e, 4.3c

Context
This is the second lesson of the unit ‘Extreme weather’.
In this lesson, children will develop their speaking and reading skills by practising the present perfect tense in a game. They will build on their listening and teamwork skills by participating in a model-making activity to illustrate the power of natural forces.
The core lesson should take around 45 minutes to an hour, with extension activities provided for extending to 90 minutes over two lessons. The timing is flexible to suit different timetables.
