PERIOD 1 — 45 MINUTES

Oxford introductory activity (original order)

  1. Introduce the concept of ‘perspective’ – how two people looking at the same thing may see totally different things or feel different emotions. Elicit from children what they understand by the word. Additionally, there is a definition of a drawing’s perspective in Oxford Discover SB 4 p.76, and a further discussion of the concept in the text ‘Sketches in a gallery’ in Oxford Discover SB 4 pp.78–79.
  2. If possible, do an online search for: ‘the dress that broke the internet’. Show children a picture of a blue and black dress that incited debate about whether it really was blue and black, or white and gold. Ask children what colour they think the dress is. Tell children that most people see blue and black, while some people see white and gold! Removing the scientific element, link this to the idea of perspective – we all might see things in different ways.

Teacher ICQ: Are you discussing the opening question with a partner/group before reporting back?

Scaffolding: Elicit prior knowledge, provide relevant visuals or word cues, and model one short response.

Oxford main activity — teacher demonstration and input

  1. Now show children a selection of pictures of everyday objects that look like different things to stimulate their interest. There are plenty of examples online.

TESOL teacher modeling: Demonstrate the language or skill in the first Oxford step with a think-aloud, accurate pronunciation, and one worked response.

Oxford main activity — guided practice

  1. In pairs, give children a couple of the pictures. They take turns looking at the photos and saying what they can see: e.g. I can see a car, but I can also see a smiley face! Remind children that there are no right or wrong answers, and each other’s ideas are valid.
  2. Bring the class back together. Find out if children see different things in any of the pictures. Hold a whole-class poll or debate, or ask children to move to different sides of the classroom if they agree or disagree.

TESOL ICQ: Will we complete this part together before trying it ourselves?

TESOL CCQ: Which word, picture, example, or detail tells us that our answer fits?

Oxford main activity — collaborative practice

  1. Now tell children that you are afraid of spiders! Encourage anyone else who is afraid of spiders to raise their hands, or move to one side of the classroom. Then hold up a picture of the huge spider sculpture Maman. Does anybody find it scary? Explain to children that the sculpture was actually made by the artist Louise Bourgeois to honour her mother. She compares her mother to the spider, saying that she is helpful and protective – just like a spider might protect us from mosquitoes. Her mother was also a ‘weaver’, like a spider ‘weaves’ its web.

TESOL scaffold: Provide a vocabulary bank or sentence starter; check both partners take turns, then gradually remove prompts.

PERIOD 2 — 45 MINUTES

Oxford main activity — increasing independence

  1. Ask: Do you still feel the same about this spider, now you know the story behind it? What do you think about the artist’s perspective on spiders? Is it positive or negative? Why do you think that?
  2. Challenge children to draw a picture of something that also looks like something else. They can be as creative and silly as possible! Tell them to swap their picture with a partner and talk about what they can see, giving reasons to explain their thinking/perspective.

Independent evidence: Each student completes an individual oral, written, reading, or practical response linked to the original outcome; record whether the response was independent or prompted.

Oxford additional tasks

  1. If possible, take children outside. Look up at the clouds and take a few photographs. Encourage children to observe them closely and describe what they can see in or around the cloud. Ask them to write down their observations, then discuss as a whole class.
  2. Use the internet to find more pictures and information on Maman. Create a flyer or leaflet to encourage tourists to visit the impressive sculpture, using descriptive language and explaining how some people might react to it differently.

Oxford differentiation

  1. For more support with the introductory activity, use ‘perspective’ in a sentence and give children three definitions to choose from. One is correct, two are incorrect.
  2. Challenge more confident children to use indefinite pronouns in the speaking activities: e.g. In my opinion, the spider sculpture is really cool, but someone might say it’s scary! / Nobody agrees with my perspective.

IEC TESOL adaptation: Provide targeted support to learners who need it and an optional deeper challenge for learners who are ready. Keep the original Oxford task and outcomes central.

Oxford learning review

  1. Children have discussed how people’s perspectives are different.

Exit check: Ask each learner to demonstrate one lesson-specific target independently; note secure / developing / needs reteaching.

Original OIC source sequence (reference only — expand to read)
YEAR 4 Why do we make art?
Week 14, Lesson 4: Things aren’t always as they seem
Learning outcomes: 4.2a, 4.2e

Context
This is the ninth lesson of the unit ‘Why do we make art?’.
In this lesson, children will develop their speaking skills by discussing and debating ideas on perspective.
The core lesson should take around 45 minutes to an hour, with extension activities provided for extending to 90 minutes over two lessons. The timing is flexible to suit different timetables.
