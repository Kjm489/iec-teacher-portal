TESOL learning goal: Students will demonstrate the specified OIC outcomes through the original Oxford activity sequence, first with modeling and support, then independently.

PERIOD 1 — 45 MINUTES

Oxford introductory activity (original order)

  1. Be mindful of the fact that this topic may be sensitive for some children who have personal experience of viruses and diseases, so should be treated very carefully. First, see our sensitivity guidance below. Then proceed with or adapt the following steps:
  2. Ask: What is a virus? This isn’t an easy question, so challenge children to think deeply and discuss their ideas in pairs first. Remind them that this is a ‘stretch zone’ question and it’s OK if they don’t get the answer right – today’s lesson will help to extend their understanding. Review answers as a class, helping children choose the best ones. Write these answers on the board.
  3. Elicit and add more items to the list. Help children to understand that a virus is tiny – we cannot see it with just our eyes. Can they remember the word ‘microscope’? Show them the picture of the scientist using a microscope in Oxford Discover SB 4 p.108 to explain that we need this to be able to see a virus. You may also show them the pictures of different viruses on p.110, frame 3.
  4. Now ask closed follow-up questions about viruses. Use or adapt the suggested questions (see ‘Additional information’ page). Invite children to stand up and to move to one side of the classroom if they answer ‘Yes’, and to the other side if they answer ‘No’.
  5. Write the questions on the board as you ask them for children’s reference. Finally, ask: Do you know the names of any viruses? Children might suggest ‘influenza’ or the ‘flu’.

Teacher ICQ: Are you discussing the opening question with a partner/group before reporting back?

Scaffolding: Elicit prior knowledge, provide relevant visuals or word cues, and model one short response.

Oxford main activity — teacher demonstration and input

  1. Play ‘Going viral’. Give one red and one green card to every child, except for one who only gets one red card – this child will be ‘patient zero’ during the viral outbreak scenario. Their identity is secret. Tell children to put their card(s) in their pockets, move around the classroom, and reveal one card only when they meet a new partner. Initially, they show their green card if they have one. However, they must hide their green card for the rest of the game if they meet a child who reveals a red card to them. From then onwards, they must reveal their red card too. If their card colours match (green + green / red + red), they take no action. After one, three, and five rounds, ask children still revealing their green cards to stand up. Count the number of ‘uninfected’ children and note the spread of the ‘virus’ on the board. Play more rounds until everyone is ‘infected’.

TESOL teacher modeling: Demonstrate the language or skill in the first Oxford step with a think-aloud, accurate pronunciation, and one worked response.

Oxford main activity — guided practice

  1. Teach the meaning of ‘spread’, then write more lesson vocabulary on the board. Drill the pronunciation of the words a few times, going slowly over any difficult sounds.

TESOL ICQ: Will we complete this part together before trying it ourselves?

TESOL CCQ: Which word, picture, example, or detail tells us that our answer fits?

Oxford main activity — collaborative practice

  1. For the rest of the lesson, set children the task of researching viruses in small groups. Use or adapt the research questions and topics suggested. Assign one topic to each group and ask children to make notes about their findings. Monitor actively to ensure that children are using multiple sensible sources to find relevant information.

TESOL scaffold: Provide a vocabulary bank or sentence starter; check both partners take turns, then gradually remove prompts.

PERIOD 2 — 45 MINUTES

Oxford main activity — increasing independence

  1. Guide a whole-class discussion to pull together children’s thinking about what viruses are. Encourage each group to feed back its key findings to rest of the class.

Independent evidence: Each student completes an individual oral, written, reading, or practical response linked to the original outcome; record whether the response was independent or prompted.

Oxford additional tasks

  1. Before holding the whole-class discussion, invite pairs of groups to exchange their notes. Then ask groups to quiz each other by asking questions on their research topic.

Oxford differentiation

  1. To support less able children when teaching the target vocabulary, write gapped sentences on the board which they can attempt to fill with correct words from the lesson.
  2. As an extension task for more able children, challenge them to say sentences that use the target words in context when teaching new vocabulary.
  3. Additional information: Week 25, Lesson 2
  4. Sensitivity guidance for teachers
  5. Exploring viruses is a timely topic. As a result of global events, be aware that some children might have first-hand experiences of the possible devastating consequences of a virus outbreak. Sometimes thinking or talking about these difficult, sometimes traumatic, experiences may ‘trigger’ people to relive them. Therefore, extra care and awareness are needed, and a ‘safety first’ approach must be taken.
  6. It is possible for children to be triggered in all types of lessons, but Lessons 2 and 3 in this unit may be particularly difficult for them. It is very important to guide discussion and invite sharing in a sensitive way that helps children feel as comfortable as possible. If children are triggered, you can support them by being compassionate, listening, and guiding them to further support within the school.
  7. If necessary, adapt the recommended activities to suit the needs of your class. For example, you could make it clear that the ‘Going viral’ game is to illustrate the spread of chicken pox, instead of using the terms ‘coronavirus’ or ‘deadly virus’. Alternatively, remove the game altogether.
  8. This lesson should end with an appropriate summary of what has been discussed, and an acknowledgement that difficult topics have come up. Signposting children to relevant support in school might be helpful at this point. Try to end the lesson on a lighter, perhaps humorous note to help with the transition into other lessons.
