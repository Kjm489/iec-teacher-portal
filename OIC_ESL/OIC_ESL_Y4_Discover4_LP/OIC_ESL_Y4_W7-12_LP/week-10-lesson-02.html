TESOL learning goal: Students will demonstrate the specified OIC outcomes through the original Oxford activity sequence, first with modeling and support, then independently.

PERIOD 1 — 45 MINUTES

Oxford introductory activity (original order)

  1. Ask: Did you know that the food you eat comes from around the world? Show children some food items that have been imported into your country. Try to be as representative as possible, sourcing at least one type of food from each continent. Elicit the names and origins of the foods and write them on the board.
  2. Then encourage children to think more locally. Ask: Do you know what animals and plants farmers raise and grow in your area? Children discuss their ideas.

Teacher ICQ: Are you discussing the opening question with a partner/group before reporting back?

Scaffolding: Elicit prior knowledge, provide relevant visuals or word cues, and model one short response.

Oxford main activity — teacher demonstration and input

  1. Write ‘summarize’ on the board. Elicit its meaning, or help children to understand that it means to reproduce the main points of what you’ve read or listened to in your own words (see Oxford Discover SB 4 p.57).

TESOL teacher modeling: Demonstrate the language or skill in the first Oxford step with a think-aloud, accurate pronunciation, and one worked response.

Oxford main activity — guided practice

  1. You can demonstrate this by choosing a simple English language text (fiction or non-fiction) and reading aloud a short, simple paragraph. Elicit or challenge children to write down the main points. Write their ideas on the board, then incorporate their ideas into an oral summary in your own words.
  2. Children can complete ex.D in Oxford Discover SB 4 p.57.

TESOL ICQ: Will we complete this part together before trying it ourselves?

TESOL CCQ: Which word, picture, example, or detail tells us that our answer fits?

Oxford main activity — collaborative practice

  1. Review answers as a class. Regardless of the results, praise children for their effort – summarizing is a difficult skill that takes practice to get right.
  2. Now look at the words in ex.E in Oxford Discover SB 4 p.57. Tell children to scan the text ‘From the World to Your Table’ on pp.58–59 and try to work out the meaning of the words. Review as a class.

TESOL scaffold: Provide a vocabulary bank or sentence starter; check both partners take turns, then gradually remove prompts.

PERIOD 2 — 45 MINUTES

Oxford main activity — increasing independence

  1. Read the text aloud for children to follow, or break it down into paragraphs. Assign a different paragraph to each group of children. They work together to summarize their paragraph. Alternatively, go through the text paragraph by paragraph as a class and create whole-class summaries, eliciting from children how each summary could be improved.
  2. At the end of the lesson, discuss: Did you learn anything new? How did you feel about the summarizing activity? What strategies can help us to summarize a text? (e.g. read the paragraph for gist first, then identify key words and main points)

Independent evidence: Each student completes an individual oral, written, reading, or practical response linked to the original outcome; record whether the response was independent or prompted.

Oxford additional tasks

  1. Read ‘Olive Oil: From the Tree to Your Table’ in Oxford Discover WB 4 p.50. Complete ex.A on p.51.
  2. Practise summarizing by looking at a range of texts and reading books.

Oxford differentiation

  1. For more support with the scanning activity, write simple definitions of the words on the board and ask children to match the word to its definition.
  2. Challenge more able children to summarize more than one paragraph.
  3. Additional information: Week 10, Lesson 2
  4. Summarize this paragraph.
  5. We all know that sweet foods taste great, but did you know that too much sugar can do more harm than good? There are large quantities of sugar in soft drinks, biscuits, jam, chocolate, sweets, and a range of sauces. Surprisingly, even cereal-based products are high in sugar, so choose a healthy whole grain option next time you go shopping. Too much sugar can make us feel tired and is really bad for our hearts!
  6. Where in the world is your food from?
  7. Have you ever opened your fridge, looked at all the different foods, and thought ‘Where did all this come from?’ It used to be an easy question. For centuries, many people grew their own fruit and vegetables in their gardens.
  8. Today, food and drink exports have changed our routines and behaviour. People from
  9. some countries don’t grow much food, but prefer to buy it from a grocery store. What’s more, the food we buy from supermarkets comes from all around the world. We can enjoy rice from Vietnam, tea from Kenya, oranges from Spain, and corn from the USA without leaving our town.
  10. ‘Food miles’ show how far our food travels. If somebody in England buys an apple from China, the apple has to travel more than 7,800 miles! The journey consumes energy and produces harmful pollution. Thanks to special chemicals, exports often last a long time,
  11. but experts say this is bad for our health. Can you believe what needs to happen to get a break-time snack?
  12. Next time you go shopping for food, check the labels on what you buy – it’s probably travelled further than you!

IEC TESOL adaptation: Provide targeted support to learners who need it and an optional deeper challenge for learners who are ready. Keep the original Oxford task and outcomes central.

Oxford learning review

Exit check: Ask each learner to demonstrate one lesson-specific target independently; note secure / developing / needs reteaching.

Learning outcomes: 4.3g, 4.3i

Context
This is the second lesson of the unit ‘Getting food to the table’.
In this lesson, children will develop their reading skills by identifying key words and main points, and practising summarizing a text.
The core lesson should take around 45 minutes to an hour, with extension activities provided for extending to 90 minutes over two lessons. The timing is flexible to suit different timetables.
