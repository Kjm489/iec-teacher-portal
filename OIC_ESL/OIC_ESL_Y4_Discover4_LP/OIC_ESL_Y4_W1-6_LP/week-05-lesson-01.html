Original Oxford lesson: What can we learn from an old object?

Original learning outcomes: 4.1b, 4.4g

TESOL learning goal: Students will demonstrate the specified OIC outcomes through the original Oxford activity sequence, first with modeling and support, then independently.

PERIOD 1 — 45 MINUTES

Oxford introductory activity (original order)

  1. Ask children to think of one object that they own that represents one of their strengths. Give an example: e.g. I chose my favourite poetry book because I’m good at writing poems. Divide the class into small groups. Ask children to take turns saying what item they chose – but not why. Children discuss what they can learn about their classmate from the object they chose and how it may relate to one of their strengths: e.g. I think Jeremy is good at salsa dancing. / I think Clara is a fantastic painter.
  2. Have a short discussion about how recognizing our own and each other’s strengths can improve our wellbeing, and help us to form positive relationships with others.

Teacher ICQ: Are you discussing the opening question with a partner/group before reporting back?

Scaffolding: Elicit prior knowledge, provide relevant visuals or word cues, and model one short response.

Oxford main activity — teacher demonstration and input

  1. Show children the vocabulary and play the audio in Oxford Discover SB 4 p.36 ex.A. Alternatively, read the words ‘dinosaur’, ‘skull’, ‘ravine’, ‘examine’, ‘discover’, ‘excavate’, ‘layers’, ‘paleontologist’, ‘ash’, ‘sedimentary rock’, and ‘pastime’. Ask children to listen carefully and repeat the words. Can they count the syllables in ‘paleontologist’ and ‘sedimentary’?
  2. Ask children which words they haven’t seen before or don’t know. Ask them what they think they mean. Elicit answers and ensure children’s understanding of all the words.

TESOL teacher modeling: Demonstrate the language or skill in the first Oxford step with a think-aloud, accurate pronunciation, and one worked response.

Oxford main activity — guided practice

  1. Search for the story ‘Paleontology with Penelope’ on the Unite for Literacy website. Play the audio and follow the book as a class.
  2. Ask comprehension questions: e.g. What are fossils? What tools does Penelope use?

TESOL ICQ: Will we complete this part together before trying it ourselves?

TESOL CCQ: Which word, picture, example, or detail tells us that our answer fits?

Oxford main activity — collaborative practice

  1. Ask: Would you like to be a paleontologist? What would you like to find? Children discuss their answers in pairs. Write on the board ‘I would like to + verb’ for support.
  2. Provide the example sentences: I found a dinosaur skull in a field. It shows that dinosaurs used to live here. Instruct children to write their own similar sentences, explaining what a particular discovery shows or means.

TESOL scaffold: Provide a vocabulary bank or sentence starter; check both partners take turns, then gradually remove prompts.

PERIOD 2 — 45 MINUTES

Oxford main activity — increasing independence

  1. Children share their sentences with a partner. Tell them to give each other constructive feedback, focusing on the language, punctuation, and content: 1. Say 1–2 things their partner did well; 2. Highlight 1–2 mistakes or improvements that their partner could make, and explain why. Remind children that it is good to share and learn from mistakes.
  2. Children rewrite or improve their sentences in line with their partner’s feedback.
  3. Before the lesson ends, ask children to find a photo of a person from long ago (in a newspaper article, a magazine, or a photo from home) to use in an activity later in the week.

Independent evidence: Each student completes an individual oral, written, reading, or practical response linked to the original outcome; record whether the response was independent or prompted.

Oxford additional tasks

  1. Complete the exercises in Oxford Discover WB 4 pp.29–30.
  2. In pairs, role-play a radio interview between a paleontologist and a presenter, in which the paleontologist talks about their job and what their discoveries teach us about the past.

Oxford differentiation

  1. For more support with the writing activity, list several discoveries and possible things we can learn from them on the board.
  2. Encourage more able children to use a variety of different grammar structures and punctuation when writing their sentences.

IEC TESOL adaptation: Provide targeted support to learners who need it and an optional deeper challenge for learners who are ready. Keep the original Oxford task and outcomes central.

Oxford learning review

  1. Children have learned new topic vocabulary and listened to a story about a paleontologist’s job. They have written their own sentences, then peer-corrected each other’s work.

Exit check: Ask each learner to demonstrate one lesson-specific target independently; note secure / developing / needs reteaching.
