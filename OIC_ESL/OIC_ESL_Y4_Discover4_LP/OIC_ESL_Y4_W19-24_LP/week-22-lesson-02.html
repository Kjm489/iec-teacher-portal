TESOL learning goal: Students will demonstrate the specified OIC outcomes through the original Oxford activity sequence, first with modeling and support, then independently.

PERIOD 1 — 45 MINUTES

Oxford introductory activity (original order)

  1. Explain to children that thinking about what their strengths are (i.e. what they are good at) will be helpful when choosing a job in the future. Help them to understand that a ‘strength’ can be a skill (e.g. being able to speak a second language, solve mathematical problems, or play the piano) or certain characteristics (e.g. being organized, self-motivated, or a team player). Elicit more examples of strengths from the class.
  2. In pairs, encourage children to take turns talking about their strengths and interests. Write possible sentence openers on the board: e.g. ‘I’m (very) good at …’, ‘I enjoy (doing) …’, ‘I’d like to be a … because I’m interested in …’, ‘I get good grades in …’.
  3. Then prompt children to say what they think their partner’s main strength is and why.
  4. Bring the class back together. Elicit how they felt when their partner recognized what they are good at / what their positive qualities are.

Teacher ICQ: Are you discussing the opening question with a partner/group before reporting back?

Scaffolding: Elicit prior knowledge, provide relevant visuals or word cues, and model one short response.

Oxford main activity — teacher demonstration and input

  1. Tell children about a job you wanted when you were their age. It can be real or made-up. Say what the job was, where you wanted to work, and why you wanted that job.

TESOL teacher modeling: Demonstrate the language or skill in the first Oxford step with a think-aloud, accurate pronunciation, and one worked response.

Oxford main activity — guided practice

  1. Write ‘What do you want to be?’ and ‘What would you like to be?’ on the board. Explain that this is how we ask someone about the job they want in the future. Ask children if they can remember some jobs from the previous lesson (see Oxford Discover SB 4, p.96). For example: mayor, assistant, president, garbage collector. Ask them if they can think of other jobs. Point to yourself to begin with to elicit teacher. They may know other jobs from the course, such as reporter.
  2. Invite a confident volunteer to respond to the question. Correct them if necessary, then write ‘I want to be …’ and ‘I’d like to be …’ on the board. Invite other children to use one of these structures in their responses.

TESOL ICQ: Will we complete this part together before trying it ourselves?

TESOL CCQ: Which word, picture, example, or detail tells us that our answer fits?

Oxford main activity — collaborative practice

  1. Repeat the activity with the questions ‘Where do you want to work?’ and ‘Where would you like to work?’ Elicit that these are asking about the location of the job.

TESOL scaffold: Provide a vocabulary bank or sentence starter; check both partners take turns, then gradually remove prompts.

PERIOD 2 — 45 MINUTES

Oxford main activity — increasing independence

  1. Divide the class into small groups. Tell children to take turns asking and answering questions about their future job aspirations, using the questions and sentence openers on the board. Encourage them to expand on their answers by giving a reason using because and thinking about how their strengths (from the introductory activity) will help them in their chosen job.
  2. Invite volunteers to share their job aspirations with the class, demonstrating new language learned and giving reasons for their choices.

Independent evidence: Each student completes an individual oral, written, reading, or practical response linked to the original outcome; record whether the response was independent or prompted.

Oxford additional tasks

  1. Ask children to write a paragraph about a job they want in the future and where they would like to work, giving reasons for their decisions. Encourage them to think about their strengths and how they can apply these to their future job.
  2. Invent a new job that doesn’t exist (yet!). It can be as silly and creative as they like. Ask children to write a paragraph saying what the job is, where people do this job, and why they would like to do it. They can also add illustrations.

Oxford differentiation

  1. In the introductory activity, write different characteristics on the board for children to think about (e.g. ‘patient’, ‘kind’, ‘organized’, ‘team player’, ‘honest’, ‘hardworking’).
  2. Mix together abilities in pairwork and group work so more confident children will have the opportunity to support their peers, modelling useful vocabulary and expressions.

IEC TESOL adaptation: Provide targeted support to learners who need it and an optional deeper challenge for learners who are ready. Keep the original Oxford task and outcomes central.

Oxford learning review

  1. Children have learned new expressions for talking about future jobs. They have considered their own and their peers’ strengths, and thought about how they can be applied in the workplace.

Exit check: Ask each learner to demonstrate one lesson-specific target independently; note secure / developing / needs reteaching.

YEAR 4 Working in a city

Learning outcomes: 4.2b, 4.2d, 4.2e

Context
This is the second lesson of the unit ‘Working in a city’.
In this lesson, children will develop their speaking skills by asking and answering questions about future job aspirations.
The core lesson should take around 45 minutes to an hour, with extension activities provided for extending to 90 minutes over two lessons. The timing is flexible to suit different timetables.
