PERIOD 1 — 45 MINUTES

Oxford introductory activity (original order)

  1. Play a game. Demonstrate the activity with two confident children. Give them a few adjective cards each. You choose a noun card (e.g. ‘elephant’) at random and read it to the class. Ask the volunteers to put the adjective card that they think is the funniest face down on the table. Read both phrases (e.g. ‘enormous elephant’ and ‘hungry elephant’), accompanied by silly actions, and invite the class to vote on their favourite one.
  2. Divide the class into small groups for children to continue the game.

Teacher ICQ: Are you discussing the opening question with a partner/group before reporting back?

Scaffolding: Elicit prior knowledge, provide relevant visuals or word cues, and model one short response.

Oxford main activity — teacher demonstration and input

  1. Demonstrate using tag questions: That was a fun game, wasn’t it? You made lots of funny phrases, didn’t you? You’re ready to work hard now, aren’t you? Place emphasis on the tag questions and continue until children recognize what you are doing. Elicit what the tag questions are in each example.
  2. Help children to understand that they are used at the end of statements that we think are true, but aren’t entirely sure about; or in statements we think other people will agree with. Reinforce that they only have ‘Yes’ or ‘No’ answers. See Oxford Discover SB 4 p.101.
  3. Write examples of tag questions on the board using different verbs. Include an affirmative and a negative statement. For example:

TESOL teacher modeling: Demonstrate the language or skill in the first Oxford step with a think-aloud, accurate pronunciation, and one worked response.

Oxford main activity — guided practice

  1. The shopping centre is busy today, isn’t it? (affirmative)
  2. You didn’t want to get out of bed this morning, did you? (negative)
  3. In pairs, tell children to look at the examples on the board. Give them a few minutes to try to work out the form of tag questions. Elicit answers. Clarify that if the main sentence is in the affirmative, the tag question is in the negative, and vice versa.

TESOL ICQ: Will we complete this part together before trying it ourselves?

TESOL CCQ: Which word, picture, example, or detail tells us that our answer fits?

Oxford main activity — collaborative practice

  1. Ask children to complete ex.F in Oxford Discover SB 4 p.101. Alternatively, hand out sets of statements and sets of tags to pairs of children. Instruct them to match the two parts together to make complete tag questions. Check answers as a class. When a child gives an answer, encourage them to explain their thinking.
  2. Instruct children to write down three to five statements they think are true, and three to five opinions that they think others will agree with. Encourage them to give affirmative and negative examples. Invite volunteers to share their sentences with the class.
  3. Now ask them to add tag questions to the end of their sentences.

TESOL scaffold: Provide a vocabulary bank or sentence starter; check both partners take turns, then gradually remove prompts.

PERIOD 2 — 45 MINUTES

Oxford main activity — increasing independence

  1. Review as a class. If a tag question isn’t quite right, encourage peer-correction and elicit how the child arrived at the correct answer.
  2. Then ask: How do we respond to tag questions? Give an example: e.g. We’ve learned about tag questions in class today, haven’t we? Elicit or give the correct response: Yes, we have! (or No, we haven’t! in case of disagreement). Try more examples until children grasp the idea: e.g. You want to be an engineer, don’t you? Yes, I do. / No, I don’t.
  3. Form new pairs of children. They take turns asking each other tag questions, using the sentences they created. To extend, ask them to make tag questions about future jobs.

Independent evidence: Each student completes an individual oral, written, reading, or practical response linked to the original outcome; record whether the response was independent or prompted.

Oxford additional tasks

  1. Complete exs.A–F in Oxford Discover WB 4 pp.90–91.
  2. Create a revision poster explaining what tag questions are and how they are formed.

Oxford differentiation

  1. To support less able children, focus on just affirmative statements to begin with.
  2. Challenge more able children to form tag questions in a variety of tenses.

IEC TESOL adaptation: Provide targeted support to learners who need it and an optional deeper challenge for learners who are ready. Keep the original Oxford task and outcomes central.

Oxford learning review

  1. Children have recognized how tag questions are formed and used them in conversation.

Exit check: Ask each learner to demonstrate one lesson-specific target independently; note secure / developing / needs reteaching.

Context
This is the fifth lesson of the unit ‘Working in a city’.
In this lesson, children will develop their speaking skills by practising using tag questions 
in informal conversations.
The core lesson should take around 45 minutes to an hour, with extension activities provided for extending to 90 minutes over two lessons. The timing is flexible to suit different timetables.
