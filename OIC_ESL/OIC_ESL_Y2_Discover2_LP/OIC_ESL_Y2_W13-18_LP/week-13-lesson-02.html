PERIOD 1 — 45 MINUTES

1. Warm-up & Activate Prior Knowledge · 8 min

Teacher: Quickly revise school subjects with children. Show the flashcards and elicit the words. Ask a confident child to come to the front. They choose a card and say some words related to the subject: e.g. four, ten = Maths. Children play the game in small groups.

Students: Think silently, discuss in pairs, share one idea. ICQs: “Do you discuss first or write the final answer?” “Do both partners share?” CCQs: “Which picture or example is connected to What are your favourite subjects??”

2. Teacher Modeling — I DO · 12 min

Teacher think-aloud: Name a subject and describe what students learn or do. Show a complete example, pronounce target words, and explain why it is correct. Original main activity: Ask: What is your favourite subject? Elicit responses and encourage children to give reasons for their choices. Give an example yourself: e.g. I like History because I like stories about the past. Show children your ‘predictor’ made from folded paper. Ensure that they can all see the question points (there should be four at the beginning, followed by eight), and then the fold-over flaps. Give some examples of tasks for each point (e.g. spell a word on the first four, draw an icon for a school subject on the next, then a question for the final flap). Elicit some questions for the final question: e.g. What is your favourite / least favourite subject? Which subject do you like best/worst? Why? Which subject is the most difficult/boring/exciting/fun? Hand out the paper squares and, in unison, children make their predictor with you by folding. You could also play them an instructional video. Children either work in pairs or individually. When everyone has finished, allow children to play with their predictor. They ask and answer each other

CCQs: Is this an activity or a school subject? What clue tells you? “Can you point to the evidence?”

3. Guided Practice — WE DO · 15 min

Step 1: Complete the first original task example together. Step 2: Provide a visual and partial language frame. Step 3: Invite learners to complete another example with fewer prompts. Original task anchor: Ask: What is your favourite subject? Elicit responses and encourage children to give reasons for their choices. Give an example yourself: e.g. I like History because I like stories about the past. Show children your ‘predictor’ made from folded paper. Ensure that they can all see the question points (there should be four at the beginning, followed by eight), and then the fold-over flaps. Give some examples of tasks for each point (e.g. spell a word on the first four, draw an icon for a school subject on the next, then a question for the final flap). Elicit some questions for the final question: e.g. What is your favourite / least favourite subject? Which subject do you like best/worst? Why?

ICQs: “Are we doing this one together?” “Where do we check our answers?” CCQs: Is this an activity or a school subject? What clue tells you?

4. Supported Pair/Group Practice · 10 min

Partners use school-subject picture cards and timetable samples to explain, question or solve a new example related to What are your favourite subjects?. Partner A gives an answer; Partner B asks for evidence; switch roles and compare. ICQs: “Do both partners have a turn?” “Do you show your reason?” CCQs: Is this an activity or a school subject? What clue tells you?

PERIOD 2 — 45 MINUTES

5. Independent Practice — YOU DO · 25 min

Independent task: Make a personal timetable, subject preference response or illustrated school activity. Record the results of the questions: e.g. ‘Three people like English best. Two people think Art is boring.’ Encourage children to draw a picture of themselves doing their favourite subject. Tell them to think of things they need for this subject (e.g. a paintbrush for Art). Ask children to reflect on, and discuss in pairs, whether their best subject is always their favourite subject, and vice versa.

Collect an individual oral, written or illustrated response that directly demonstrates the source learning outcomes; do not let pupils copy the teacher’s example.

6. Extension & Differentiation · 10 min

Emerging: use picture choices, labeled vocabulary, gesture and an oral sentence frame. Developing: give a full answer with one accurate detail. Extending: justify, compare or design an original example. Original source differentiation: To support less able children, allow them to only ask four questions rather than eight at the question points. As an extension task for more able children, see if they can ask eight questions at both question points. Challenge more confident children to try to always give reasons for their choices.

7. Review, Formative Assessment & Exit Ticket · 10 min

Review: Children have learned about school subjects and asked and answered questions using a predictor game.

Exit ticket: Complete one new response related to What are your favourite subjects? without copying. Record independent / prompted / not yet. CCQ: Is this an activity or a school subject? What clue tells you?

1. Odd One Out — What are your favourite subjects?

Lesson-specific language focus: asking permission and sharing

Original teaching source: Teacher: Quickly revise school subjects with children. Show the flashcards and elicit the words. Ask a confident child to come to the front. They choose a card and say some words related to the subject: e.g. four, ten = Maths. Children play the game in small groups.

Materials: classroom object cards, please/thank-you dialogue strips and a puppet

Teacher preparation: Use the exact vocabulary, pictures and examples from the original teaching source above. Prepare the cards or prompts required for “Odd One Out”.

I DO — teacher models: Model one complete turn using a real example from the source. Say the target language aloud: “May I borrow your ___? Yes, you may. / Sorry, I need it.” Point to the supporting picture or text.

WE DO — guided example: Complete a second example together. Ask students to identify the correct source clue, then elicit a full sentence about asking permission and sharing.

YOU DO — game procedure: Present four source items. Teams identify the odd one out, name the grouping rule and defend a second possible answer.

ICQ: Will you show your own answer? Do you need to use the lesson pictures or words?

CCQ: Do we ask before taking someone else’s things?

Success evidence: Learner uses the lesson vocabulary accurately and completes the activity with one correct example about asking permission and sharing.

Support: Show two visual choices; rehearse the frame “May I borrow your ___? Yes, you may. / Sorry, I need it.” before learners respond.

Challenge: Ask for a second example or an explanation using because, while remaining on the topic of asking permission and sharing.
