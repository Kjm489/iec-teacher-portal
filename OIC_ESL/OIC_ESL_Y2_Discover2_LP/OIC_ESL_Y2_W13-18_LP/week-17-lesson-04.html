PERIOD 1 — 45 MINUTES

1. Warm-up & Activate Prior Knowledge · 8 min

Teacher: Ask children to remind you what a ‘rule’ is. Elicit the different people who make rules and the places where they work. Write a list of people (and their jobs / places of work) on the board. Review if these are people children can trust.

Students: Think silently, discuss in pairs, share one idea. ICQs: “Do you discuss first or write the final answer?” “Do both partners share?” CCQs: “Which picture or example is connected to Rules and consequences?”

2. Teacher Modeling — I DO · 12 min

Teacher think-aloud: Model an appropriate rule or response in a familiar situation. Show a complete example, pronounce target words, and explain why it is correct. Original main activity: Put children into groups of four or six. Hand out the words provided to each group and ask them to read them and stand up in order. See how quickly they can do this. Focus on the word ‘consequence’ and ask: What is a consequence? Elicit ideas. (The consequence is what happens if people don’t follow the rules. Establish that it isn’t necessarily the ‘punishment’ – it might be an injury or somebody’s feelings getting hurt.) In the same groups, ask children to finish the sentence ‘A consequence is …’ in their own words and in a maximum of eight words. Tell children that not following the rules has consequences. Elicit the consequence of one of your classroom rules: e.g. put your hand up when you want to speak – the consequence of not doing this is that everyone speaks at once and there’s too much noise! Now write on the board a broken rule. Ask children to copy it down. Put children into pairs. Ask them to think of the consequence of this broken rule and to write it down together. CCQs: What happens if the rule is followed? Why is it fair or useful? “Can you point to the evidence?”

4. Supported Pair/Group Practice · 10 min

Partners use scenario pictures, classroom rule cards and role cards to explain, question or solve a new example related to Rules and consequences. Partner A gives an answer; Partner B asks for evidence; switch roles and compare. ICQs: “Do both partners have a turn?” “Do you show your reason?” CCQs: What happens if the rule is followed? Why is it fair or useful?

PERIOD 2 — 45 MINUTES

5. Independent Practice — YOU DO · 25 min

Independent task: Write, illustrate or role-play a practical rule and its consequence. Complete the exercises in Oxford Discover SB 2 p.92. Write the rules for the children’s favourite game and the consequences of not following the rules.

Collect an individual oral, written or illustrated response that directly demonstrates the source learning outcomes; do not let pupils copy the teacher’s example.

6. Extension & Differentiation · 10 min

Emerging: use picture choices, labeled vocabulary, gesture and an oral sentence frame. Developing: give a full answer with one accurate detail. Extending: justify, compare or design an original example. Original source differentiation: To support less able children, encourage them to write words rather than full sentences. As an extension task for more able children, ask them to think about who makes rules and why they make rules. Challenge more confident children by asking them to consider why consequences are not all the same. Additional information: Week 17, Lesson 4 [A text about what a rule is. Enlarge the words and give one to each child.] Group of 6 When people don’t follow the rules Group of 4 there is a consequence. Ideas for broken rules: Not putting litter in the bin at school Not cleaning your bedroom Running around a swimming pool Crossing the road without looking Not taking turns when playing a game Making noise in a library

7. Review, Formative Assessment & Exit Ticket · 10 min

Review: Children have learned about consequence and have written the consequences of some broken rules.

Exit ticket: Complete one new response related to Rules and consequences without copying. Record independent / prompted / not yet. CCQ: What happens if the rule is followed? Why is it fair or useful?

Original teaching source: Teacher: Ask children to remind you what a ‘rule’ is. Elicit the different people who make rules and the places where they work. Write a list of people (and their jobs / places of work) on the board. Review if these are people children can trust.

Materials: classroom object cards, please/thank-you dialogue strips and a puppet

Teacher preparation: Use the exact vocabulary, pictures and examples from the original teaching source above. Prepare the cards or prompts required for “Mime and Guess”.

I DO — teacher models: Model one complete turn using a real example from the source. Say the target language aloud: “May I borrow your ___? Yes, you may. / Sorry, I need it.” Point to the supporting picture or text.

WE DO — guided example: Complete a second example together. Ask students to identify the correct source clue, then elicit a full sentence about asking permission and sharing.

YOU DO — game procedure: One learner silently acts out a source action; partners identify it and say a complete sentence.

ICQ: Will you show your own answer? Do you need to use the lesson pictures or words?

CCQ: Do we ask before taking someone else’s things?

Success evidence: Learner uses the lesson vocabulary accurately and completes the activity with one correct example about asking permission and sharing.

Support: Show two visual choices; rehearse the frame “May I borrow your ___? Yes, you may. / Sorry, I need it.” before learners respond.

Challenge: Ask for a second example or an explanation using because, while remaining on the topic of asking permission and sharing.

Week 17, Lesson 4: Rules and consequences

Context
This is the ninth lesson of the unit ‘Let’s follow rules’.
In this lesson, children will develop their writing skills by considering and writing about the consequences of rules.
The core lesson should take around 45 minutes to an hour, with extension activities provided for extending to 90 minutes over two lessons. The timing is flexible to suit different timetables.
