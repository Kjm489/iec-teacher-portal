Measurable goals: Students demonstrate the language and skills required by the original lesson, use topic vocabulary in a meaningful response, and produce one independent piece of evidence.

PERIOD 1 — 45 MINUTES

1. Warm-up & Activate Prior Knowledge · 8 min

Original warm-up: Put children into groups of five. Assign a word to each child: ‘train’, ‘horse’, ‘car’, ‘child’, and ‘tortoise’. Challenge them to put themselves in order of these words – fastest to slowest, then slowest to fastest. Elicit from the class other things or animals that go fast and slow.

Scaffold: Show a relevant picture or object; invite think–pair–share before asking for full sentences.

ICQs: “Are you thinking first or answering immediately?” “Will both partners speak?” CCQs: “What is happening in this example?” “Which example belongs to this unit? How do you know?”

2. Teacher Modeling — I DO · 12 min

Teacher demonstration: Model the original task with a worked example: Write on the board: ‘A horse is faster than a child.’ Focus children’s attention on the ‘–er’ ending in ‘faster’ and the word ‘than’. Using the same model, elicit other comparison sentences. Ask concept-checking questions: e.g. Is a child faster or slower than a horse? Model correct intonation and show children how stress is placed on the adjective. Now ask children to make a comparison sentence with ‘long’ or ‘short’: e.g. A train is longer than a car. Elicit/Pre-teach the adjectives ‘tall’, ‘short’, ‘fast’, ‘slow’, ‘heavy’, ‘light’, ‘small’, and ‘big’. Explain how we can turn these into comparative adjectives using ‘–er’ (e.g. ‘taller’) or ‘–ier’ (e.g. ‘heavier’) and ‘than’. Say a sentence: e.g. An elephant is heavier than a mouse. On their whiteboards, ask children to either draw a tick or a cross to indicate whether it is true or false. Put children into small groups to repeat the activity among themselves. As a class, choose six items. Write or draw them on the board. Number them 1 to 6. Children throw two dice, or spin a spinner twice, to discover which two items they are going to compare. See which group comes up with the best ideas. Complete the exercises in Oxford Discover SB 2 p.153. Explain to the class that they are going to carry out an experiment to explore forces.

Think aloud, highlight key language and demonstrate an accurate answer before students try. Use gestures, visual evidence or a short example as appropriate.

CCQs: “What did I do first?” “What makes the example accurate?”

4. Supported Pair/Group Practice · 10 min

Partner application: Partners use the original lesson’s pictures, vocabulary, reading, listening, demonstration or project materials to explain, ask and respond about speed. Swap roles after two turns; require a meaningful response, not just a single word.

ICQs: “Who speaks first?” “When do you swap?” CCQs: “Does your response fit the original example?”

PERIOD 2 — 45 MINUTES

5. Independent Practice — YOU DO · 25 min

Independent task: Complete Oxford Discover WB 2 pp.132–133. Do a picture dictation with children. For example, instruct them to draw a house with three windows. Then invite children to compare their pictures in a sentence: e.g. My house is bigger than your house. This window is longer than that window.

Students create an original response, illustration with labels, written explanation, or review-project artifact. Teacher checks accuracy against the original learning outcomes.

6. Extension & Differentiation · 10 min

Emerging: picture choices, word bank, modeled phrase, gestures and oral rehearsal. Developing: complete the target response independently with one detail. Extending: add a reason, comparison, prediction or further example.

Original support: To support less able children, focus on just a few adjectives that take ‘–er’. As an extension task for more able children, ask them to identify things outside the classroom, how fast they move, and how forces affect its speed.

7. Review, Formative Assessment & Exit Ticket · 10 min

Original review: Children have learned how to compare two things in a sentence. They have carried out an experiment to find out how force can change speed.

Exit ticket: Give one new example directly linked to speed; students respond without copying. Mark I = independent, P = prompted, N = not yet. CCQ: “What did you learn and how can you show it?”

1. Picture Evidence Hunt — Speed

Lesson-specific language focus: animals and their abilities

Original teaching source: Original warm-up: Put children into groups of five. Assign a word to each child: ‘train’, ‘horse’, ‘car’, ‘child’, and ‘tortoise’. Challenge them to put themselves in order of these words – fastest to slowest, then slowest to fastest. Elicit from the class other things or animals that go fast and slow.

Materials: animal picture cards, action-verb cards and the original animal examples

Teacher preparation: Use the exact vocabulary, pictures and examples from the original teaching source above. Prepare the cards or prompts required for “Picture Evidence Hunt”.

I DO — teacher models: Model one complete turn using a real example from the source. Say the target language aloud: “An animal can ___, but it cannot ___.” Point to the supporting picture or text.

WE DO — guided example: Complete a second example together. Ask students to identify the correct source clue, then elicit a full sentence about animals and their abilities.

YOU DO — game procedure: Show four source pictures; learners locate a requested detail and explain what in the picture supports it.

ICQ: Will you show your own answer? Do you need to use the lesson pictures or words?

CCQ: Can a whale swim? Can it fly?

Success evidence: Learner uses the lesson vocabulary accurately and completes the activity with one correct example about animals and their abilities.

Support: Show two visual choices; rehearse the frame “An animal can ___, but it cannot ___.” before learners respond.

Challenge: Ask for a second example or an explanation using because, while remaining on the topic of animals and their abilities.
