Measurable goals: Students demonstrate the language and skills required by the original lesson, use topic vocabulary in a meaningful response, and produce one independent piece of evidence.

PERIOD 1 — 45 MINUTES

1. Warm-up & Activate Prior Knowledge · 8 min

Original warm-up: Quickly revise shape vocabulary using the flashcards to elicit the words. Invite a confident child to come to the front and to turn around. With your finger, draw a shape on the child’s back. Ask: What shape is this? Encourage the volunteer to tell you. Put children into pairs to do the same.

Scaffold: Show a relevant picture or object; invite think–pair–share before asking for full sentences.

ICQs: “Are you thinking first or answering immediately?” “Will both partners speak?” CCQs: “What is happening in this example?” “Is it an animal? What body part or behaviour tells you?”

2. Teacher Modeling — I DO · 12 min

Teacher demonstration: Model the original task with a worked example: Tell children that today they are going to learn to draw an animal by reading and drawing. They must follow the instructions. Elicit that instructions are a set of sentences that begin with a capital letter and end in a full stop. Commas might be used in longer sentences so we can stop for a short breath. They tell us how to do something. Say that you aren’t going to tell them what the animal is – you want them to read carefully and to work it out for themselves. Hand out the prepared text from Oxford Discover WB 2 p.148. First, ask children to quickly read the text to see if they can guess what animal they are going to draw. Next, ask children to scan the text and underline all of the shape words. Elicit that these are nouns or ‘things’. After, ask children to scan the text for (imperative) verbs or ‘doing words’ that indicate instructions. Repeat with adjectives. Finally, encourage children to follow the instructions and draw the animal. They may compare their work with a partner as they go along. Explain to children that it doesn’t matter if they don’t get it right on their first attempt, or at all. What’s important is that they tried something new, stretched themselves, and problem-solved. 4. Supported Pair/Group Practice · 10 min

Partner application: Partners use the original lesson’s pictures, vocabulary, reading, listening, demonstration or project materials to explain, ask and respond about how to draw an animal. Swap roles after two turns; require a meaningful response, not just a single word.

ICQs: “Who speaks first?” “When do you swap?” CCQs: “Does your response fit the original example?”

PERIOD 2 — 45 MINUTES

5. Independent Practice — YOU DO · 25 min

Independent task: Complete Oxford Discover WB 2 p.149 exs.C–D. Find another animal to draw using shapes on the internet. Look around the classroom and find something that could be drawn with shapes (e.g. a window).

Students create an original response, illustration with labels, written explanation, or review-project artifact. Teacher checks accuracy against the original learning outcomes.

6. Extension & Differentiation · 10 min

Emerging: picture choices, word bank, modeled phrase, gestures and oral rehearsal. Developing: complete the target response independently with one detail. Extending: add a reason, comparison, prediction or further example.

Original support: To support less able children, provide them with a list of the key words and shapes (e.g. a crescent/half a crescent) for them to refer to while they are drawing. Challenge more confident children to also find pronouns and/or prepositions in the text. As an extension task for more able children, ask them to add some more shapes to the animal as decoration. They can then write additional sentences: e.g. It’s wearing a necklace around its neck. The necklace has got five triangles.

7. Review, Formative Assessment & Exit Ticket · 10 min

Original review: Children have followed instructions to draw an animal using simple shapes.

Exit ticket: Give one new example directly linked to how to draw an animal; students respond without copying. Mark I = independent, P = prompted, N = not yet. CCQ: “What did you learn and how can you show it?”

1. Sort and Explain — How to draw an animal

Lesson-specific language focus: animals and their abilities

Original teaching source: Original warm-up: Quickly revise shape vocabulary using the flashcards to elicit the words. Invite a confident child to come to the front and to turn around. With your finger, draw a shape on the child’s back. Ask: What shape is this? Encourage the volunteer to tell you. Put children into pairs to do the same.

Materials: animal picture cards, action-verb cards and the original animal examples

Teacher preparation: Use the exact vocabulary, pictures and examples from the original teaching source above. Prepare the cards or prompts required for “Sort and Explain”.

I DO — teacher models: Model one complete turn using a real example from the source. Say the target language aloud: “An animal can ___, but it cannot ___.” Point to the supporting picture or text.

WE DO — guided example: Complete a second example together. Ask students to identify the correct source clue, then elicit a full sentence about animals and their abilities.

YOU DO — game procedure: Teams sort six lesson cards into two meaningful groups and orally explain the rule.

ICQ: Will you show your own answer? Do you need to use the lesson pictures or words?

CCQ: Can a whale swim? Can it fly?

Success evidence: Learner uses the lesson vocabulary accurately and completes the activity with one correct example about animals and their abilities.

Support: Show two visual choices; rehearse the frame “An animal can ___, but it cannot ___.” before learners respond.

Challenge: Ask for a second example or an explanation using because, while remaining on the topic of animals and their abilities.
