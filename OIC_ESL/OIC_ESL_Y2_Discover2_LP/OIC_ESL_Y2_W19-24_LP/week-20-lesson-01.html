Measurable targets: Identify key ideas about asking permission politely; practise May I come in? Yes, you may.; independently practise permission dialogues. Check understanding through a relevant oral or written response.

PERIOD 1 — 45 MINUTES

1. Warm-up & Activate Prior Knowledge · 8 min

Teacher: Tell children that you are going to act something out for them. Ask them to observe carefully. Walk out of the classroom. Then, in a brusque way, come into the classroom, instruct a child to write the date on the board, and tell the rest of the class to open their books. Don’t say ‘please’ or ‘thank you’. Now smile at children and ask them how they felt. They will probably say they didn’t like it. Elicit that you were being rude. Elicit that saying ‘please’, ‘thank you’, and ‘may I …?’ are all ways to be polite.

Student action: Think, pair, share a lesson-related example before viewing the model. ICQs: “Are we sharing what we know or writing our final answer?” “Who speaks first?” CCQs: “Which action, picture or word connects to asking permission politely?”

2. Teacher Modeling — I DO · 12 min

Teacher demonstration: Explicitly model the first step of the original task: Tell children to imagine that they want to leave the classroom. Elicit ‘May I …?’ or ‘Can I …?’. Then model ‘Yes’ and ‘No’ answers. Say: Yes, you may. / Of course. / No, you may not. / Not now. Drill these thoroughly so that children are confident. Do this using fun choral drilling (e.g. you say the phrase and children repeat, you shout the phrase and children shout back, you shout and children whisper, etc.). Ask: May I come in? 4. Supported Pair/Group Practice · 10 min

Partner exchange: In pairs, learners practise May I come in? Yes, you may. through asking permission politely prompts. Partner A gives a description, question or example; Partner B responds and points to evidence. Swap roles and repeat with a different prompt.

ICQs: “Do both partners have a turn?” “When do you switch?” CCQs: “Was the response appropriate for this situation?”

PERIOD 2 — 45 MINUTES

5. Independent Practice — YOU DO · 25 min

Independent application: Complete exs.A–D in Oxford Discover WB 2 pp.86–87. Repeat the activity, but when children have to say ‘No’, they must give a reason why.

Students complete their own response, drawing, map, chart, writing, dialogue or project artifact as the original lesson requires. Teacher checks whether each learner can independently practise permission dialogues without copying the group model.

6. Extension & Differentiation · 10 min

Emerging: picture/word cards, gesture, two-choice questions and a sentence starter using May I come in? Yes, you may.. Developing: complete a full response with one relevant detail. Extending: explain why, compare two examples, or create a new scenario.

Original differentiation: Pair up mixed abilities in the speaking task so more confident children can support their less confident peers by modelling correct language. As an extension task for more able children, ask them to write out a couple of the dialogues.

7. Review, Formative Assessment & Exit Ticket · 10 min

Original review aim: Children have practised making and responding to polite requests.

Exit ticket: Respond to a fresh prompt about asking permission politely using May I come in? Yes, you may.. Mark Independent / Prompted / Not yet and identify who needs follow-up practice. CCQ: “What did we learn and how do we show it?”

YEAR 2 Thinking of others
Week 20, Lesson 1: May I come in?
Learning outcomes: 2.2b, 2.2e 

Context
This is the sixth lesson of the unit ‘Thinking of others’.
In this lesson, children will develop their speaking skills by role-playing some polite requests.
The core lesson should take around 45 minutes to an hour, with extension activities provided for extending to 90 minutes over two lessons. The timing is flexible to suit different timetables.


Introductory activity
Tell children that you are going to act something out for them. Ask them to observe carefully. Walk out of the classroom. Then, in a brusque way, come into the classroom, instruct a child to write the date on the board, and tell the rest of the class to open their books. Don’t say ‘please’ or ‘thank you’. Now smile at children and ask them how they felt. They will probably say they didn’t like it. Elicit that you were being rude. Elicit that saying ‘please’, ‘thank you’, and ‘may I …?’ are all ways to be polite.

Main activity
Tell children to imagine that they want to leave the classroom. Elicit ‘May I …?’ or ‘Can I …?’. Then model ‘Yes’ and ‘No’ answers. Say: Yes, you may. / Of course. / No, you may not. / Not now. Drill these thoroughly so that children are confident. Do this using fun choral drilling (e.g. you say the phrase and children repeat, you shout the phrase and children shout back, you shout and children whisper, etc.). Ask: May I come in? Nod your head to indicate ‘Yes’ and children reply: Yes, you may. Repeat, but this time shake your head to indicate ‘No’ and children reply: No, you may not. Keep this upbeat. Turn to Oxford Discover SB 2 p.101 and play the song in ex.D (track 2.19). Teach the lesson’s vocabulary using the word cards and drill. You say the noun and children say the verb (e.g. ‘TV’ – ‘watch’). Then swap roles. Do this with all the phrases. Hand out the prepared word cards and a dice or spinner to each pair. Child A turns over the card and asks child B the question using ‘May I …?’. Child B rolls the dice or spins the spinner. If the number is even, the answer is ‘Yes’; if the number is odd, the answer is ‘No’. If child B responds correctly, they keep the card. If not, it goes back into the pile. The game ends when there are no more cards. Monitor actively. Hold a whole-class discussion and ask children how it feels when someone says ‘No’ to them. Explain that when we say ‘No’, it can help to give a reason why. It might be for a valid or sensible reason.

Additional tasks
Complete exs.A–D in Oxford Discover WB 2 pp.86–87. Repeat the activity, but when children have to say ‘No’, they must give a reason why.

Learning review
Children have practised making and responding to polite requests.

Differentiation

the dialogues.
