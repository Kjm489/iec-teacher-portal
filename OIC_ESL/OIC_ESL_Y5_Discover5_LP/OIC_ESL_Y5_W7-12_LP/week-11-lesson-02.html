PERIOD 1 — 45 MINUTES

1. Warm-up & Activate Prior Knowledge · 8 min

Oxford introductory activity: Remind children that Week 11, Lesson 1 started by looking at how humans greet each other. Ask: How do animals say hello? Play one or two short video clips showing animals greeting each other. Search online for ‘animals greet each other video’. Invite children to respond to what they have seen, and compare these greetings to human greetings. Ask: Why else do you think animals might communicate?

ICQ: What will you do first? Are you listening, speaking, reading, or writing?

2. Teacher Modeling — I DO · 12 min

Oxford teacher demonstration: Read and analyse a non-fiction text about types of animal communication. Remind children about the use of titles, subtitles, paragraphs, and pictures to structure and convey information in non-fiction texts. Use the text and activities in Oxford Discover SB 5 pp.80–82.

Teacher modeling: Demonstrate the first source task and think aloud before learners attempt it.

3. Guided Practice — WE DO · 15 min

Oxford guided practice: Alternatively, search online for ‘Dolphin communication – National Geographic Kids’ or ‘Secret language of dolphins – National Geographic Kids’. Guide them through the text(s) slowly, explaining challenging vocabulary. You may want to focus on just one paragraph. Ask children to use the questions Where? When? Why? and How? to summarize the information from the text in a table or mind map.

CCQ: Which words or evidence from the Oxford task support your answer?

4. Supported Pair/Group Practice · 10 min

Oxford collaborative practice: Humans have a big impact on the environment, especially in cities. Ask: What effect does this have on animals? How do they communicate this to us? Remind children of the stress response (fight, flight, freeze). Ask volunteers to describe experiences they have had where an animal has had a stress response to them, or they have had a stress response to an animal. Remind children that this is a natural response. Invite suggestions for strategies to deal with this type of situation.

Scaffolding: Provide a short model or sentence frame, then gradually withdraw support.

PERIOD 2 — 45 MINUTES

5. Independent Practice — YOU DO · 25 min

Oxford independent practice: Hold a class discussion about animal and human communication. Invite children to name animals that live in your city. Ask: Are they dangerous? Do humans hurt them? Humans have a big impact on the environment, especially in cities. Ask: What effect does this have on animals? How do they communicate this to us? Remind children of the stress response (fight, flight, freeze). Ask volunteers to describe experiences they have had where an animal has had a stress response to them, or they have had a stress response to an animal. Remind children that this is a natural response. Invite suggestions for strategies to deal with this type of situation.

Independent evidence: Each learner demonstrates the original learning outcome independently.

6. Extension & Differentiation · 10 min

Oxford differentiation: To support less able children, work in a small group with adult support to analyse the non-fiction text. Encourage less confident children to focus on one aspect of the text or one animal, before moving on to another section. As an extension task for more able children, ask them to suggest ways that humans could improve life for animals in cities.

Oxford additional tasks: Complete the activities in Oxford Discover WB 5 pp.72–73. Research different ways that nocturnal (active at night) animals communicate.

7. Review, Formative Assessment & Exit Ticket · 10 min

Oxford learning review: Children have read an information text about animal communication. They have used layout conventions and questions to identify the key information. They have discussed the communication between animals and humans in cities.

Assessment: Check the stated OIC learning outcome using individual spoken, written, or practical evidence.
