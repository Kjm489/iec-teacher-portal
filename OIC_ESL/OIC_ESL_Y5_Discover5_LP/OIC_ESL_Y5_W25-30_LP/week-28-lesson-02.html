PERIOD 1 — 45 MINUTES

1. Warm-up & Activate Prior Knowledge · 8 min

Oxford introductory activity: Tell the class that this unit will look at ‘self-expression’. Elicit what children understand by the term by breaking it into parts (using ‘self’ and ‘express’), then clarify its meaning. Display four to five silhouettes illustrating self-expression on the board (e.g. painting, music, dancing, sculpture, and singing). Alternatively, draw stick figures. Challenge children to write the names of the activities on their whiteboard. Check answers. Before each answer, encourage children to use their bodies to copy the pose of the silhouette, or stick figure. Explain that people often use drawing, painting, or sculpture to express their thoughts and feelings. Give each child a sheet of paper and a choice of coloured pens. Invite them to think about how they are feeling today. Ask them to choose a colour that represents their feelings and to make marks on the paper to express them. They can draw pictures, or any abstract lines or shapes, that represent their mood. Draw an example on the board before starting the activity: e.g. I’m feeling calm today. I’ll choose a blue pen, and draw some gentle waves in the ocean. Stop the activity after a few minutes. Tell children they can keep, throw away, or present their picture. If they wish to present it, ask: Why did you choose this colour/shape/line? What feelings does it represent? Introduce an adjective challenge. Ask children to write the alphabet on their whiteboard. Give them two to three minutes to write adjectives next to as many letters as they can (e.g. A – angry, B – beautiful). The child(ren) with the most adjectives at the end win(s)! Ask children to circle two adjectives on their list that they like best. Invite volunteers to share their favourites with the class. Write them on the board. Choose three music clips which express different emotions (e.g. happy, sad, angry). Suggestion: excerpts from Gustav Holst, The Planets. Write three different adjectives (emotions) on the board to match to the music clips. Ask children to close their eyes. Play the music. Encourage children to match each word to a music clip. Share responses as a class. Ask: Why did you choose this emotion for this piece of music? Display four to five pictures of street art from around the world. Search online for ‘global street art images’. Add key words for four or five different art forms (e.g. ‘music’ to find a painted street piano or ‘dance’ to find a street ballet performance). Tell children which cities or countries the street art comes from. See if they can guess the theme of each one (i.e. music, dance, poetry, mime, etc.). Give children time to discuss their favourites. Now ask them to think about the themes. Ask: Which form of self-expression do you think is the most interesting? Why?

ICQ: What will you do first? Are you listening, speaking, reading, or writing?

2. Teacher Modeling — I DO · 12 min

Oxford teacher demonstration: Play a video introducing the unit topic. Use the Big Question Wrap Up video in Oxford Discover SB 5 p.159. Before watching, explain that the video will show different ways that people express themselves. Encourage children to predict what activities they think they might see. Take ideas and write key vocabulary on the board. Tell children that they will not understand everything, but should try to listen for the key points. They can make notes to identify the activities in the video (e.g. art gallery, painting, poetry). Give comprehension questions to more confident classes. For example: What name is given to a group which plays/sings music? (e.g. orchestra, band, choir). Invite children to share what they heard. Write vocabulary related to self-expression and the arts on the board. Play the video again. See if children can understand more information this time. Pair up children. Ask them to think about why people express themselves through artistic activities. Invite them to draw on their own experience: What artistic activities do you do to express yourself? How do you feel before, during, and after the activity? Why is it important to be able to express your feelings? Children share their answers with the class. Revisit the concept of ‘flow’. Children thought about this idea in the ‘Historical revolutions’ unit. Explain it again. Ask: Is there an artistic activity that helps you to find ‘flow’? Show children the reading text in Oxford Discover SB 5 pp.154–155. See if children can tell you if it is fiction or non-fiction (non-fiction). Ask: How do you know? If they are able, give children time to read the text independently or in pairs. Break it into short sections using natural break points. Encourage them to focus on general understanding, using context to predict what the highlighted words might mean. They can then use peer-discussion and dictionaries to check unknown words and connect them to what they have read. Pause at the end of each section or paragraph (three to five minutes). Ask children to do five star jumps and elicit how this makes them feel. Then check their understanding of the text. Move on to the next section. After reading, ask children to complete the activities in Oxford Discover SB 5 p.156. Teach the use of sense verbs with adjectives. Use your own subject knowledge, Oxford Discover SB 5 p.157 and WB 5 p.146 ex.A.

Teacher modeling: Demonstrate the first source task and think aloud before learners attempt it.

3. Guided Practice — WE DO · 15 min

Oxford guided practice: Take 20–30 minutes to do a Sense Survey. Ask children to wash their hands before you start. Set the five groups of sensory objects around the classroom. Arrange them by sense. Give each child a Sense Survey handout. Space children evenly around the room, near each group of objects. Ask children to visit each group of objects in turn. Encourage them to use hands-on experimentation to explore the objects with their senses. Children discuss their thoughts with other children around them. They then write a sentence about each object on their handout. Tell children they are responsible for managing their own time. They should visit all the objects in 20 minutes. Remind them when half the time has gone. Observe children as they work. Praise those who approach the task systematically. At the end of the lesson, invite children to read out their sentences. Choose different children to talk about each object. Talk about similarities and differences in their reactions. Play the listening exercise in Oxford Discover SB 5 p.158. Complete exs.A–B. Listen to the audio again. Pause and see if children can hear how Ramiro speaks when he is feeling proud. (Listen to him emphasize ‘big hit’, ‘proudest moment’, and ‘my song’.) Then see if children can hear how he speaks when he feels less sure (‘Yes … I guess …’). Show children one or more video clips of a sportsperson showing pride after winning a competition. Explain that it isn’t important to understand everything they hear. They should consider how the speaker is speaking. Ask: What clues tell you this person is proud? Ask children to practise using tone and intonation with a partner to show pride. Write the following sentences on the board for them to use. They can then use their own sentences. I’m so proud that my football team won the tournament! I’m excited that I’m going to be in the talent show! I got the top mark in class for English today!

CCQ: Which words or evidence from the Oxford task support your answer?

4. Supported Pair/Group Practice · 10 min

Oxford collaborative practice: Tell children that they will not understand everything, but should try to listen for the key points. They can make notes to identify the activities in the video (e.g. art gallery, painting, poetry). Give comprehension questions to more confident classes. For example: What name is given to a group which plays/sings music? (e.g. orchestra, band, choir). Pair up children. Ask them to think about why people express themselves through artistic activities. Invite them to draw on their own experience: Space children evenly around the room, near each group of objects. Ask children to visit each group of objects in turn. Encourage them to use hands-on experimentation to explore the objects with their senses. Ask children to practise using tone and intonation with a partner to show pride. Write the following sentences on the board for them to use. They can then use their own sentences. I’m so proud that my football team won the tournament! Ask: What makes a ‘good’ winner? (e.g. being kind and humble, respecting the feelings of others) and a ‘bad’ winner? (e.g. thinking you are better than other people) Who would be better as a sports team captain? Why? Encourage children to support others in their group. Group members should identify one thing each speaker does well from the list on the board.

Scaffolding: Provide a short model or sentence frame, then gradually withdraw support.

PERIOD 2 — 45 MINUTES

5. Independent Practice — YOU DO · 25 min

Oxford independent practice: Hold a short class discussion about how to show pride in achievements. Ask: What makes a ‘good’ winner? (e.g. being kind and humble, respecting the feelings of others) and a ‘bad’ winner? (e.g. thinking you are better than other people) Who would be better as a sports team captain? Why? Talk about the difference between ‘pride’ and ‘boasting’. Demonstrate the tone of voice used by someone who is boasting, or see if children can demonstrate it themselves. Ask: How might boasting affect your friendships? Do a creative art activity. Allow 20–30 minutes. Encourage children to use coloured pencils, paint, or clay to make a small artwork or sculpture. This should illustrate their favourite form of self-expression. Examples: a painting of a guitar (music) or a sculpture of a ballerina (dance). Display the materials which they can use. Use this as a practical opportunity to review understanding of ‘most’, ‘many’, ‘several’ (see Oxford Discover SB 5 p.159). Most of the materials are on the table. Many of the pencils are green. Several of the paintbrushes are broken. Observe the activity. Gently question children to check understanding of the unit topic. Do a speaking activity. Allow 10–15 minutes. Play the speaking model in Oxford Discover SB 5 p.158. Divide children into groups of four or five. Instruct each child to present their artwork or sculpture for two minutes. Encourage them to practise what they have learned this week. Write success criteria on the board. Use vocabulary linked to the unit topic. Use sense verbs and adjectives. Show pride in your work through your voice. Encourage children to support others in their group. Group members should identify one thing each speaker does well from the list on the board. Establish how confident children are feeling. Ask: What words and phrases have been most useful this week? What more do you want to know about this topic? Use this to feed into the lessons in Week 29.

Independent evidence: Each learner demonstrates the original learning outcome independently.

Why? To support less able children before the reading activity, complete Oxford Discover SB 5 p.152. Work as a class or in a small group. After reading, focus on on p.156 ex.A. To support less able children, give them a list of appropriate adjectives to choose from during the survey activity. Challenge more able children to describe each object using more than one sense verb. Encourage less confident children to explore the objects by pairing them with children who will encourage them. Invite them to read their work aloud in groups, practising showing pride in a positive way through tone and intonation. Complete the writing tasks in Oxford Discover WB 5 p.149. Ask children to write a paragraph describing a form of self-expression that they enjoy. Ask children to find pictures or photos in their Student Book or Class Book in which people are expressing themselves in different ways, then describe them to a partner. 7. Review, Formative Assessment & Exit Ticket · 10 min

Oxford learning review: Children have been introduced to the unit topic and key vocabulary. They have thought about the role self-expression plays in emotional health. Children have read a non-fiction text about visual arts. They have completed activities related to the text. Children have understood how to use sense verbs with adjectives. They have completed a Sense Survey evaluating different objects using their senses. Children have listened to how a speaker is feeling in a listening text about self-expression. They have explored how to express pride in achievements appropriately. Children have made a painting, drawing, or clay sculpture. They have presented it to a small group using language learned in Week 28.

Assessment: Check the stated OIC learning outcome using individual spoken, written, or practical evidence.
