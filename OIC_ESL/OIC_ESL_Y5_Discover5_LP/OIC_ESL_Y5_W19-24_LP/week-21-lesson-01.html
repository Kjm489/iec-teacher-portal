PERIOD 1 — 45 MINUTES

1. Warm-up & Activate Prior Knowledge · 8 min

Oxford introductory activity: Ask children to reflect on the English language skills they have used over the last couple of weeks, while working through the ‘Exploration’ unit. Encourage them to think about the core communicative skills: Listening, Speaking, Reading, Writing. Children discuss in pairs when they used the skills. They can assess areas that need further work, what they are comfortable with, and where they feel they were stretched and challenged. Teachers should support lower-attaining children. Children may wish to keep a log of their goals – no matter how big or small – which they can refer back to during future ‘Reflection and review’ weeks to track their progress and achievements.

ICQ: What will you do first? Are you listening, speaking, reading, or writing?

2. Teacher Modeling — I DO · 12 min

Oxford teacher demonstration: Explain that this week, children will participate in an extended activity. They will practise their English language skills in a practical and relevant way. Teachers may choose one of the following activity ideas: A historical explorer (individual) Children research a famous explorer from history, using websites, non-fiction books, and any other available sources. Encourage children to choose from a range of explorers of different nationalities, ethnic backgrounds, and genders. During the project, children manage their own time to research, plan, and write either a booklet or a webpage about the explorer they have chosen. You may wish to set aside time during the week to talk about ethical issues related to historical exploration. Discuss the motivations of explorers for exploring new places. Encourage children to think about how exploration would have been seen from the point of view of the native people, and what the impact was on their lives.

Teacher modeling: Demonstrate the first source task and think aloud before learners attempt it.

3. Guided Practice — WE DO · 15 min

Oxford guided practice: Photography exhibition (groups) Choose one or more local places which most children will be very familiar with, such as a park, shopping street, beach, or even the school grounds. Before visiting the location(s), form groups of three or four. Ask children to think about what it means to view a location ‘through an explorer’s eyes’. For inspiration, show children photos of natural and built environments which use unusual perspectives or focus in on specific details. Children then visit the location(s) and actively try to find new ways of ‘seeing’. Allow plenty of time for wandering, sitting, and thinking. Give each group a camera to take photos. After the visit(s), print off the photos. Children work together to create a mini photo exhibition with text captions explaining their point of view.

CCQ: Which words or evidence from the Oxford task support your answer?

4. Supported Pair/Group Practice · 10 min

Oxford collaborative practice: Children then visit the location(s) and actively try to find new ways of ‘seeing’. Allow plenty of time for wandering, sitting, and thinking. Give each group a camera to take photos. At the end of the week, invite others in the school community to view the exhibitions. Ask each group to explain to the rest of the class what they saw differently when they looked ‘through an explorer’s eyes’. Alternatively, set up an obstacle course with a variety of different challenges (mental, physical, team-building) in the school grounds.

Scaffolding: Provide a short model or sentence frame, then gradually withdraw support.

PERIOD 2 — 45 MINUTES

5. Independent Practice — YOU DO · 25 min

Oxford independent practice: At the end of the week, invite others in the school community to view the exhibitions. Ask each group to explain to the rest of the class what they saw differently when they looked ‘through an explorer’s eyes’. Exploring in the wild (class/individual) Arrange a visit to an activity centre, national park, or similar location, where children can experience activities explorers often do such as hiking, canoeing, sailing, high ropes, or rock climbing. This visit can be anywhere from half a day to a week long. Alternatively, set up an obstacle course with a variety of different challenges (mental, physical, team-building) in the school grounds. Before the visit, talk about proper preparation and safety. Draw up an ‘explorer’s behaviour charter’ with the class. Explain to children that when they face struggles or challenges during the visit, this is helping their brain to grow. After the visit, ask children to reflect on their experience. Each child then writes a personal diary describing their trip. They should write in detail about the activity or activities they did, their thoughts and feelings, as well as what they have learned about exploring and their own skills and talents.

Independent evidence: Each learner demonstrates the original learning outcome independently.

6. Extension & Differentiation · 10 min

Oxford differentiation: Give more guidance initially to children requiring extra support in a small-group setting, to provide them with the tools to plan their work and make a confident start. Check on these children more frequently as they work. For the historical explorer task, work in a small group with adult support and levelled resources chosen in advance. More able children can be asked to stretch themselves in writing tasks by consciously incorporating new and difficult English words or grammar in their work. At the end of each task, they can evaluate their writing and think about ways they can improve it through, for example, the use of synonyms or more complex sentence structures. For the photography task, they can be given an optional extension task to research and write about a wildlife or landscape photographer who works in extreme environments.

Oxford additional tasks: Write a letter to a famous contemporary explorer. Ask them about their achievements, and any advice they would give to children looking to follow in their footsteps.

7. Review, Formative Assessment & Exit Ticket · 10 min

Oxford learning review: Children have identified the core English language skills that that they would like to improve or consolidate, and have done so over the week's activities. They have participated in an extended activity which has helped to develop and reinforce these skills, while firmly placing them in a real-world context.

Assessment: Check the stated OIC learning outcome using individual spoken, written, or practical evidence.

Independent reflection

Ask children to reflect on the English language skills they have used over the last couple of weeks, while working through the ‘Exploration’ unit. Encourage them to think about the core communicative skills: Listening, Speaking, Reading, Writing.

Children discuss in pairs when they used the skills. They can assess areas that need further work, what they are comfortable with, and where they feel they were stretched and challenged. Teachers should support lower-attaining children.

Children may wish to keep a log of their goals – no matter how big or small – which they can refer back to during future ‘Reflection and review’ weeks to track their progress and achievements.

Main activity

Explain that this week, children will participate in an extended activity. They will practise their English language skills in a practical and relevant way.

Teachers may choose one of the following activity ideas:

A historical explorer (individual)

Children research a famous explorer from history, using websites, non-fiction books, and any other available sources. Encourage children to choose from a range of explorers of different nationalities, ethnic backgrounds, and genders.

During the project, children manage their own time to research, plan, and write either a booklet or a webpage about the explorer they have chosen.

You may wish to set aside time during the week to talk about ethical issues related to historical exploration. Discuss the motivations of explorers for exploring new places. Encourage children to think about how exploration would have been seen from the point of view of the native people, and what the impact was on their lives.

Photography exhibition (groups)

Choose one or more local places which most children will be very familiar with, such as a park, shopping street, beach, or even the school grounds.

Before visiting the location(s), form groups of three or four. Ask children to think about what it means to view a location ‘through an explorer’s eyes’.

For inspiration, show children photos of natural and built environments which use unusual perspectives or focus in on specific details.

Children then visit the location(s) and actively try to find new ways of ‘seeing’. Allow plenty of time for wandering, sitting, and thinking. Give each group a camera to take photos.

After the visit(s), print off the photos. Children work together to create a mini photo exhibition with text captions explaining their point of view.

At the end of the week, invite others in the school community to view the exhibitions. Ask each group to explain to the rest of the class what they saw differently when they looked ‘through an explorer’s eyes’.

Exploring in the wild (class/individual)

Arrange a visit to an activity centre, national park, or similar location, where children can experience activities explorers often do such as hiking, canoeing, sailing, high ropes, or rock climbing. This visit can be anywhere from half a day to a week long.

Alternatively, set up an obstacle course with a variety of different challenges (mental, physical, team-building) in the school grounds.

Before the visit, talk about proper preparation and safety. Draw up an ‘explorer’s behaviour charter’ with the class.

Explain to children that when they face struggles or challenges during the visit, this is helping their brain to grow.

After the visit, ask children to reflect on their experience. Each child then writes a personal diary describing their trip. They should write in detail about the activity or activities they did, their thoughts and feelings, as well as what they have learned about exploring and their own skills and talents.

Additional task

Write a letter to a famous contemporary explorer. Ask them about their achievements, and any advice they would give to children looking to follow in their footsteps.
