PERIOD 1 — 45 MINUTES

1. Warm-up & Activate Prior Knowledge · 8 min

Oxford introductory activity: Show children a video about mountains, without commentary, to generate excitement and enthusiasm for the topic. Use the Big Question opener video from Oxford Discover SB 5 p.109 or search online for ‘majestic mountains soundtrack video’. Ask children to share what they know already about mountains. Allow them to share any experiences of visiting or living in mountains themselves. Share your own experiences. Ask children to name any mountain ranges they know. Ask: Why do you think mountains are important? Show children photos of people using water for a range of different purposes, such as drinking, washing clothes or dishes, sports, or cooking. Ask: Do you think these photos are connected to mountains? Why? (Our fresh water often comes from the mountains.) Encourage children to take a sip of water. Ask them to close their eyes and to focus on how they feel when they drink the water. Remind them that it’s important not to take water and where it comes from for granted. It’s a valuable natural resource. Review the water cycle as a drama game. Use a watery soundtrack if you wish. Ask children to crouch down, imagining that they are a drop of water in the ocean. Invite them to imagine the sun is warming them. Ask them to stand up slowly, stretching their arms to the sky. Now tell children they are high in the sky, and it is getting colder. Invite them to move into groups to form ‘clouds’. As soon as a cloud has more than five people, it must break up and ‘fall’ to the ground as rain, so that those children are crouching again. Children repeat the exercise as a fluid activity, rising, forming clouds, and sinking again. Play a game to practise the passive. Divide children into pairs. Draw three columns on the board and invite children to tell their partner silly sentences by mixing and matching the examples. Use or adapt the examples on the ‘Additional information’ page. Remind children that the third column isn’t always needed in a passive sentence. Invite children to share their sentences with the class. Tell any child who makes a sentence with your name in it to do five star jumps (or an alternative forfeit)! Use Google Earth to explore Mount Everest online. If you are unable to access Google Earth directly, search online for ‘Mount Everest Google Earth video’ instead. Convey to children the wonder of the tallest mountain in the world. Ask: What do you know about Mount Everest? What can you work out from the Google Earth pictures?

ICQ: What will you do first? Are you listening, speaking, reading, or writing?

2. Teacher Modeling — I DO · 12 min

Oxford teacher demonstration: Use the listening activity in Oxford Discover SB 5 p.110 to teach vocabulary for this unit. Draw a basic outline of a mountain on the board. Then say new sentences, but leave out the new vocabulary word. Ask children to work out which word you need. e.g. The top of a mountain is called the _____. (peak) Annotate your drawing, where relevant, to illustrate that word. Divide children into pairs. Ask each pair to think about the questions in Oxford Discover SB 5 p.109 exs.B–C, or give them the following questions: What activities can you do in the mountains? Are all mountains the same as each other? What makes them different? (such as climate, plants, animals, height, and how remote they are) Encourage children to listen attentively to their partner's ideas and opinions. They might even be able to share their own experiences of mountains, which their partner can relate to. Share ideas as a class. Encourage children to use new vocabulary in their answers. Use ‘What’s So Good about Mountains?’ in Oxford Discover SB 5 pp.112–113. Children work in groups of four. In each group, two children read the first section, ‘Water!’, one child reads ‘Animals!’, and one child reads ‘Food!’. Encourage them to take notes. Then ask children to use their notes to explain the important points in the section they read to the rest of their group. Use dictionaries for support. As a class, create a diagram on the board of the water cycle. Invite volunteers to explain each step, using the information from the text. Write each step on the board to form a labelled diagram. Discuss the concept of ‘cause and effect’, using Oxford Discover SB 5 p.111 or your own subject knowledge. Remind children that they have thought about this in other units, in particular, ‘Protecting wildlife’ (Week 1). Challenge children to identify a ‘cause’ and its ’effect’ from what they know about the water cycle. Discuss in pairs and then as a class.

Teacher modeling: Demonstrate the first source task and think aloud before learners attempt it.

3. Guided Practice — WE DO · 15 min

Oxford guided practice: See if children can explain to you how climate change is affecting the water cycle in mountains. Encourage them to use the terms ‘cause’ and ‘effect’. (Ideas: glaciers are melting, mountains can no longer store as much water as ‘water towers’, sea levels are rising, there is more flooding.) Teach the difference between active and passive sentences, and how to construct the passive in present tense statements and questions. Use your own subject knowledge and Oxford Discover SB 5 p.115. For example: Mountains are called the water towers of the world. Why are mountains called water towers? Divide children into pairs. Use Oxford Discover SB 5 p.115 exs.E–F, or write five to six simple active sentences on the board. Tell children to practise changing active sentences into the passive voice. Children say their new sentence to their partner, who checks it. Then they write it on a whiteboard. Correct the exercises as a class. Review any common errors. Ask children to reread the information on the water cycle in Oxford Discover SB 5 p.112 or in the completed sentences from Week 16, Lesson 2 (see ‘Additional information’). Working in their pairs, children then say questions and statements about the water cycle in the passive voice. Encourage them to note sentences they are proud of on a whiteboard. Observe children as they talk. Note common errors discreetly. Explain that the concept of the passive voice is challenging and that they are in the stretch zone. As a class, invite children to share their sentences. Correct common errors together. Ask children to say how confident they are in using the passive voice. Reassure them that they will spend more time on the passive in the next couple of lessons. Remind them that completing challenging work helps our brain to grow. Do the listening activity in Oxford Discover SB 5 p.116 exs.A–B. Encourage children to listen for detail. Check answers as a class. Divide children into pairs. Tell them to take turns to interview each other about living in the mountains. Use Oxford Discover SB 5 p.116 ex.C, or spend a few minutes at the beginning of the task making a short list of suitable topics to cover from the unit so far. Ask children to consider the impact of humans on the mountains. As they move deeper into their interviews, pause the activity and encourage them to build on their discussion so far by talking about what it means to be a responsible resident or visitor to the mountains. Ask them to think about different kinds of human activity (such as hiking, transport, farming, hunting, tourism), as well as the different parts of the mountain or its wildlife that could be affected (such as the peak, upper slopes, animals, plants).

CCQ: Which words or evidence from the Oxford task support your answer?

Oxford collaborative practice: Divide children into pairs. Ask each pair to think about the questions in Oxford Discover SB 5 p.109 exs.B–C, or give them the following questions: Encourage children to listen attentively to their partner's ideas and opinions. PERIOD 2 — 45 MINUTES

5. Independent Practice — YOU DO · 25 min

Oxford independent practice: Hold a class discussion. Pull together children’s thinking about being responsible residents or visitors to mountains. Invite them to contribute to a list of ‘golden rules’ to follow in the mountains. Write them on the board. Ask: Who could you share these rules with? Write on the board: ‘Mount Everest was climbed for the first time in 1953.’ See if children can identify that the sentence is in the passive voice, and that it is in the past tense. Teach the use of the passive voice in the past tense. Use your own subject knowledge and Oxford Discover SB 5 p.123. For example: Active: The teacher told us about the trip to the mountains. Passive: We were told about the trip to the mountains [by the teacher]. Play the video ‘Everest by Alexandra Stewart and Joe Todd-Stanton – Book Trailer’ as a brief introduction to the Sir Edmund Hillary and Tenzing Norgay story. Ask: Why do you think they were motivated to climb Mount Everest? Would you like to climb it one day? Use the model text ‘Mount Everest’. Tell children to read the story carefully and then retell it in writing, using the passive. Once children have written their retellings, ask them to swap with a partner. They should check whether the passive has been used correctly, as well as checking punctuation, capital letters, and spelling. Observe and support children during this task. In cases where children have written different sentences to each other, and disagree, encourage them to work together to find the correct version. Check answers as a class. Correct common errors. Conduct a brief confidence survey on the passive voice. Give children the opportunity to ask any questions. Praise children for persevering in their work on the passive this week. Highlight any particular areas where you have seen good progress.

Independent evidence: Each learner demonstrates the original learning outcome independently.

Oxford differentiation: Challenge more able children by asking them to draw and label a mountain as an individual task, rather than as a class. Provide a list of the new vocabulary and dictionaries, if required, for support. To support less able children, pair or group up mixed abilities so that the more able child/children can support and encourage them. As an extension task for more able children, encourage them to carry out a ‘water cycle in a bowl’ experiment. Several versions of this experiment are available online. As an extension task for more able children, ask them to write a paragraph or record a short talk about any aspect of mountains they choose, using passive sentences. To support less able or confident children, pair them with more able or confident children for the interview task. Encourage them to be the interviewer first and provide them with a list of questions or question starters. Challenge more able children to think of ways in which mountains can be better protected, creating community solutions (such as park ranger services, recycling services, animal sanctuaries), in addition to the golden rules for personal activity. Additional information: Week 16, Lesson 4 Create a sentence using one name or phrase from each column. e.g. My brother’s socks are highly respected by sharks. To support less able children, use the ‘Learn Grammar’ box in Oxford Discover WB 5 p.114 as a memory aid. Encourage them to highlight the verbs and nouns in each sentence in different colours. Show them how each sentence is formed of these ‘building blocks’ which can be reordered. To challenge more able children, ask them to extend the story to talk about the descent back down Mount Everest, in their own words. They were on top of the world!

Oxford additional tasks: Complete a template of the water cycle (source online or create your own). Play the ‘by elephants’ circle game. Ask the first child to say a sentence in the passive: e.g. Mountains are climbed every year. The whole circle then exclaims: by elephants! This reminds children that passive sentences often have an unspoken subject. Explain to children that it isn’t possible to add ‘by elephants’ when the subject is already named: e.g. Chocolate is eaten by many people around the world. Complete the activities in Oxford Discover WB 5 pp.102–103. Complete the activities in Oxford Discover WB 5 pp.104–105. Make a poster with a short list of rules to follow as a resident or visitor. Complete the grammar practice in Oxford Discover WB 5 pp.106–107. Find out five facts about Mount Everest, using appropriate websites and non-fiction books. Share facts with a partner.

Oxford learning review: Children have watched and listened to information about mountains, and learned new topic vocabulary. Children have learned how to describe the water cycle using an input text, new topic vocabulary, and their understanding of cause and effect. They have reflected on their own consumption of water. Children have learned the difference between the active and the passive voice. They have learned how to construct a passive sentence in the present tense. Children have listened for detail in a text about mountains. They have interviewed each other about living in the mountains, building on this to create a list of rules for being a responsible resident of, or visitor to, the mountains. Children have learned about the first successful climb of Mount Everest. They have used the passive voice to construct statements in the past tense.

Play the ‘by elephants’ circle game. Ask the first child to say a sentence in the passive: e.g. Mountains are climbed every year. The whole circle then exclaims: by elephants! This reminds children that passive sentences often have an unspoken subject. Explain to children that it isn’t possible to add ‘by elephants’ when the subject is already named: e.g. Chocolate is eaten by many people around the world.
