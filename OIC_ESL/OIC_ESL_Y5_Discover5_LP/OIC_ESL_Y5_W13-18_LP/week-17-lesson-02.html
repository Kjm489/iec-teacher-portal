PERIOD 1 — 45 MINUTES

1. Warm-up & Activate Prior Knowledge · 8 min

Oxford introductory activity: Display an online weather report for your location for the week ahead, which shows weather symbols and temperature. Compare this with one for a mountain range from another part of the world. Invite children to talk about the similarities and differences in this week’s weather, reviewing basic weather vocabulary as part of the discussion. See if children can talk about what precautions they might need to take if they want to visit [the Himalayas, the Rockies, or another mountain location] this week. Ideas: suncream, a hat, sunglasses, water; or warm clothes, gloves, warm drinks. Introduce a mountain-themed game. Call out instructions and model actions for children to follow. Keep varying the instructions throughout. Play a variation of the passives game from Week 16, Lesson 4, this time using the past tense. You can adapt the model provided. Again, remind children that the third column isn’t always needed in a passive sentence. Invite children to share their sentences with the class. This time, tell any child who makes a sentence with your name in it to jog on the spot for one minute (or an alternative forfeit)! Spend a few minutes sharing the joy of reading with your class. Show and tell them about one or two books for their age group which you have enjoyed recently. Give children the opportunity to share any books and stories they have enjoyed reading recently, too. Ask: Have you ever ‘got lost’ – that means to experience ‘flow’ – in a book? Share any personal experience you might have of this. Tell children that they will be sitting down and writing for most of today’s lesson, so they need to be warmed up and energized. Lead children in a repeat of the mountain game from Week 17, Lesson 2, or create your own simple warm-up routine.

ICQ: What will you do first? Are you listening, speaking, reading, or writing?

2. Teacher Modeling — I DO · 12 min

Oxford teacher demonstration: Do the listening activity in Oxford Discover SB 5 p.124. Check answers. Play or say the forecast again. Ask children to make notes about audience and purpose. Who is the audience for this weather forecast? (people who are living in or visiting the mountains, hikers) What is the purpose of the forecast? (to give information and advice to help people plan their day, to help people stay safe on the mountains) Use the listening text one more time. Ask: What key phrases do you hear that tell you about facts and predictions? (use of the future ‘will [be/descend]’, ‘you can expect’, ‘there is a strong possibility of’, ‘is forecast’) Write key phrases and vocabulary for weather forecasts on the board. Divide children into pairs. Tell them to make notes for a mountain weather report on their whiteboard. They should plan their report individually, but use their partner for support. Encourage children to ask you, or each other, for help when planning their forecasts by adapting the phrases in Oxford Discover SB 5 p.124 for this purpose. Would it be a good idea to say …? Do you think I should talk about …? Then divide the class into small groups of four to five. Ask children to practise delivering their weather report to the group. Observe and encourage speakers. Remind other group members to give positive feedback about the strengths of each report (such as delivery, a strong phrase or sentence, detail, use of the passive). If time allows, invite children to present their weather report to the class. Repeat the positive feedback exercise. Watch a short video about a day in the life of a mountain rescue volunteer. You can search for ‘A day in the life: Mountain rescue volunteer Derbyshire Evie’. Watch the experiences of eight-year-old Evie starting from 2:27 in the video. Children are not expected to understand every single word, but it will give them a good idea about what mountain rescue involves. See what children can understand from the audio and the camera footage. Highlight and replay key vocabulary such as ‘casualty’, ‘evacuation’, ‘instructions’, and ‘volunteer’.

Teacher modeling: Demonstrate the first source task and think aloud before learners attempt it.

3. Guided Practice — WE DO · 15 min

Oxford guided practice: Ask: Would you like to be a mountain rescue volunteer? Why / Why not? Arrange a mountain rescue freeze frame. Select one child to be the casualty, who has broken their leg. Then choose three children to be mountain rescue volunteers, who pretend to care for the injured person. Invite the rest of the class to provide atmosphere by imitating a terrible snow storm. Now read aloud the story script. Check understanding of the story. On the board, write the underlined phrases from the story which use irregular past participles. Allow enough time to teach the use of irregular past participles using your own subject knowledge and Oxford Discover SB 5 p.125. In pairs, ask children to complete Oxford Discover WB 5 p.117. Adapt or extend the chart to include verbs appropriate for your class. Ask pairs to work together to use each irregular past participle in a sentence. Observe, encourage, and correct each pair as they talk. Remind them to focus on accuracy. Teach or review the use of question words Who, What, Where, When, and Why to understand and extract information from a story. Use your own subject knowledge, and information in Oxford Discover SB 5 p.119 ex.D. Give children sufficient time to read the story in Oxford Discover SB 5 p.126. Encourage them to think about the question words as they read. In pairs, ask children to make notes on the story. Use the key question words as a prompt. Discuss answers as a class. Begin with simple questions such as Who are the characters in the story?, and progress to more challenging Why? questions. In pairs, ask children to look in detail at how the story is written. Optionally, provide handouts of the story and highlighter pens. This activity will stretch children and extend their understanding. Encourage them as they work. Ask them to look at the use of tenses and the active and passive voice. Ask: How does the author use these to structure their story? Read a short story to the class, modelling the appropriate use of stress and intonation to add clarity, interest, and energy to your telling. Use ‘The Lost Explorer’ in Oxford Discover SB 5 pp.120–121. Pause to check understanding of the story as you read. Explain any new vocabulary. Draw children’s attention to the use of the passive voice and any irregular past participles. In pairs or small groups, children practise reading the story aloud. Encourage children to stand up and use their bodies as they read. Prompt them to experiment with reading sentences in several different ways to find the best option. After several minutes, pause the activity. Encourage children to share any difficult sentences or questions that they have. Then continue.

CCQ: Which words or evidence from the Oxford task support your answer?

4. Supported Pair/Group Practice · 10 min

Oxford collaborative practice: Divide children into pairs. Tell them to make notes for a mountain weather report on their whiteboard. They should plan their report individually, but use their partner for support. Then divide the class into small groups of four to five. Ask children to practise delivering their weather report to the group. Observe and encourage speakers. Remind other group members to give positive feedback about the strengths of each report (such as delivery, a strong phrase or sentence, detail, use of the passive). Ask pairs to work together to use each irregular past participle in a sentence. Observe, encourage, and correct each pair as they talk. Remind them to focus on accuracy. At the end of the activity, give children 5 minutes to evaluate their performance with their partner(s). Ask them to share one strength and one thing to improve. Remind children to be positive and tactful in how they share this feedback. Invite volunteers to read to the class. You may wish to break the story into parts and allocate parts to different children to make it a team effort. Allow time at the end of the lesson for children to read their story aloud, either to a partner or to the whole class. Celebrate their work.

Scaffolding: Provide a short model or sentence frame, then gradually withdraw support.

PERIOD 2 — 45 MINUTES

5. Independent Practice — YOU DO · 25 min

Oxford independent practice: At the end of the activity, give children 5 minutes to evaluate their performance with their partner(s). Ask them to share one strength and one thing to improve. Remind children to be positive and tactful in how they share this feedback. Invite volunteers to read to the class. You may wish to break the story into parts and allocate parts to different children to make it a team effort. If time allows, invite children to share their opinions of the story. Remind them of the work they did on film reviews in Week 14. Ask: What would you write in a review of this story? Hand out copies of the template of a story mountain. Search online for ‘story mountain template’ to find examples of this teaching tool. Lead the class in a brief explanation of the five sections of the story mountain using the question words discussed this week. Draw a mountain on the board and create a very simple story to illustrate this (example given in parentheses). Opening: Who, When, Where? (two friends, in winter, on a mountain) Build-up: What is happening? (They are skiing down the mountain. It’s difficult but fun.) Problem: What goes wrong? (There is an avalanche!) Resolution: How do the characters solve the problem? (They ski to a mountain hut.) Ending: How does the reader feel? (The characters are safe. The reader feels happy.) Tell children that they will be writing their own mountain story today. Give children time to plan their story using their story mountain template. If you prefer, use the story planner in Oxford Discover WB 5 p.119. Encourage children to use ‘Mountain Hike’ in Oxford Discover SB 5 p.126 as model texts to help them plan. Give children around 20 to 30 minutes to write their story. To create a suitable atmosphere, you may wish to play a video of mountains with quiet, calm music. Search online for ‘majestic mountains soundtrack video’. Halfway through the time available, ask children to stop, close their eyes, and breathe deeply and quietly. Ask them to reflect on their work so far. Then continue. Praise children who are focusing attentively on the task. Remind them that they will reach their goals more quickly and effectively by concentrating fully on their work. Allow time at the end of the lesson for children to read their story aloud, either to a partner or to the whole class. Celebrate their work.

Independent evidence: Each learner demonstrates the original learning outcome independently.

6. Extension & Differentiation · 10 min

Oxford differentiation: To support less able children, provide vocabulary banks and a list of sentence starters. Consider providing a blank template with times of the day to help them with structure. Challenge more able children by asking them to think about tailoring their report to a specific audience such as skiers, bird watchers, or farmers. To support less able children, work in a small group on the grammar exercises, with adult support, rather than in pairs. Challenge more able children to add more irregular past participles to the list. Additional information: Week 17, Lesson 2 Story script It was a dark, dark night. [Frieda] was lost in a terrible snowstorm. This mountain was known for being very dangerous. [Frieda] was scared. Suddenly, [she] fell. [She] tried to stand up, but [her] leg was broken! It was impossible to move. [Frieda] thought [she] was in big trouble. But the ending of [her] story wasn’t written quite yet. Far away, in the dark, [she] saw lights. It was the mountain rescue team! [Frieda] was given strong medicine for the pain. Then [she] was evacuated from the mountain by the mountain rescue team. [She] was taken to hospital. After a few months, [her] leg was better. [She] was ready to return to the mountains. And this time, [Frieda] became a mountain rescue volunteer! To support less able or less confident children in the reading task, work together to make a list of questions on the class whiteboard using the five question words. To support less able children in the analysis task, provide handouts of the story text with key phrases already highlighted. Work in a group with adult support to identify and categorize them (e.g. finding past passive phrases or exclamations). To challenge more able children, ask them to discuss this question in pairs: Does every story need to have a Who, What, When, Where, and Why? Search online for ‘six-word short stories’ and share some with children. Discuss the way some parts of a story can be implied rather than explained. Additional information: Week 17, Lesson 3 Create a sentence using one name or phrase from each column. e.g. Mr Patel was shouted at by a duck. Extension question: Identify which phrases use irregular past participles. Encourage less confident children to read aloud by creating mixed ability pairs. Ask a confident and able child to model the sentence and encourage the less confident child to repeat it using similar stress and intonation. Challenge more able children by seeing if they can read a simple story aloud at first sight. To support less confident or less able children, work with an adult to create a joint list of possible storylines. Work together to fit them into the story mountain. Then ask children to write their story independently, but have adult support remain available. Challenge more confident children by encouraging them to evaluate their own work. Ask: How well have you met the aims of the different sections of the story mountain? How could you make your story more interesting or exciting?

Oxford additional tasks: Find out what mountain rescue teams (or rescue teams for other environments, such as the ocean or desert) exist in your country. Practise the learning from this lesson using the story in Oxford Discover WB 5 p.118. Record children’s weather reports using audio or audiovisual recording equipment. Watch or listen to weather reports on English language television or radio. See how much detail children can understand. Practise reading more stories aloud. Choose from a range of stories in the Oxford Discover Student Book, or use any levelled story books available in class. Video the storytelling. Use the recording as a learning tool to improve performance. Write and illustrate final drafts of the stories for display. Use another copy of the story mountain template to plan and write another story, with a completely free choice of topic.

7. Review, Formative Assessment & Exit Ticket · 10 min

Oxford learning review: Children have listened to a weather report to identify audience and purpose. They have used key features of a weather report to plan and present their own. Children have learned about the work of mountain rescue teams. They have studied and practised a range of common irregular past participles. Children have used key question words to understand and extract information from a story, and analysed usage of common grammatical forms and patterns. Children have listened to and read a short story, and practised reading it aloud. They have worked on interesting, clear, and energetic delivery of the story using appropriate stress and intonation. Children have written an interesting and exciting story about mountains.

Assessment: Check the stated OIC learning outcome using individual spoken, written, or practical evidence.

Challenge more confident children by encouraging them to evaluate their own work. Ask: How well have you met the aims of the different sections of the story mountain? How could you make your story more interesting or exciting?

Additional task

Find out what mountain rescue teams (or rescue teams for other environments, such as the ocean or desert) exist in your country.
