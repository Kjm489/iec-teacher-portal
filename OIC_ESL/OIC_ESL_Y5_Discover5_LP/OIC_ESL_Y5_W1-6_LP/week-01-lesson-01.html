PERIOD 1 — 45 MINUTES

1. Warm-up & Activate Prior Knowledge · 8 min

Oxford introductory activity: Explain to children that they are going to talk about animals which are in danger and why humans must protect them. Divide the class into teams of four to six. Teams have one minute per category to think of animals which: run, swim, fly, climb. Go through the answers as a class, adding animals to the board, and awarding a point for each correct answer. The team with the most points wins. Display a picture of an animal whose habitat is at risk from human activity, such as a woodland animal standing in the remains of a forest fire. Ask children: Is it important to help animals? Why? What will happen if we don’t protect them? Introduce the definition of altruism in relation to protecting wildlife, as well as vocabulary such as ‘common’, ‘protect’, ‘rare’, ‘sanctuary’, ‘survive’.

ICQ: What will you do first? Are you listening, speaking, reading, or writing?

2. Teacher Modeling — I DO · 12 min

Oxford teacher demonstration: Share photos, books, or magazines showing a variety of habitats (such as desert, forest, ocean) and animals to generate interest. The photos from Oxford Discover SB 5 p.6 may also be used. Ask children what habitats they can think of and write them on the board.

Teacher modeling: Demonstrate the first source task and think aloud before learners attempt it.

3. Guided Practice — WE DO · 15 min

Oxford guided practice: Ask: Which habitats have you visited? Is this habitat near our home, or far away? In pairs, ask children to identify animals they know that live in these habitats. Add their ideas to the board. Draw a line on the board, with ‘Extinct’ on the left, ‘Endangered’ in the centre, and ‘Safe’ on the right. Talk about what these words mean.

CCQ: Which words or evidence from the Oxford task support your answer?

4. Supported Pair/Group Practice · 10 min

Oxford collaborative practice: Ask: Why do you think this animal is safe/in danger? How can we protect this animal? Revisit the topic of altruism, encouraging further discussion about selflessness and kindness towards all living things. Use the activities in Oxford Discover SB 5 pp.6–7 to discuss what children already know, and what they would like to learn about this topic.

Scaffolding: Provide a short model or sentence frame, then gradually withdraw support.

PERIOD 2 — 45 MINUTES

5. Independent Practice — YOU DO · 25 min

Oxford independent practice: Either on the board, or as a physical activity using three locations in the classroom, ask children to say, or place themselves, where they would rank (put) different animals on the line. Use a traffic light system: red = extinct, amber = endangered, and green = safe. Ask: Why do you think this animal is safe/in danger? How can we protect this animal? Revisit the topic of altruism, encouraging further discussion about selflessness and kindness towards all living things. Use the activities in Oxford Discover SB 5 pp.6–7 to discuss what children already know, and what they would like to learn about this topic.

Independent evidence: Each learner demonstrates the original learning outcome independently.

6. Extension & Differentiation · 10 min

Oxford differentiation: For more support with this lesson, provide vocabulary banks for habitats and animals. Challenge more able children by asking them to give reasons for their decisions. As an extension task for more able children, encourage them to choose an endangered animal to create a sanctuary for. Ask them to create a poster for their animal sanctuary to persuade people to donate their money or time.

Oxford additional tasks: Complete the activities in Oxford Discover WB 5 pp.2–3. Use the internet to check the children’s ranking of animals in the main activity, and create their own ranking line on paper with different animals and their habitat.

7. Review, Formative Assessment & Exit Ticket · 10 min

Oxford learning review: Children have begun to think about which animals are in danger, and ways to protect them and their habitats. They have conducted a simple ranking exercise.

Assessment: Check the stated OIC learning outcome using individual spoken, written, or practical evidence.
