PERIOD 1 — 45 MINUTES

1. Warm-up & Activate Prior Knowledge · 8 min

Oxford introductory activity: Explain that this lesson will look in more detail at one aspect of how animals are important to humans – hunting. You may like to show short videos showing hunting activities. Check in advance that they are suitable for this age group, and that they are not too graphic. Try to show a range of viewpoints: hunting for food, sport, or prized parts of the animal; culling for environmental reasons or pest control; hunting common versus rare animals; local versus global contexts; legal hunting versus illegal hunting (poaching). Give children an opportunity to respond to what they have seen if they wish.

ICQ: What will you do first? Are you listening, speaking, reading, or writing?

2. Teacher Modeling — I DO · 12 min

Oxford teacher demonstration: Ask: What animals do people hunt? Why? Is it always for positive, or negative, reasons? What’s the impact for humans and for the animals? Is it controversial? What’s the difference between hunting and poaching? Ask: How did the animals feel and react in the videos we saw? Would you react the same way? Why? Talk about stress and how it affects the hunter and the hunted both physically and emotionally (such as fast heart rate, more energy sent to the muscles, feeling excited or anxious).

Teacher modeling: Demonstrate the first source task and think aloud before learners attempt it.

3. Guided Practice — WE DO · 15 min

Oxford guided practice: Divide the class into two sides: Hunters and Animals. Explain that they will debate why they need to hunt (Humans) and why they should be protected (Animals). Their task is to persuade you to support their side. Divide each side into small teams of three to four to prepare their points using their whiteboards.

CCQ: Which words or evidence from the Oxford task support your answer?

4. Supported Pair/Group Practice · 10 min

Oxford collaborative practice: Hold the debate as a class. Encourage children to take turns by allowing one minute for one side to speak, and then one minute for the other to respond. Ask: Why do you say that? [Name], do you agree or disagree? Write their points on the board. At the end of the debate, review the points on the board. Discuss possible compromises that could achieve a good outcome for both humans and animals.

Scaffolding: Provide a short model or sentence frame, then gradually withdraw support.

PERIOD 2 — 45 MINUTES

5. Independent Practice — YOU DO · 25 min

Oxford independent practice: Remind children of key vocabulary to use when providing opinions and giving reasons. Hold the debate as a class. Encourage children to take turns by allowing one minute for one side to speak, and then one minute for the other to respond. Ask: Why do you say that? [Name], do you agree or disagree? Write their points on the board. At the end of the debate, review the points on the board. Discuss possible compromises that could achieve a good outcome for both humans and animals.

Independent evidence: Each learner demonstrates the original learning outcome independently.

6. Extension & Differentiation · 10 min

Oxford differentiation: To support less able children, pair them with more able children for the debate planning. Challenge more able children by asking them to counter the points made during the debate (give a contrasting point of view directly responding to the original point). As an extension task for more able children, they can research hunting in their own country, looking at which types of hunting are legal or illegal. They can then research an additional country to compare and contrast.

Oxford additional tasks: Listen to ex.A in Oxford Discover SB 5 p.14 and complete the questions in section B as a class. Alternatively, source a suitable text providing three to five facts about an animal and one to two reasons why it is hunted. Read this to the class and check understanding of the key facts. Write a summary of their opinions on hunting, from the point of view of a human or an animal.

7. Review, Formative Assessment & Exit Ticket · 10 min

Oxford learning review: Children have discussed the reasons for hunting and its impact on the natural world. They have prepared and conducted a class debate.

Assessment: Check the stated OIC learning outcome using individual spoken, written, or practical evidence.
