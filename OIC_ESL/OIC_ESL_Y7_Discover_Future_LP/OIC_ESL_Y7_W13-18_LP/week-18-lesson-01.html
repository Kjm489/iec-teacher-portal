3. Guided Practice — WE DO · 15 min WE DO — shared example: Choose a second example from the textbook about images and interpretation. Elicit one answer and its evidence; build a response together on the board. Teacher prompts: What does the source say or show? Which detail supports the answer? How can we improve the sentence? Oxford main activity — guided practice: Then ask: How do you feel if you have had a very good night’s sleep? Write more adjectives on the board.

4. Supported Pair/Group Practice · 10 min

Previous lesson directions (reference)

4. Supported Pair/Group Practice · 10 min Partner task: In pairs, compare two source examples related to images and interpretation and explain a difference using evidence. Roles: A describes; B asks a follow-up; then swap. ICQ: Will both partners speak and switch roles? CCQ: What evidence shows evidence from an image?

Oxford main activity — supported collaboration: Explain to students that they are going to make a list of ‘top tips’ for a good night’s sleep. On the board, write: ‘To make sure I am well-rested and can work well in class the next day, I should/shouldn’t …’.

Oxford main activity — supported collaboration: To give students an example, say: If I eat too much late at night, I don’t sleep well. The next day, I feel exhausted. And if I stay up late to watch a film, I can’t get up and then I feel grumpy!

Collaborative scaffold (IEC): Discuss one response with a partner, explain your reasoning, and take turns.

Introductory activity

Walk into the classroom and pretend to be really tired. If you feel it is appropriate, be a little grumpy, too. Say: Listen. I’ve had very little rest and hardly any sleep. So, please be quiet, open your Student’s Book and just read quietly. Oh, and, um (look at a student), sorry, what’s your name again? Oh, I’m so tired. I can’t think!

Then ask: Do you ever feel tired and grumpy because you didn’t sleep well?

Main activity

Show students the pictures of the child sleeping, the child being grumpy, the child getting up happily, and the tired students. Ask: Do any of these pictures show how you feel? How do you feel if you haven’t had a good night’s sleep?

Write any adjectives or phrases that students suggest on the board. Elicit that these feelings prevent them from concentrating in class.

Then ask: How do you feel if you have had a very good night’s sleep? Write more adjectives on the board.

Tell students to look at the adjectives on the board, and ask which state they prefer. Then ask: Why is it important to make sure we are always well-rested? Try to elicit from students that not having enough sleep affects our focus and motivation to learn. Also, help them to understand that sleep has an impact on the consolidation of memory, that is, how much information we can retain and remember.

In pairs, encourage students to think about the consequences of sleepless nights. How would this affect their performance at school? Then feed back as a class.

Explain to students that they are going to make a list of ‘top tips’ for a good night’s sleep. On the board, write: ‘To make sure I am well-rested and can work well in class the next day, I should/shouldn’t …’.

To give students an example, say: If I eat too much late at night, I don’t sleep well. The next day, I feel exhausted. And if I stay up late to watch a film, I can’t get up and then I feel grumpy!

Make a final draft of students’ ‘top tips’. Display them around the classroom.

Learning review

Differentiation

For the group activity, create mixed-ability groups so peer-support can be provided.

As an extension task for fast finishers, ask them to research five interesting facts or statistics on the importance of sleep.

Context
This is the first lesson in the unit ‘Education’.
In this lesson, students will develop their speaking skills by discussing and producing ideas on how to get a regular good night’s sleep.
This lesson should take around 45 minutes to an hour, although the timing is flexible to suit different timetables.
