Independent Practice — YOU DO · 25 min YOU DO — independent task: Individually create a written, spoken or diagram response explaining destination, purpose and impact in the context of travel planning. Oxford main activity — independent application: Tell students that thinking of as many solutions as possible to a problem is a useful strategy. However, it is important to evaluate these solutions as not all of them will work. Say: When I was in the traffic jam, I asked the bus driver what time I would arrive so I could tell my boss. Ask: Is this a solution that works? (no) Why not? (The result doesn’t solve the problem. Instead, it will make you feel anxious or look at the situation more pessimistically.)

Introductory activity

Ask: Can you tell me some reasons why people travel? Write students’ suggestions on the board. Try to elicit: work, visiting family/friends, recreation/holidays, language learning.

Then draw a circle on the board with ‘On my way to work …’ inside. Say: As I was on my way to work, there was a big traffic jam. I started to feel stressed. Draw an arrow to the word ‘traffic jam’.

Encourage students to suggest other possible problems or obstacles. As they give answers, add to the mind map on the board. They might suggest leaving a purse or a lunch at home, running out of petrol, getting lost, or losing a bus ticket.

Main activity

Divide the class into groups of four or five. Give each group a sheet of A3 paper and one reason for travelling from the introductory activity, such as ‘holidays’.

Ask groups to create a mind map of possible problems that could happen when travelling for the reason given. They should think of at least three problems.

Invite one spokesperson from each group to share their group’s answers with the class.

Then model saying: As I was sitting in the traffic jam, I realized I couldn’t do anything about being late so I called my boss to explain. I was realistic. To stop feeling bad, I opened my notebook and started doing some work. When I got to the office, I had less work to do because I had managed my time effectively on the bus. Instead of worrying, I had been optimistic and that felt good.

Tell groups to swap mind maps. Their task is to turn at least three of the travel-related problems into positive experiences. Remind them to be ‘realistic’ and ‘optimistic’ – you might want to elicit or explain the meaning of these words first.

As students work, ask them to focus on how they feel as they turn a negative experience into a postive one by being optimistic. Ask: How can being realistic about a situation instead of worrying about it help to find a solution?

Tell students that thinking of as many solutions as possible to a problem is a useful strategy. However, it is important to evaluate these solutions as not all of them will work. Say: When I was in the traffic jam, I asked the bus driver what time I would arrive so I could tell my boss. Ask: Is this a solution that works? (no) Why not? (The result doesn’t solve the problem. Instead, it will make you feel anxious or look at the situation more pessimistically.)

Students return the mind maps to the original groups. As a class, discuss: Which solutions were the most interesting? Can you use any of these solutions next time you face difficulties when travelling?

Learning review

Students have considered different reasons for travel and different problems that can happen when travelling. They have worked together in groups to find solutions to these problems, thinking optimistically and realistically.

Additional task

Differentiation

Ask fast finishers to produce a text giving students advice on a difficult travel experience.
