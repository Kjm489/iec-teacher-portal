Original Oxford Week 36 source text — complete reference

Main activity

Explain that this week, students will be honing their productive skills by exploring a specific speaking or writing genre and producing their own text and/or performance.

These sessions will enable students to practise their integrated language skills in enjoyable and creative ways.

They will produce a piece of work that focuses on an area of particular interest. Try to centre the work on a locally and/or personally relevant topic.

Start by familiarizing students with the chosen genre, and work together to identify and analyse its features. This might be:

video input (e.g. of an inspirational public speaker or a captivating storyteller);

a model text (e.g. a persuasive letter or a short story extract of your choice);

a visiting guest speaker (e.g. a local poet or playwright who performs their work to students in class, then answers students’ questions).

Provide opportunities for critical thinking, discussion, and interaction. For example:

Students could compare and contrast two texts and consider how they use different techniques to achieve their purposes, and why.

Students could talk about how a public speaker or performer delivers their speech successfully or unsuccessfully, and why.

In doing so, students will analyse the effectiveness of particular language choices and think about how to incorporate these into their own work. Where applicable, they will also examine the way in which a speaker delivers a text.

After the initial teacher-led input, tell students that they will be putting their newly acquired skills into practice by producing their own piece of written and/or spoken creative work. Allocate enough time (at least one or two lessons) for them to complete this.

Aim to include a peer feedback stage and, if possible, one-to-one student–teacher discussions to guide students’ thinking and to help them identify areas for improvement.

Remind students that the sessions are to experiment with ideas and techniques, and that there is no formal presentation or assessment.

You may choose one of the workshop ideas below.

Performance poetry

If possible, invite a local performance poet to visit the class and perform in English. The visit should be preceded by researching and writing some questions about performing.

Alternatively, search online for video clips of performance poets. For example:

Michael Rosen – ‘Chocolate Cake’

Poetry for the Classroom – ‘You Look Like a Rainbow’ by Joseph Coelho

Zaro Weil reads ‘Preposterous Penguins’ from Cherry Moon

John Lyons – ‘Happy Hummingbird Food’

Youth Poet Laureate Amanda Gorman Performs ‘Gathering Place’ at the UN Social Good Summit

Before the visit or watching the online performance, search for the text of the poem and study it with students. Focus on how the poet creates atmosphere and the language choices they have made.

Students can either write and perform their own poem, or choose a published poem to perform. Begin by studying a poem in some detail (e.g. search ‘the Children’s Poetry Archive’ for a suitable poem).

Follow these steps when studying poems:

Ask students to read the poem to themselves.

Study the content of the poem.

What is the poem about?

Is it telling a story, painting a word picture, or making an argument? Or a mix of these?

Study the rhythm and rhyme.

Does the poem follow a rhyming scheme, or is it blank or free verse?

If the poem uses rhymes, how are they used, and where?

If the poem is blank verse, what is the rhythm?

If the poem is free verse, are there any sections which use rhyme or rhythm?

What is the effect of the rhymes and rhythms on the sound of the poem?

Study the language.

Does the poem use metaphors or similes to create images?

Does the poem use words for their sound (e.g. onomatopoeia), or words which repeat the same consonants or vowel sounds? Why does the poet choose these words?

Ask students to read the poem aloud.

Think about the meaning first.

Mark the sentence stress and the phrasing on the poem.

Practise any difficult words, or sequences of words. Sometimes poems are like tongue twisters!

Use your voice – change the volume, speed, and emotion according to the meaning of the words.

Now encourage students to write their own prose poem to perform, based on an incident in their own lives. Provide the following guidance:

What moment will you choose? It could be something funny, a time when you felt a strong emotion, a time when you did something difficult or unusual, or just a powerful memory that you have.

Create a mind map about the incident. Include sections for the sounds, sights, and smells, as well as the events themselves. Add interesting words to each section, thinking about the sound of the words as well as their meaning.

Write your poem, choosing from the language in your mind map. Decide if you want to include rhyme and rhythm.

Read your poem aloud to a partner, and give each other feedback on how you could improve your poems.

Alternatively, ask students to choose a published poem to perform.

For both tasks, help students to perform effectively:

Search online for ‘how to perform a poem Michael Rosen poet’ or ‘how to perform a poem Simon Mole poet’ for a video of tips on how to perform poetry. This can be watched in sections, and students can apply the tips to their own performances. Suggest ways they can mark up their poems to indicate emphasis, pace, mood, and so on. Use these tips to create a set of success criteria for their own performances.

Rehearse in pairs, encouraging students to support each other to meet the success criteria more effectively.

Monitor and give additional feedback, modelling alternative delivery and helping with difficult sections.

Students should perform individually, in a group, or in a combination of ways (some parts can be performed by individuals, and others by groups).

Journals and diaries

If there are any famous diary keepers in your culture, ask students to research these writers and the significance of their diaries.

Explore the importance of diaries. Some ideas to consider:

Why do people keep a diary? Who do they write the diary for?

How is a diary structured? How does this make it different from an autobiography, in which someone is looking back at their life?

What kinds of things do people usually write about in diaries? Why could this be interesting to others, perhaps in the future?

How is a diary different from a blog post? What might you include in a diary that you wouldn’t put in a blog?

What kind of language do people use in a diary? How could it be different from other text types?

Search online for ‘famous diary writers’ for examples and ideas. Some historical diaries you could consider include:

The Diary of a Young Girl (Anne Frank)

The Diaries of Nella Last

Zlata’s Diary

Journals: Captain Scott’s Last Expedition

The Diary of Lena Mukhina

You could also use fictional diaries as inspiration, for example:

Diary of a Wimpy Kid (Jeff Kinney)

The Brilliant World of Tom Gates (Liz Pichon)

The Secret Diary of Adrian Mole (Sue Townsend)

Dork Diaries (Rachel Renée Russell)

Study an extract from a diary, or contrast extracts from two different diaries. Use the extracts to explore:

How does the writer organize the diary entry?

Does the writer talk about:

events

descriptions (of places, people, objects)

their feelings and thoughts

other people’s actions?

What tenses does the writer use? What kind of language do they use (formal, informal, chatty, colloquial)?

If contrasting two different diaries, ask students to rewrite a section of one diary in the style of the other.

Set students the task of writing a diary extract of a famous person they admire, or a fictional character they like, in the style of a diary they have studied or are familiar with. They should consider:

What will the extract be about?

What do you know about the character of the person writing?

How long will the diary extract last?

What is the sequence of events?

Give students time to plan and write their diary extracts, individually, in pairs, or in groups. Include a review stage for them to edit and improve on the diary entries.

Consider the final format of the diaries, and how they will be presented.

Will there be one entry per page?

Will there be any illustrations, maps, or other visuals?

If the diary is for a historical figure, will they ‘age’ the paper?

Will they make a book for their diary entry?

Encourage students to keep their own diaries for the Workshops week as a way to develop their writing fluency. These should be kept personal. At the end of the week, invite volunteers to share how they found the experience of keeping a diary.

Creative writing (fiction)

Search online for suitable short stories. Alternatively, use ‘Great Expectations’ in ODF SB 2 p.139, or ‘How the Camel got his Hump’ on p.143.

Before reading, build up interest and prepare students.

Discuss the title and some key words from the story. What can students predict about the story’s content?

Find a picture or some pictures which illustrate the story. Imagine what it is about.

If there is a film version of the story, show an extract or a still from the film. Ask students to imagine what is happening, and what the characters are saying or thinking.

Read the story for pleasure. You could choose one of the following ideas:

Read the story in sections. Invite students to discuss each one in pairs or in a group.

Read the story as a timed reading activity. Set a time limit for students to read as far as possible, discuss their comprehension, then repeat two or three times, starting at the beginning each time.

Divide the text into two or three sections. Read the first part of the text as a class, then put students into groups to predict what will happen next. Elicit ideas before repeating with the next section.

Explore different aspects of a story, including some or all of the following:

the plot and the stages of a story

Students can create a timeline of the key events in the story.

characters

Students can find or draw pictures to represent the characters in the story, and annotate them with their ideas about their personality and characteristics.

Students can draw a diagram to show the relationships between the characters in the story.

Students can create interview questions to ask the characters, then ‘hot seat’ playing the role of the character to answer the questions.

setting

Identify everything in the text which gives information about the setting.

Students can create a collage or draw a representation of the setting.

Students can write a paragraph to describe the setting.

dialogue

If the story contains dialogue:

What does the dialogue reveal about the character?

What is the character thinking when they say the words?

Students can act out the dialogue.

If the story doesn’t contain dialogue:

What conversations might take place between the characters?

Students can choose an incident in the story and write a dialogue between the characters.

language choices

Identify interesting language, such as word choices which help to create an atmosphere, or give information about a character or setting.

Identify synonyms which the writer uses to avoid repetition.

Identify how the writer uses sentence structure – for instance, short sentences are dramatic, long sentences can be more descriptive and create atmosphere, and questions engage the reader.

Stimulate students’ imaginations to create their own short stories. You could choose one of the following ideas:

Give students the opening or closing sentence or clause of a story.

Students can pick (for example) a place, an object, an animal, and a physical characteristic from a bag. They must try to incorporate these into their story.

Bring a collection of objects into class. Students can choose a minimum of three objects to incorporate into their story.

Use picture prompts to stimulate students’ imaginations.

Give students time to plan, write, and edit their short stories in pairs or groups.
