3. Guided Practice — WE DO · 15 min

Previous lesson directions (reference)

3. Guided Practice — WE DO · 15 min WE DO — shared example: Choose a second example from the textbook about communication. Elicit one answer and its evidence; build a response together on the board. Teacher prompts: What does the source say or show? Which detail supports the answer? How can we improve the sentence? ICQ: Do we complete the first example together? 4. Supported Pair/Group Practice · 10 min

Previous lesson directions (reference)

4. Supported Pair/Group Practice · 10 min Partner task: In pairs, compare two source examples related to communication and explain a difference using evidence. Roles: A describes; B asks a follow-up; then swap. ICQ: Will both partners speak and switch roles? CCQ: What evidence shows audience and purpose?

Oxford main activity — supported collaboration: Then play the audio of three conversations twice (see ODF SB 2 p.32 ex.4, track 1.08). Ask students to rate the conversations on a scale of 1–3, where 1 is the best and 3 is the worst.

Oxford main activity — supported collaboration: Check answers by saying the conversation number in turn (1, 2, 3) and asking students to stand up if they have rated that conversation as the best.

Oxford main activity — supported collaboration: Put students in groups. Give each group a copy of the listening transcript. Ask them to work together to underline examples of conversational Dos and Don’ts in two different colours. Review groups’ answers and check if there are any differences of opinion.

Collaborative scaffold (IEC): Discuss one response with a partner, explain your reasoning, and take turns.

PERIOD 2 — 45 MINUTES

PERIOD 2 · 45 MINUTES — YOU DO, Differentiation, Review

5. Independent Practice — YOU DO · 25 min

Previous lesson directions (reference)

5. Independent Practice — YOU DO · 25 min YOU DO — independent task: Individually create a written, spoken or diagram response explaining audience and purpose in the context of communication. Success check: Include an accurate example and one supporting detail. ICQ: Do you complete your answer independently before comparing? CCQ: Would you use the same greeting in a formal email and a message to a friend?

Oxford main activity — independent application: Ask one student from each group to take one of the numbered slips of paper. If the group draws 1, their task is to role-play a good conversation; if 2, a reasonably successful conversation; if 3, a bad conversation.

Oxford main activity — independent application: Groups perform their conversations to the whole class. Find out whether students have any further ideas about what makes a good conversation after watching their classmates’ role-plays.

Oxford main activity — independent application: Challenge students to try and introduce good conversation etiquette into their real-life conversations. Can they think of one thing that they can improve on? (e.g. interrupting less, showing more interest in others, paying attention, maintaining eye contact, smiling more)

Independent check (IEC): Complete an individual response demonstrating the specified Oxford outcome.

6. Extension & Differentiation · 10 min

Previous lesson directions (reference)

6. Extension & Differentiation · 10 min Support: Use a visual, short word bank and a claim–evidence sentence frame. Core: Give a complete explanation with one relevant detail. Challenge: Compare two examples of communication, evaluating the strength of the evidence. Check: Ask learners to underline the evidence in their response.

Oxford differentiation: Allow less able students to follow the listening transcript during the listening activity.

Oxford differentiation: Challenge more able students to think of colloquial expressions to use in conversation.

Oxford differentiation: More confident students can take a lead role in the performance of the role-play, while less confident students may take a more active role in scripting the conversation.

Oxford additional task: In pairs, students can prepare a different conversation from the one they prepared in their groups.

7. Review, Formative Assessment & Exit Ticket · 10 min

Previous lesson directions (reference)

7. Review, Formative Assessment & Exit Ticket · 10 min Review: Invite two different responses and compare their supporting evidence. Exit ticket: Independently write one claim about communication and one supporting detail. Assessment: Can the learner explain audience and purpose without a model? Record: I = independent; P = prompted; N = not yet.

Oxford learning review: Students have listened to three different conversations of varying levels of success and identified Dos and Don’ts. They have worked collaboratively to understand and practise the etiquette of good conversation, which they can apply to real-world contexts.

Exit ticket (IEC): Show one independently completed response or explanation linked to the outcome.

Introductory activity

Ask students for a definition of the word ‘conversation’.

When the definition is established, ask: What are the elements of a good conversation? Do you have any Dos and Don’ts to ensure a good conversation?

List students’ ideas on the board.

Main activity

Give students the ‘match the sentence’ activity handouts.

Divide the sentences from ‘Conversation Dos and Don’ts’ (see ODF SB 2 p.32) in half. Leave the first halves of the sentences in order, but mix up the second halves.

Example of a mixed-up sentence: Be polite and patient so … / … someone doesn’t understand.

Ask students to decide whether the correct sentences are conversational Dos or Don’ts.

Then play the audio of three conversations twice (see ODF SB 2 p.32 ex.4, track 1.08). Ask students to rate the conversations on a scale of 1–3, where 1 is the best and 3 is the worst.

Check answers by saying the conversation number in turn (1, 2, 3) and asking students to stand up if they have rated that conversation as the best.

Put students in groups. Give each group a copy of the listening transcript. Ask them to work together to underline examples of conversational Dos and Don’ts in two different colours. Review groups’ answers and check if there are any differences of opinion.

Ask one student from each group to take one of the numbered slips of paper. If the group draws 1, their task is to role-play a good conversation; if 2, a reasonably successful conversation; if 3, a bad conversation.

Groups perform their conversations to the whole class. Find out whether students have any further ideas about what makes a good conversation after watching their classmates’ role-plays.

Challenge students to try and introduce good conversation etiquette into their real-life conversations. Can they think of one thing that they can improve on? (e.g. interrupting less, showing more interest in others, paying attention, maintaining eye contact, smiling more)

Additional task

In pairs, students can prepare a different conversation from the one they prepared in their groups.

Learning review

Students have listened to three different conversations of varying levels of success and identified Dos and Don’ts. They have worked collaboratively to understand and practise the etiquette of good conversation, which they can apply to real-world contexts.
