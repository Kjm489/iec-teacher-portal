Introductory activity

Write ‘GOAT’ (greatest of all time), ‘LOL’ (laugh out loud), and ‘FOMO’ (fear of missing out) on the board. Challenge students to decode these text speak abbreviations.

As a class, discuss the features of ‘text speak’ – use of abbreviations, use of emojis, substitution of single letters/numbers for whole words (e.g. ‘r’ = are; ‘2’ = too/to), leaving vowels out of words (e.g. ‘txt’ = text), sentences beginning with lower case letters, extra punctuation marks or vowels for emphasis (e.g. It’s sooooo gd!!!!). Do students employ these conventions in their own language when texting?

Main activity

Establish that text language has its place, as do all forms of register.

In groups of three, ask students to discuss the following questions:

Do you know the origins of text speak? (In early texting it was difficult to press the keys, so texts needed to be shorter and quicker. Phone companies charged by the number of characters written, so writing in short form made it cheaper.)

What other form of communication was similar to text speak? (sending telegraphs)

Do you write texts? How many do you write in a day?

Do you use any particular language, words, or abbreviations only in texts?

What are the pros and cons of texting, in your opinion?

Invite groups to share their answers with the class and discuss.

Complete the following practice exercises, then review as a whole class.

1. Match the text speak in column A to their English translation in column B.

Column A: imo / JK / ASAP / TTYL / thx / b4 / G2G / BAE

Column B: before / as soon as possible / in my opinion / before anyone else (i.e. a best friend) / talk to you later / thanks / just kidding / got to go

2. Put the messages into grammatically correct English.
