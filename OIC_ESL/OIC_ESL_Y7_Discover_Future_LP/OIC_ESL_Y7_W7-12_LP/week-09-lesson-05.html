3. Guided Practice — WE DO · 15 min

Previous lesson directions (reference)

3. Guided Practice — WE DO · 15 min WE DO — shared example: Choose a second example from the textbook about communication. Elicit one answer and its evidence; build a response together on the board. Teacher prompts: What does the source say or show? Which detail supports the answer? How can we improve the sentence? ICQ: Do we complete the first example together? CCQ: Would you use the same greeting in a formal email and a message to a friend?

Oxford main activity — guided practice: Show students a conversation jar and explain how it works. (The jar holds conversation starter questions. A student takes a question from the jar and uses it to start a conversation with another student.)

CCQ (IEC): Which details in the example explain your answer?

4. Supported Pair/Group Practice · 10 min

Previous lesson directions (reference)

4. Supported Pair/Group Practice · 10 min Partner task: In pairs, compare two source examples related to communication and explain a difference using evidence. Roles: A describes; B asks a follow-up; then swap. ICQ: Will both partners speak and switch roles? CCQ: What evidence shows audience and purpose?

Oxford main activity — supported collaboration: Groups then exchange jars so that they don’t know what questions the new jar contains.

Oxford main activity — supported collaboration: Divide groups into pairs. Each student takes turns to pick a starter question to ask their partner. Their partner must make an appropriate response so that they can work together to build an interesting conversation.

Collaborative scaffold (IEC): Discuss one response with a partner, explain your reasoning, and take turns.

PERIOD 2 — 45 MINUTES

PERIOD 2 · 45 MINUTES — YOU DO, Differentiation, Review

5. Independent Practice — YOU DO · 25 min

Previous lesson directions (reference)

5. Independent Practice — YOU DO · 25 min YOU DO — independent task: Individually create a written, spoken or diagram response explaining audience and purpose in the context of communication. Success check: Include an accurate example and one supporting detail. ICQ: Do you complete your answer independently before comparing? CCQ: Would you use the same greeting in a formal email and a message to a friend?

Oxford main activity — independent application: When pairs have had some time working together, form larger conversation groups of three or four to develop a wider conversation. Remind students to practise taking turns, listening carefully, and showing interest when participating.

Oxford main activity — independent application: At the end of the lesson, give praise to students for demonstrating effective teamwork in their lessons this week, which have involved a lot of discussion and role-play activities. Ask: Do you think you are now a better conversationalist? What have you learned? What would you still like to practise and work on?

Independent check (IEC): Complete an individual response demonstrating the specified Oxford outcome.

6. Extension & Differentiation · 10 min

Previous lesson directions (reference)

6. Extension & Differentiation · 10 min Support: Use a visual, short word bank and a claim–evidence sentence frame. Core: Give a complete explanation with one relevant detail. Challenge: Compare two examples of communication, evaluating the strength of the evidence. Check: Ask learners to underline the evidence in their response.

Oxford differentiation: Pair up students in line with their abilities.

Oxford differentiation: Challenge more able students to think of some questions on a global topic or ethical issue which they are aware of or have read about, then respond with their opinions.

Oxford differentiation: Write a few generic responses on the board to assist less confident students. Prompt these students with follow-up questions.

Oxford additional task: Students can research further examples of good ways of starting and developing a conversation. They can incorporate what they have learned in new conversations with each other.

7. Review, Formative Assessment & Exit Ticket · 10 min

Previous lesson directions (reference)

7. Review, Formative Assessment & Exit Ticket · 10 min Review: Invite two different responses and compare their supporting evidence. Exit ticket: Independently write one claim about communication and one supporting detail. Assessment: Can the learner explain audience and purpose without a model? Record: I = independent; P = prompted; N = not yet.

Oxford learning review: Students have learned how to initiate and develop a conversation with appropriate language, demonstrating empathy, turn-taking abilities, and effective teamwork.

Ask students how they would feel if they were talking to somebody who kept looking away and talking to other people. Ask: Why you would feel like this? Establish that it is important to show interest when someone is speaking to you.

Ask: In what ways can you show interest when someone is speaking to you? (e.g. body language and eye contact, asking questions, using phrases that show interest). Elicit some examples and demonstrations from students.

Main activity

Divide the class into groups. Give each group a set of cut-up conversation starters and responses. (You can take the starters from ODF TG 2 p.129 ex.6 and responses from ODF SB 2 p.33 ex.6.) Ask students to match the starters to the responses.

Ask students for more examples of how you can start a conversation. Then elicit some possible responses to the conversation starters.

Show students a conversation jar and explain how it works. (The jar holds conversation starter questions. A student takes a question from the jar and uses it to start a conversation with another student.)

Give groups a time limit to produce some conversation starter questions of their own (e.g. What TV shows do you like? What makes you laugh? If you could invent an ice cream, what flavour would it be?). They add their questions to the jar.

Groups then exchange jars so that they don’t know what questions the new jar contains.

Divide groups into pairs. Each student takes turns to pick a starter question to ask their partner. Their partner must make an appropriate response so that they can work together to build an interesting conversation.

When pairs have had some time working together, form larger conversation groups of three or four to develop a wider conversation. Remind students to practise taking turns, listening carefully, and showing interest when participating.

At the end of the lesson, give praise to students for demonstrating effective teamwork in their lessons this week, which have involved a lot of discussion and role-play activities.
